Session 11 · Capstone

You Teach the Teacher

Choose a discourse · read it the Buddha's way · teach it back
This session: There's no assigned sutta. You pick one — any of the 152 — read it with the full method you've built over ten weeks, and lead our final session yourself. This page is your toolkit and your rubric.
Part 1

What This Session Is

For ten sessions I chose the discourse and led the way in. Today the roles turn. The surest sign that you've truly learned to read the suttas is that you can now do it without me — and then help someone else see what you saw. So this capstone asks you to become, for one session, the teacher.

"Be islands unto yourselves, a refuge unto yourselves, with the Dhamma as your island, the Dhamma as your refuge." — the Buddha, near the end of his life

That's the whole aim of this course in one line. Not dependence on a teacher, but the confidence to stand on the teaching yourself. Today you show — mostly to yourself — that you can.

Look how far you've come

Before you choose, glance back over the ground you've walked. This is the arc you now carry:

  • 1The poisoned arrow — why the Buddha teaches the end of suffering, not metaphysics.
  • 2The raft & the snake — how to hold a teaching: use it, don't cling to it.
  • 3Mindfulness — training attention as the ground of the path.
  • 4The breath — one simple object carried all the way to insight.
  • 5The saw — keeping your own heart free even under provocation.
  • 6Stilling thoughts — five tools for a mind that won't settle.
  • 7Self-effacement — ethics as wearing down the self from the inside.
  • 8Action & results — karma as responsibility, not blame.
  • 9Not-self — no fixed owner behind the changing parts.
  • 10The six sixes — how a moment assembles, and where to let go.
Part 2

Choose Your Discourse

Pick one discourse from the Majjhima Nikāya — any of the 152. Some guidance:

  • •Choose something that genuinely tugs at you — a title that intrigues, a theme you've been circling, a question you still have. Curiosity is the best fuel for teaching.
  • •You may go deeper into a sutta we touched (revisit MN 10 or MN 118 in full), or strike out into new territory entirely.
  • •If you'd like a suggestion: MN 2 (the taints), MN 19 (two kinds of thought), MN 38 (on consciousness and craving), or MN 152 (the development of the faculties) all reward a motivated beginner.

Part 3

The MethodHow to read any sutta on your own

Everything the course did to a discourse, you now do yourself. Run your chosen sutta through these six steps — this is the portable skill you're taking with you:

  1. Set the scene. Who's speaking, to whom, and why? What question or situation prompts the teaching? (Context first.)
  2. Read once, all the way through, for the feel of it — no stopping to analyze. Let it land.
  3. Read again with your four marks — ! (strikes you), ? (unclear), ★ (remember), ~ (push back). Annotate freely.
  4. Name the core teaching in one plain sentence, as if to a friend who's never heard it. If you can't yet, you haven't found it — reread.
  5. Find its similes and images. The Buddha almost always teaches through pictures. Which one carries the meaning best, and why?
  6. Connect it to a life — yours, or a student's. An untethered teaching is forgotten; a teaching tied to real experience stays.
Part 4

Your CapstonePrepare a ~15-minute teach-back

Come to our final session ready to teach me your discourse — about fifteen minutes, informal, as if I were the beginner and you the guide. Use this checklist to prepare:

Teach-back preparation checklist
  • I can set the scene of my discourse in two or three sentences.
  • I can state its core teaching in one plain sentence, no jargon.
  • I've chosen one simile or passage to read aloud and unpack.
  • I have one honest question or objection of my own about it (the ~ mark).
  • I can connect it to one real situation in a life.
  • I've prepared one question to ask me — turning the discussion around.

You'll be assessed gently and generously — this is a celebration of what you've learned, not an exam. Here's what I'll be looking for:

DimensionWhat a strong teach-back shows
ComprehensionYou've grasped what the discourse actually says — not a vague gist, but its real claim.
ClarityYou can say it plainly, to a beginner, without hiding behind Pali terms.
EngagementYou've wrestled with it honestly — including where you resist or still wonder.
ConnectionYou've tied it to real life, so it means something beyond the page.
Part 5

Final Reflection

Bring, in writing, a short response to these — the closing conversation of the course:

  1. Which single discourse across all eleven sessions changed something in how you actually live or see? What changed?
  2. What still puzzles or unsettles you — the question you'd want to keep studying?
  3. Now that you can read a sutta on your own: what will you read next, and why?

That third question isn't rhetorical. The course ends; the reading doesn't. An island unto yourself — with the Dhamma as your island — was always the point.

Where to go from here

You've read eleven discourses closely and can now open any of the remaining ones yourself. Natural next steps: read a full chapter of the Majjhima Nikāya in sequence, or branch into another collection entirely. And if you ever want to walk a stretch together again — you know where to find me.
