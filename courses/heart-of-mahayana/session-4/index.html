Session 4 · Compassion and wisdom as one practice, not two

What a Bodhisattva Actually Is

《華嚴經》〈淨行品〉 Jìngxíng Pǐn — 141 vows that turn washing your hands into the whole path
Before class: Sessions 1–3 (The Heart Sūtra and The Diamond Sūtra). This session assumes "no fixed nature" is already familiar — it puts that seeing to work rather than adding a new philosophical claim.
Part 1

Lecture Notes

From seeing through, to seeing everything as an opportunity

Three sessions have been spent learning to see that nothing — not form, not merit, not even the bodhisattva vow itself — has a fixed, independent nature. That seeing can start to feel like a private, almost philosophical achievement: something you understand, sitting with a text. This chapter asks the question those sessions were quietly building toward: now that you see it, what do you actually do, all day, with that seeing? Its very first verse answers by simply reusing what you already know. A bodhisattva at home — 當願眾生，知家性空，免其逼迫 — "the wish: may all beings know that 'home' has no fixed nature of its own, and so be freed from its pressures." Same teaching as Sessions 1 through 3. Not a negation this time — a wish.

One formula, repeated 141 times

Every verse in this chapter follows the same four-part shape: an ordinary moment (waking, washing, walking a road), the turn — the fixed phrase 當願眾生, "the wish: may all beings…" — then the wish itself, and nothing more. No verse explains itself. Seeing a bridge on the road: 若見橋道，當願眾生，廣度一切，猶如橋梁 — "may all beings be widely carried across, like a bridge." The pattern is the whole method: see, turn, wish, move on to the next thing.

The line that carries the whole chapter

Mañjuśrī is asked, at the start, a question with over a hundred parts — essentially, "what does a bodhisattva need, to be free of every fault and possess every virtue?" He answers first with one sentence, before the 141 verses ever begin: 若諸菩薩善用其心，則獲一切勝妙功德 — "if a bodhisattva skillfully applies the mind, every supreme and wondrous virtue follows." Consider what that collapses. The Diamond Sūtra spent thirty-two sections examining giving, merit, vows, and attainment from every angle. This chapter says the entire result of all of that is available in how the mind meets the next ordinary moment — no austerity, no special circumstance, no monastery required to begin.

Equal treatment: sunrises and toilets

The chapter gives the identical four-line treatment to a beautiful view and to using the toilet, to deep meditation and to putting on socks. This is deliberate, not careless. If liberation only shows up in rare, elevated states, then most of an actual life — the eating, the walking, the waiting — is wasted time the practice has nothing to say about. By treating every object the eye lands on as sufficient material for the whole path, the chapter makes compassion a habit of attention rather than an occasional mood — which is exactly why it has always resonated as much with laypeople doing dishes as with monastics on the road.

Why this matters before you read the full text
  • This is not a new teaching — it's the same one, redirected. "No fixed nature" from Sessions 1–3 shows up explicitly in this chapter's own verses. What changes is the form: negation becomes wish.
  • The whole bodhisattva path is claimed to fit inside one line. "Skillfully applying the mind" is offered as sufficient — not as a summary of a harder practice, but as the practice itself.
  • Nothing is too small to count. A text willing to spend four lines on a toilet is making an argument about where the sacred is allowed to live — everywhere, or nowhere.

Further reading — in progress

Part 3

Discussion Questions

  1. Compare the chapter's first verse — "may all beings know that 'home' has no fixed nature of its own" — to the Diamond Sūtra's "there is in truth no fixed thing called a bodhisattva." Same move, applied to different objects. What changes when it's expressed as a wish rather than a negation — for the person making it, if not for the philosophy itself?
  2. "If a bodhisattva skillfully applies the mind, every supreme and wondrous virtue follows" is offered as sufficient, not as a summary. Do you believe a single sentence can actually carry that much weight — or is the rest of the chapter doing real work the opening line only pretends to make unnecessary?
  3. The chapter treats a sunrise and a toilet identically. Pick one activity from your own day that you would never have thought to treat as "sacred material," and explain, in the chapter's own terms, why excluding it undermines the whole approach.
  4. Is a silently made wish just sentiment, or does it do something? Connect this to Diamond Sūtra Session 3's point that a bodhisattva's vow doesn't require a fixed self to keep it — does that discussion help explain why a wish, made and released rather than tracked, might be more than decoration?

Option A — Write your own day's verses

Pick five ordinary moments from your actual day — not from the sūtra's list — and write your own 當願眾生 verses for each, following the text's four-part form: the moment, the turn, the wish, nothing more.

Option B — Trace the emptiness thread

Find two or three more verses in the chapter, beyond the opening one, that explicitly draw on "no fixed nature" or similar emptiness language. For each, explain how it connects to specific material from Sessions 1 through 3.

Pilot complete

Four sessions, two methods, one teaching applied to an ordinary day.

Sessions 1 and 2–3 taught you to see that nothing — not form, not merit, not the bodhisattva vow itself — has a fixed, independent nature, first through the Heart Sūtra's negations, then through the Diamond Sūtra's questions. This session asked what that seeing is actually for, and answered with 141 wishes attached to the most repeated, least dramatic moments of a single day.

That is the course's title, taken literally: not wisdom in one text and compassion in another, but one mind meeting one moment, over and over, until seeing through and wishing well stop being two different things to practise.
