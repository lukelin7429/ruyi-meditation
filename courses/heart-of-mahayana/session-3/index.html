Session 3 · The bodhisattva's vow, and giving without a giver

The Diamond Sūtra, Part Two

《金剛般若波羅蜜經》 — Sections 17–32, the vow restated and merit no one keeps
Before class: Session 2 (The Diamond Sūtra, Part One). This session assumes Sections 1–16 and the "is not X, that is why it is called X" formula are already familiar.
Part 1

Lecture Notes

The same question, asked again

Section 17 opens with Subhūti asking, almost word for word, the same question he opened the whole sūtra with in Section 2: how should one stand, how should one subdue the mind? It is easy to read past this as repetition. It is not. The first time, the question opens a teaching that has not yet happened. The second time, it comes after fifteen sections of "is not X, that is why it is called X" have already been applied to merit, worlds, bodies, and fruits of practice. Read the two answers side by side and you can watch exactly what those fifteen sections were for — the shape of the practice doesn't change, but what you're allowed to hold onto while doing it does.

No fixed thing called "bodhisattva"

The vow itself gets the same treatment as everything else in the text. Section 17: a bodhisattva must resolve to liberate every being, and once every being is liberated, in truth no being at all has been liberated — and then, more pointedly, 「實無有法名為菩薩」, "there is in truth no fixed thing called a bodhisattva." This is not the sūtra taking back the vow. It's denying that keeping the vow requires there to be a solid, permanent "bodhisattva" standing behind the vowing — which is exactly the kind of self-notion the vow exists to dissolve.

Merit no one keeps: giving without a giver

Part One's Section 4 taught giving without dwelling on a giver, a gift, or a receiver. Part Two completes the picture from the far side of the transaction. Section 28: even a bodhisattva who gives away treasure filling worlds beyond counting 「不受福德」 — does not receive the merit. Subhūti asks the obvious question — how can you give and not receive? — and the answer is precise: 「菩薩所作福德，不應貪著」, a bodhisattva should not crave or cling to the merit they create. Not refusing the merit exists. Refusing to let it become one more thing collected by a self.

Part One taught you not to dwell on the giving. Part Two teaches you not to dwell on having given. The second is the harder discipline — it has to survive the moment after the good deed is already done, when there's finally something real to feel proud of.

One and many: is the universe a single thing?

Section 30 runs the "is not X, called X" formula on the largest possible object: a whole universe, ground into dust. The dust is not truly dust (each speck has no fixed essence); the universe, likewise, is not truly a universe — and if it were a real, solid thing, the text says, it would have to be 一合相, "a single, indivisible whole." That too is denied. If you've studied Collected Topics Session 2 (One and Many), this passage is worth returning to directly — the sūtra is asking exactly the question that session trains you to ask of any object: is it one thing, or many things, and what happens to that question when the object in front of you is literally everything?

The closing verse

The sūtra ends (Section 32) with its most chanted line: all conditioned things are like a dream, an illusion, a bubble, a shadow, like dew, like lightning. The sūtra's own reading guide unpacks why five different images do work that one alone couldn't — read it there before class.

Why this matters before you read the full text
  • The doubled question is the sūtra's own before-and-after. Sections 2 and 17 are the same words carrying a different weight — that difference is the whole middle of the text, compressed.
  • Not receiving merit isn't refusing merit. It's refusing to let merit become a possession — the same move as "giving without dwelling," applied after the giving is finished.
  • Even "the universe" doesn't survive the formula. If the sūtra is willing to deny a fixed essence to literally everything at once, that tells you how far the method is meant to reach — nothing is exempted for being too large or too sacred to examine.

Part 3

Discussion Questions

  1. Write out Section 2's question-and-answer and Section 17's question-and-answer side by side. In a sentence or two, what has actually changed between them — and what would a student who had only ever read Section 2 still be missing?
  2. Section 28 says a bodhisattva "does not receive merit" even while giving away treasure vaster than a universe. Using the giving-without-dwelling lesson from Part One, explain why "not receiving merit" is the same move as "giving without dwelling" — just applied to the other side of the transaction.
  3. Section 30 says a universe is neither truly "many" (a heap of dust) nor truly "one" (a single indivisible whole). If you've studied Collected Topics Session 2 (One and Many), does that framework help you see what's being denied here, or does this passage push past what that framework can handle? Explain.
  4. The closing verse uses five images — a dream, an illusion, a bubble, a shadow, dew, and lightning (six, depending how you count). Why not just one image? Pick two of the images and explain what each captures that the others might miss.

Option A — The doubled question

Place Section 2's question-and-answer next to Section 17's, and write a paragraph explaining exactly what fifteen sections of "is not X, that is why it is called X" changed about how the same question gets answered.

Option B — Explain "giving without a giver"

Write a short explanation, for someone who has never read the sūtra, of why a bodhisattva who tracks and takes quiet pride in their own generosity has, in the text's own terms, not yet completed the practice of giving — and what completing it would actually look like.
