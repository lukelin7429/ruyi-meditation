The Diamond Sūtra, Part One

《金剛般若波羅蜜經》 — Sections 1–16, and why no mark is ever the real thing
Before class: Collected Topics Session 3 (What a Definition Actually Is) is genuinely useful here, though not required.
Part 1

Lecture Notes

A text made of questions, not lists

Session 1 read a text built almost entirely out of negated nouns: no form, no eye, no suffering, no path. The Diamond Sūtra teaches the same emptiness a completely different way. The Buddha almost never simply asserts. He asks Subhūti a question, and Subhūti — the disciple foremost in understanding emptiness — has to work out the answer out loud, often catching and correcting his own first instinct mid-sentence. Where the Heart Sūtra hands you the finished insight, this sūtra hands you the method for reasoning your own way toward it. You are meant to notice yourself doing, silently, what Subhūti does out loud.

The engine underneath: "is not X, that is why it is called X"

Read a few sections and one pattern is impossible to miss. Section 8 is a clean first example: someone fills a billion-world universe with treasure and gives it all away — great merit, Subhūti says. Then: 「是福德即非福德性，是故如來說福德多」 — "this merit has no fixed nature as merit, that is exactly why the Tathāgata calls it great merit." Three beats, repeated for dozens of different words across the whole sūtra:

Name it — the word is granted its ordinary, functional use Negate it — denies the word points to a fixed, independent essence Rename it — the word returns to use, no longer mistaken for something solid

The formula is not a trick and not a paradox for its own sake. Each beat is doing real work, and the third beat is the one first-time readers most often skip past — without it, "is not X" reads as nihilism about language. With it, the sūtra is training you to use words without being captured by them.

Definitions that pass the test, and still aren't the real thing

If you've studied Collected Topics Session 3, you already have a formal tool for testing whether a definition is correct — whether it's too wide, too narrow, or exactly right. That tool and this sūtra are not doing the same job, and the difference is worth being precise about. A correct definition of "bodhisattva" could pass every test Session 3 gives you — include every real bodhisattva, exclude everyone who isn't one — and Section 17 would still say 「實無有法名為菩薩」, "there is in truth no fixed thing called a bodhisattva." The sūtra is not attacking bad definitions. It's attacking the assumption that even a perfectly correct definition proves something solid sits behind the word being defined.

A definition can be exactly right about where a word applies, and still say nothing at all about whether the word names a fixed essence. The Diamond Sūtra grants you the first and denies you the second — for every word in the text, including its own most sacred ones.

Giving without dwelling — the first practice, not a conclusion

Notice where the sūtra starts: not with argument, but with instruction. Section 4 teaches that a bodhisattva should give 「不住於相」 — without dwelling on a giver, a gift, or a receiver as fixed, separate things. This is the emptiness teaching arriving first as ethics, before it ever becomes philosophy. Giving without dwelling is giving without the quiet bookkeeping — I gave, they received, I am now generous — that turns generosity back into a transaction for the self. Its merit is called as unmeasurable as space in every direction, precisely because it was never collected as a possession.

The line that reached Huineng

Section 10 holds the sūtra's single most famous sentence — 應無所住而生其心, "give rise to a mind that dwells nowhere at all." Tradition says the illiterate woodcutter who became the Sixth Chan Patriarch, Huineng, first woke up on merely overhearing this one line recited in a marketplace, before he had studied a single scripture. The sūtra's own reading guide goes deep on this line and the story behind it — read it there before class.

Why this matters before you read the full text
  • "Is not X" is not nihilism about language. The third beat — "that is why it is called X" — always restores the word to ordinary use. Skip that beat and the whole formula is misread.
  • A correct definition and a fixed essence are two different claims. The sūtra can grant you the first while flatly denying the second, for the same word, in the same sentence.
  • Emptiness starts as a practice, not a theory. "Giving without dwelling" comes before any of the sūtra's harder philosophical moves — the ethics is the entry point, not an afterthought.

Part 3

Discussion Questions

  1. Take Section 8's "this merit has no fixed nature as merit, that is exactly why the Tathāgata calls it great merit." Walk through the three beats — name it, negate it, rename it — and explain what would be lost if the sūtra only did the first two beats and stopped.
  2. Suppose you wrote a technically correct definition of "bodhisattva" — one that passes Collected Topics Session 3's three-part test perfectly. Would that definition, on its own, satisfy what Section 17 is demanding when it says "there is in truth no fixed thing called a bodhisattva"? Why or why not?
  3. "Give rise to a mind that dwells nowhere at all" is easy to say and hard to picture. Apply it to one very ordinary activity — eating a meal, or replying to a text message. What would "not dwelling" actually look like in that specific case, moment to moment?
  4. Where did your first instinct land when you met "is not X, that is why it is called X" for the first time — as circular nonsense, or as doing real work? What, if anything, changed once you had the three-beat breakdown?

AssignmentChoose one · half to one page · bring it next session

Option A — Trace the formula twice more

Beyond the merit example from Section 8, find two more instances of "is not X, that is why it is called X" within Sections 1–16. For each, write out what "name it, negate it, rename it" is specifically doing to that word.

Option B — Explain the mind that dwells nowhere

Write a short explanation, for someone who has never read the sūtra, of what "a mind that dwells nowhere" means — and just as importantly, what it doesn't mean. (It is not the same as having no thoughts, and not the same as not caring.)
