Session 4 · The second fire, at its most extreme test case

The Saw

鋸喻經 — 就算被兩刃鋸切成兩段也不生瞋恨
Part 1

Lecture Notes

A monk who couldn't take criticism — on someone else's behalf

The occasion is the monk Phagguna, who has grown so close to a group of nuns that when they are criticized in his presence, he grows angry and quarrels on their behalf. The Buddha's response doesn't stay narrow. He uses this ordinary case of secondhand irritation to launch into a graduated teaching that ends somewhere far more demanding than the original incident.

The chariot, the sal grove, and a housewife named Vedehikā

Two similes bridge from the personal case to a general teaching: a master charioteer needing only the lightest touch of the reins to guide well-trained horses, and a sal grove choked by weeds that matures once the crooked saplings are cleared. Then comes the story of the housewife Vedehikā, famous for her gentleness — until her maid oversleeps once, and her composure turns out to have depended entirely on nothing ever going wrong.

Five ways speech can come at you

The sutta names five paired axes along which any criticism can fall: timely or untimely, true or false, gentle or harsh, beneficial or harmful, said with a mind of love or of hostility. Whichever combination lands, the training response is stated to be the same — this is a structural map of every possible way you can be spoken to, not advice for the easy cases only.

The limit case: bandits with a two-handed saw

The discourse's climax: "Even if low-down bandits were to sever you limb from limb with a two-handed saw, anyone who had a malevolent thought on that account would not be following my instructions." This is worth reading carefully rather than flinching from. It is not a moral demand made of beginners — the discourse is addressed to mendicants already deep in training, and the response it describes (spreading a heart of love to the attackers, and through them to the whole world) assumes a practice mature enough to do this. It describes what a mature practitioner can do, not a standard to feel guilty for missing.

Before you read the sutta itself
  • The real test of goodwill is what happens under criticism, not what happens when things go well. Vedehikā's gentleness was only ever tested by a single overslept morning — and failed.
  • Five categories cover every way speech can come at you. Timely/untimely, true/false, gentle/harsh, beneficial/harmful, loving/hostile — a structural map, not advice limited to the easy combinations.
  • The saw is a description of maturity, not a demand on beginners. It is addressed to mendicants already deep in training — a limit case that shows what the practice can eventually do, not a bar you should already be clearing.

Part 3

Discussion Questions

  1. Vedehikā's reputation for gentleness depended on nothing ever going wrong. Do you know of a reputation for patience or calm — your own, or someone else's — that has never actually been tested the way hers finally was?
  2. Of the five paired axes of speech (timely/untimely, true/false, gentle/harsh, beneficial/harmful, loving/hostile), which combination do you personally find hardest to receive with an unchanged mind — true-but-harsh? false-and-hostile? something else?
  3. The lecture notes insist the saw simile is "a description of maturity, not a demand on beginners." Do you find that reading convincing, or does the text's own placement and phrasing suggest otherwise?
  4. Phagguna's anger was on someone else's behalf, not his own. Is anger defending another person easier or harder to work with than anger defending yourself? Does this sutta's method apply equally to both?

AssignmentChoose one · half to one page · bring it next session

Option A — Map a real criticism onto the five axes

Recall a specific piece of criticism you received recently. Classify it honestly along all five axes (timing, truth, manner, benefit, and the speaker's state of mind), and describe what an unchanged, goodwill-based response would have looked like for that exact combination.

Option B — Distinguish the limit case from the daily standard

Write half a page on the difference between using the saw simile as inspiration (what patience can eventually become) versus as a standard you judge yourself against daily. Which use is this sutta actually making, based on its own text?
