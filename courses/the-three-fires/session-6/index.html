Session 6 · What extinguishing the three fires is actually called

The Ending of the Fires

應答閻浮車經 — 涅槃的最短定義
Part 1

Lecture Notes

A wanderer's blunt question

Sāriputta is staying near Nālakagāmaka in Magadha when the wanderer Jambukhādaka approaches, exchanges greetings, sits down, and asks his question directly: what is extinguishment (Nibbāna)? No elaborate setup, no framing story beyond this — and, notably, this bare exchange becomes the template the rest of this entire chapter of the canon (saṃyutta) will reuse, with only the term under discussion changing, for fourteen further discourses.

The shortest possible definition

Sāriputta's answer arrives in a single breath: "the ending of greed, the ending of hatred, the ending of delusion." This is the exact same three fires this course has spent five sessions naming together, then splitting apart, then examining one at a time. They return here, unglossed, as the definition itself — not a side effect of the goal, not one path toward it among several, but the thing itself, stated as plainly as a definition can be stated.

Not elaborated further

What is notable is what doesn't happen next. Sāriputta doesn't unpack the definition, doesn't qualify it, doesn't add nuance. After five sessions built from seven methods, five categories of speech, and sixteen repeated four-part analyses, the arrival at the actual goal is almost startlingly spare — as if all that architecture existed to make this one sentence fully graspable, not to replace it with something more complicated.

The path, once more

Jambukhādaka's natural follow-up — is there a path to this? — receives the same answer this course has already met once, in Session 3: the noble eightfold path, spelled out again in full. The exchange closes with Jambukhādaka's own approval: "just this much is enough for diligence." The same path that answered one narrow question about greed alone turns out to be the answer to the ending of all three fires together.

Before you read the sutta itself
  • The goal of this entire course has the shortest definition of anything studied in it. One sentence, given in a single breath, with nothing added afterward.
  • All three fires from Session 2 return here, unglossed, as the definition of the thing that ends them. Not a side effect of awakening — stated as the definition itself.
  • The same eightfold path answers both the narrow question (Session 3, greed alone) and the total one (this session, all three fires). One path, asked to do a small job and the whole job, without needing to change.

Discussion Questions

  1. Why might the tradition preserve such a spare, unelaborated exchange as canonical, rather than a more developed philosophical treatment of Nibbāna? What does the brevity itself communicate?
  2. This definition names the ending of the same three fires Session 2's Fire Sermon set ablaze. Having now studied each fire individually across Sessions 3 through 5, does this one-sentence definition feel more or less adequate to you than it would have before this course?
  3. The eightfold path answers both a narrow question (Session 3: how do I get rid of greed specifically?) and this session's total question (what is the complete ending of all three fires?). What does it mean that the same answer scales from one problem to all of them?
  4. Looking back across all six sessions — the seven methods of Session 1, the totalizing fire of Session 2, the single-fire treatments of Sessions 3 through 5, and this session's spare definition — what would you now say, in your own words, is this course's single underlying claim about how the mind actually changes?
