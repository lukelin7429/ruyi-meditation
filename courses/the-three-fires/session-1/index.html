Session 1 · The mechanism underneath all three fires

Where Defilements Actually Come From

一切漏經 — 煩惱不是意志力問題，是注意力問題
Part 1

Lecture Notes

Not a battle of willpower

The popular picture of overcoming greed, hatred, and delusion is a contest of willpower — resist the urge, grit your teeth, want it less. MN 2 offers a different, far more precise mechanism, and it has almost nothing to do with willpower. It has to do with attention: specifically, what kind of attention you were just paying to whatever arose.

Rational and irrational attention

The sutta's whole architecture rests on one distinction. Irrational attention (ayoniso manasikāra) is what makes an unarisen defilement arise, and an arisen one grow. Rational attention (yoniso manasikāra) does the reverse — it is what keeps a defilement from arising, and lets an arisen one fade. This is not a claim that thinking hard fixes everything; it is a claim that the entry point for defilements is a specific, identifiable cognitive event, not a vague failure of character.

Seven methods, not one

Having located the mechanism, the sutta refuses to give one universal technique. It gives seven — taints given up by seeing, by restraining, by using requisites properly, by enduring, by avoiding, by removing, and by developing the factors of awakening. Which method applies depends entirely on which kind of situation you are actually in. A wound needing avoidance is not treated the same way as an itch needing endurance; the sutta's discipline is diagnostic before it is prescriptive.

The three āsavas this course will keep meeting

The taints (āsava) named here — the taint of sensual desire, the taint of desire for continued existence, the taint of ignorance — are close cousins of the three roots this course spends the next five sessions on, but not identical to them. Worth holding both frameworks side by side rather than collapsing one into the other: the āsavas are what flow out and keep the round of existence going; greed, hatred, and delusion are the roots that generate unwholesome action. They overlap heavily and are not the same map.

Before you read the sutta itself
  • The three roots aren't fought with willpower. They're managed by what the mind attends to — a specific, locatable event, not a vague matter of character.
  • Seven different situations get seven different methods. Not one universal technique — the sutta diagnoses which kind of taint you're facing before it prescribes anything.
  • The āsavas here are cousins of the three roots, not identical to them. Two related maps of the same territory — worth keeping both, rather than collapsing one into the other, across this course.

Part 3

Discussion Questions

  1. Pick one recent moment when a craving or an irritation grew stronger the more you thought about it. Using this sutta's language, what would "irrational attention" have looked like in that moment, and what would the rational alternative have been?
  2. The sutta refuses a single universal technique in favor of seven situational ones. Is there a risk in having seven different methods rather than one simple rule? What does the sutta gain by being this specific?
  3. Compare the āsavas (sensual desire, desire for existence, ignorance) to the three roots this course will spend the next five sessions on (greed, hatred, delusion). Where do they clearly overlap, and where do they seem to name genuinely different things?
  4. Of the seven methods (seeing, restraining, using, enduring, avoiding, removing, developing), which one do you already use most often in your own life, and which one do you suspect you underuse?

Option A — Diagnose your own taint
