Session 5 · The third fire, and why its antidote is a skill, not a belief

Seeing Through Delusion

正見經 — 無明的對治不是相信對的事，是會用一個方法
Part 1

Lecture Notes

Sixteen ways in, one underlying structure

This sutta looks, at first glance, like an enormous list: the wholesome and the unwholesome, nutriment, the four noble truths, and then the full chain of dependent origination worked backward — aging-and-death, birth, existence, clinging, craving, feeling, contact, the six sense fields, name-and-form, consciousness, choices, and ignorance itself. Sixteen separate topics, each apparently requiring its own explanation.

The four-fold move, repeated sixteen times

What actually holds the sutta together is not content but method: for each of the sixteen topics, the same four-part question is asked — what is it, what is its origin, what is its cessation, and what is the way leading to its cessation. The list is long because the method is being demonstrated on enough different material to prove it generalizes, not because sixteen unrelated doctrines needed covering.

Right view is a capacity, not a belief

This reframes what "right view" even means. It is not a fixed set of correct propositions to memorize and hold. It is the trained ability to apply that same four-fold understanding to whatever phenomenon happens to arise next — including ones not on this list. A student who has genuinely learned right view can extend it to a seventeenth topic the sutta never mentions.

The loop closes — and starts again

Tracing the chain backward, ignorance itself is eventually traced to the taints (āsava) — the exact term Session 1 opened this course with. The loop closes on itself: ignorance feeds the taints, and the taints (per MN 2's own logic) feed ignorance. This course's first and second-to-last sessions turn out to be describing the same circuit from opposite ends.

Before you read the sutta itself
  • The antidote to delusion is a transferable skill, not a memorized answer. The four-fold move — what is it, its origin, its cessation, the path to its cessation — is what makes it "right view" rather than "a right opinion."
  • The same structure runs identically across sixteen different topics. Repetition here is proof the method generalizes, not padding — the point is that it would work on a seventeenth topic too.
  • This sutta's own chain loops back to Session 1's āsavas. Ignorance is traced to the taints, closing a circuit this course opened with — the beginning and the near-end of this course meet inside the sutta itself.

Part 3

Discussion Questions

  1. Pick one of the sixteen topics not covered in class (for instance, "choices" or "name-and-form") and try running the four-fold move on it yourself: what is it, its origin, its cessation, the path to its cessation. Does the method hold up on a topic you didn't see demonstrated?
  2. Try applying the four-fold move to something entirely outside this sutta's list — a bad habit, a recurring argument, a piece of your own teaching that isn't working. Does the structure still generalize?
  3. The sutta traces ignorance back to the āsavas from Session 1, and MN 2 traces the āsavas back to irrational attention, which is arguably a form of not-seeing (ignorance). Is this circular reasoning, or a genuine description of a self-sustaining loop? What would break the loop, according to these two suttas together?
  4. "Right view is a capacity, not a belief" is a strong claim. Can you think of a case, in your own experience of religious or philosophical teaching generally, where the same distinction — a transferable method versus a fixed set of correct answers — clearly mattered?
