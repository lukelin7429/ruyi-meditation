Session 2 · The three fires, named together

Everything Is Burning

燃燒經 — 貪瞋痴同時點著六根六境
Part 1

Lecture Notes

An audience already primed for fire

This sutta — the famous "Fire Sermon" — was delivered to an audience of newly converted ascetics who had spent their previous religious life tending sacred fires. The Buddha does not introduce fire as an arbitrary metaphor; he meets an audience that already lives inside fire imagery, and redirects it toward something they had not yet been asked to examine: their own eyes, ears, and mind.

Defining "the all"

Before naming what burns, the sutta defines exactly what counts as "everything": the eye, forms, eye-consciousness, eye-contact, and the feeling — pleasant, painful, or neither — that arises from that contact. Then it repeats the identical structure for the ear, the nose, the tongue, the body, and the mind. Nothing about ordinary experience is left outside this definition. There is no seventh sense held in reserve, uncontaminated.

The formula, six times over

Having defined "the all," the sutta declares every part of it burning — burning with the fire of greed, burning with the fire of hatred, burning with the fire of delusion, and burning further with birth, old age, and death, with sorrow, lamentation, pain, sadness, and distress. This is not one fire with three names; it is stated, three separate times, as three separate fires, doing separate damage.

From burning to freedom

A instructed noble disciple, seeing this, grows disenchanted with all six sense bases — and disenchantment leads to dispassion, dispassion to freedom. The tradition's own account of this sutta is extraordinary: it reports that the entire audience of a thousand formerly fire-worshipping ascetics became arahants by the sutta's end, without further teaching. Whatever one makes of that report, the text itself is built to be total in scope — it does not exempt a single sense from the diagnosis.

Before you read the sutta itself
  • All three fires are named together here, before this course pulls them apart one at a time. Sessions 3 through 5 each take one; this sutta insists on seeing all three at once first.
  • Nothing in ordinary experience is exempt. Every sense, every object of sense, every act of knowing, every feeling that follows — all six sense-doors, without exception, get the identical formula.
  • Seeing correctly is what does the letting-go — not effort applied on top of seeing. The sutta's own sequence is disenchantment first, then dispassion, then freedom — not the other way around.

Part 3

Discussion Questions

  1. Why might the Buddha choose to address this teaching specifically to an audience of former fire-worshippers? What does meeting an audience inside their own existing frame accomplish that a fresh metaphor wouldn't?
  2. The sutta insists all six sense bases burn, with no exception. Is there a sense-door you tend to think of as "safer" or less implicated in greed, hatred, and delusion than the others? Does this sutta support that intuition?
  3. The sequence given is disenchantment, then dispassion, then freedom. Have you experienced a moment where simply seeing something clearly — without any additional effort — was what actually loosened your grip on it?
  4. Compare this sutta's method (name the fires all at once, totally) to Session 1's method (seven distinct, situational techniques). Are these in tension, or do they work at different stages of the same process?

Option A — Run the formula on one sense-door

Pick a single sense-door (sight, hearing, or mind) and write out, in your own words, what "burning with greed, burning with hatred, burning with delusion" actually looks like for you at that door, with one concrete recent example of each.

Option B — Teach the fire metaphor without doctrine

Explain, to someone with no background in Buddhism, why calling craving or resentment a "fire" is more accurate than calling it a "habit" or a "feeling." What does the fire image capture that those words miss?
