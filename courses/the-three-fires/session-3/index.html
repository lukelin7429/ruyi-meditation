Session 3 · The first fire, and one specific method for it

Removing Greed

旃陀經 — 貪為什麼被特別挑出來，怎麼拿掉
Part 1

Lecture Notes

A specific, pointed question

The wanderer Channa approaches Venerable Ānanda with a direct demand, not a philosophical inquiry: how does one get rid of greed, hatred, and delusion? This is the practical question underneath everything this course has read so far, asked plainly, by someone outside the community, expecting a real answer rather than a deflection.

Why greed is singled out first

Of the three, greed gets picked out here for a specific reason worth sitting with: greed is objected to because of what it does to seeing. This connects directly back to Session 1's mechanism — greed is not simply an intense wanting; it actively distorts what the mind is able to perceive accurately while it is active. A craving mind is not a clear-eyed mind that also happens to want something.

The answer: the same eightfold path, aimed

Ānanda does not offer Channa a specialized anti-greed technique unavailable elsewhere. He points to the noble eightfold path — right view, right thought, right speech, right action, right livelihood, right effort, right mindfulness, right immersion — as the method. Nothing new is introduced; the entire, ordinary path is presented as the answer to this one narrow, urgent question.

Using it

What makes this exchange worth studying as pedagogy rather than only as doctrine is what it doesn't do. It doesn't tell Channa to come back once he has already made progress elsewhere. It doesn't ask him to first accept a larger framework. A specific question receives a specific, actionable answer — the same eightfold path that answers everything else in the tradition, pointed, on request, at exactly the problem the asker named.

Before you read the sutta itself
  • Greed's danger here is stated precisely: what it does to seeing. Not simply an intense want — a distortion of perception itself, while it's active.
  • The remedy is not a specialized technique. It's the same eightfold path used for everything else in the tradition, offered here in direct answer to one narrow question.
  • A narrow, practical question gets a real, specific answer. Channa isn't told to come back later or accept a bigger framework first — the path is simply named, immediately, as the answer to exactly what he asked.

Part 3

Discussion Questions

  1. "Greed is objected to because of what it does to seeing." Test this against your own experience: has wanting something ever changed what you were able to notice about it, or about the situation around it?
  2. Channa asks a direct question and receives a direct answer — no requirement to first accept a larger framework. Is there a lesson here for how you handle a student's blunt, practical question in your own teaching ("just tell me how to do X")?
  3. Why might the same eightfold path serve as the answer to a question about greed specifically, rather than a technique built just for greed? What does that reuse suggest about how the path is meant to function?
  4. Channa is described as a wanderer, not a follower of the Buddha. Does it matter, pedagogically, that Ānanda gives this same core answer to someone outside the community, rather than reserving it for insiders?

Option A — Greed and distorted seeing

Write half a page describing one specific instance where you can now see, in hindsight, that wanting something distorted your perception of it or of the situation around it — using this sutta's own framing rather than a generic "I was blinded by desire."

Option B — Answer a narrow question directly

Recall a time a student asked you a narrow, practical question ("just tell me how to pass this") and you gave a broader, more general answer instead. Rewrite your response the way Ānanda answers Channa — direct, specific, immediately usable.
