Four Hundred Characters

四百字讀經 · four sessions

The Academy's first five layers — 0 through 4 — taught you the Dharma in English, from texts translated on this site. This layer teaches you to read those texts in Chinese — not fluently, and not all of them, but enough that a bilingual page becomes something you read rather than something you admire.

Assumes no Chinese at all You will not write, speak, or listen — only read By the end one complete sutra, in the original
The premise

Buddhist Chinese is a small language

To read a Chinese newspaper you need something like three thousand characters. Scripture is not a newspaper. It is a closed body of translated text, produced by a handful of workshops over roughly a thousand years, and it repeats itself relentlessly — which is precisely what makes it learnable by an adult with an hour a week.

We counted every sutra in the Taishō canon — 1,482 texts, 26,422,454 characters — and built the frequency list. Here is what it says.

The course is named for the four hundred mark. That is not a finish line — it is the point where a page of scripture stops being a wall and becomes a text with gaps in it, which is a completely different situation to be in.

The method

Formulas, not flashcards

Nobody reads a sutra character by character, and this course does not ask you to start that way. Scripture is built from fixed blocks — an opening frame, a speech formula, a gesture of respect, a transliterated Sanskrit name — that recur unchanged across hundreds of texts with a few variable slots. You learn the block, and the variable slots are where your attention goes.

This also solves the problem that stops most beginners. Scripture's rarest characters almost never appear alone; they arrive inside those blocks. 祇樹給孤獨園 contains four characters you would meet almost nowhere else, and you will never need to know them individually. Your four hundred characters get spent on connective tissue instead.

The sessions

Four sittings, one sutra at the end

Sessions 1 to 3 walk straight down the opening of a single text, the Diamond Sutra, in order — because the only honest way to learn to read scripture is to read some. Session 4 changes texts and reads the Heart Sutra entire.

  • Session 1

    How Every Sutra Opens

    如是我聞：一時，佛在舍衛國祇樹給孤獨園

    The formula that opens four in every ten sutras in the canon — and why the same twenty-two characters begin both the Diamond Sutra and the Amitabha Sutra, one character apart.

  • Session 2

    At That Time, the World-Honored One

    爾時，世尊告……言

    The canon gives you no quotation marks and no tenses, yet you can always tell who addressed whom. Speech in Classical Chinese carries its social direction in the verb.

  • Session 3

    The Grammar of Not

    無 · 不 · 非

    The most common character in the Buddhist canon is 無. The second is 不. Three negations, three different jobs, and the distinction between them is the highest-yield hour in the course.

  • Session 4

    Two Hundred Sixty Characters

    般若波羅蜜多心經

    The Heart Sutra is 260 characters long and uses only 117 distinct ones. You read all of it, in order, to the last syllable of the mantra. Nothing is summarized.

What this course does not claim

It will not make you fluent, it does not teach you to write or speak, and it covers four sessions of a subject that could fill forty. What it gives you is the threshold where the Chinese column of a bilingual page becomes usable: you read the original, get most of it, and check the English for the rest — instead of reading the English and glancing respectfully at the Chinese. Every translation on this site is laid out for exactly that.
