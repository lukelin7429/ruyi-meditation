Session 4 · The mechanism's own exit door

The Deed That Ends All Deeds

業經（詳細）— 第四種業，不是去哪裡，而是離開整個機制
Part 1

Lecture Notes

The mechanism, traced step by step

For each of three kinds of deed, this discourse gives the same five-step chain: a person makes choices by body, speech, and mind of a particular character; having made them, they are reborn in a world matching that character; in that world, contact of the same character strikes them; touched by that contact, they experience feeling of the same character. This is worth reading as a description of causal fit, not reward and punishment imposed from outside — nothing intervenes to judge or assign the consequence. The world one meets is simply what a choice of that character produces, the way a shape produces a matching shadow.

Three destinations, precisely named

Dark deeds lead to feelings that are exclusively painful, "like the beings in hell." Bright deeds lead to feelings of perfect happiness, "like the gods of universal beauty" — a specific, named class of deity, not heaven in a generic sense. Mixed deeds lead to a mixture of pleasure and pain, "like humans, some gods, and some beings in the underworld" — including the plain observation that ordinary human life, mixed in its pleasures and pains, sits exactly where mixed deeds should place it.

The fourth kind: not a destination, a departure

The pivot of the whole discourse: the fourth kind of deed is defined as the intention to give up each of the first three. Not a fourth choice alongside hurtful, pleasing, and mixed ones, and not a fourth world to be reborn into — the discourse withholds the entire rebirth-contact-feeling chain from this fourth item, because it does not lead to a world at all. It leads, in the discourse's own word, to the ending of deeds — not a better rebirth, but the cessation of the whole mechanism by which rebirths are produced.

Renunciation as intention, not inaction

It matters enormously that the fourth kind is defined as an intention — the same word, cetanā, that Session 1 named as the root of every kind of kamma. The way out of the mechanism is not the absence of intention; it is a specific intention, aimed at the mechanism itself rather than at any particular hurtful, pleasing, or mixed act. The fourth kind of deed is still, unmistakably, a kind of deed — this discourse is the clearest evidence within its own chapter of why "the Buddha teaches doing nothing at all" misdescribes the teaching.

Before you read the sutta itself
  • The three ordinary kinds of deed describe causal fit, not judgment from outside. A world, its contact, and its feeling are simply what a choice of that character produces — the way a shape produces a matching shadow.
  • The fourth kind of deed leads nowhere at all, on purpose. The discourse withholds the rebirth-contact-feeling chain from it entirely, because it ends the mechanism rather than feeding it.
  • Renunciation is still an intention, not the absence of one. The same word that defines every other kind of kamma in Session 1 also defines the deed that ends kamma — this is not inaction, it is a different, specific choice.

AN 4.233 — Deeds in Detail

Hurtful choices lead to a hurtful rebirth and purely painful feelings; pleasing choices lead to a pleasing rebirth and perfect happiness; mixed choices lead to a mixed rebirth and mixed feelings; and the intention to give up all three kinds of choice is what ends the whole mechanism.

Part 3

Discussion Questions

  1. The lecture notes describe the first three kinds of kamma as "causal fit," like a shape producing a matching shadow, rather than reward and punishment from an outside judge. Does this reframing change how the whole course's material — the seven pairs of Session 2, the complexity of Session 3 — feels to you?
  2. Why might it matter, philosophically and practically, that the fourth kind of deed is still defined as an intention rather than simply the absence of choosing? What would be lost if renunciation were taught as "stop choosing" rather than "choose this specific thing instead"?
  3. Ordinary human life is explicitly placed in the "mixed" category — mixed pleasure and pain, like the mixed deeds that produce it. Does locating ordinary life on this same three-part scale as hell and heaven change how you think about what an "ordinary" life actually is?
  4. Looking back across all four sessions of this course, how would you now explain, in one paragraph, what "kamma" actually means — to someone who has only ever heard it used loosely, outside any Buddhist context, to mean something like fate or karma-as-cosmic-payback?
