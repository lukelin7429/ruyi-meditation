Session 3 · The limit of inference, even from something you actually witnessed

Why the Same Deed Doesn't Always Lead to the Same Result

大業分別經 — 從一個個案跳到普遍法則的陷阱
Before class: Session 1's account of kamma ripening across three different timescales (this life, the next life, or later still) is the key that unlocks this session.
Part 1

Lecture Notes

A wanderer's overclaim

The wanderer Potaliputta reports to Ānanda a claim he heard directly from the Buddha: that whatever a person experiences — pleasant, painful, or neutral — is entirely caused by past action. Ānanda corrects him: the Buddha never taught anything so absolute. A junior mendicant, overhearing and imagining the Teacher needs defending, only makes the confusion worse — setting up the Buddha's own much more careful "great analysis of deeds."

Four individuals who cut against the obvious expectation

The Buddha names four kinds of people, deliberately chosen to unsettle any tidy correlation: someone who kills, steals, and holds wrong view, reborn in hell; someone with the identical conduct, reborn in a heavenly realm; someone who refrains from all of it and holds right view, reborn in a heavenly realm; and someone with that identical good conduct, reborn in hell. Stated this baldly, it looks like Session 2's whole structure collapses. It doesn't — but seeing why takes the rest of the discourse.

What the Buddha grants — and what he doesn't

Four ascetics have each observed one of these four cases directly, through genuine meditative vision, and each leaps from their one true observation to a false universal law — "everyone who kills is reborn in hell," or its opposite. The Buddha's response to all four follows one exact pattern: he grants that the general tendency is real, and he grants the specific case they actually witnessed. But he refuses to grant the leap to "everyone who does this ends up there" — "because the Realized One's knowledge of the great analysis of deeds is otherwise."

The resolution: timing, not exception

The killer reborn in heaven may be experiencing the ripening of an earlier good deed, or may have died with a wholesome state of mind — even while the killing itself still has its own painful fruit waiting, in this life, the next, or later still. Nothing is cancelled or wasted; the ledger is simply far longer and more layered than a single lifetime's visible conduct can show. Session 2's seven pairs remain a real, reliable tendency — this discourse explains why any single observed case can still look like an exception without actually being one.

Before you read the sutta itself
  • A genuinely observed single case does not license a universal law. All four ascetics saw something real. All four overreached in exactly the same way when they generalized from it.
  • The Buddha's own response distinguishes granting the particular from granting the universal. He confirms what each ascetic actually witnessed while explicitly refusing the "everyone who..." claim built on top of it.
  • The apparent exception is resolved by timing, not by exception. Deeds ripen across three timescales (Session 1) — a visible result can be the fruit of an earlier or later deed entirely, not evidence that the pattern from Session 2 is false.

MN 136 — The Longer Analysis of Deeds

The same visible result — a killer reborn well, a virtuous person reborn badly — can arise from entirely different underlying causes, so no one, however psychically gifted, is entitled to leap from a single observed case to a universal law about how kamma works.

Part 3

Discussion Questions

  1. The four ascetics each had a genuine meditative vision of a real case — this isn't a story about people being wrong about what they saw. What exactly was their error, if not seeing incorrectly?
  2. Compare this sutta's core move (grant the particular case, refuse the universal claim built on it) to the Kālāma Sutta's method of testing claims by consequences rather than authority. Are these the same underlying epistemic discipline, applied in different directions?
  3. Can you think of a case in your own life — or in public discussion generally — where someone generalized from one true, personally witnessed case ("I know someone who...") to a universal law that doesn't actually hold?
  4. Does this session's teaching about timing and complexity weaken or strengthen Session 2's seven paired tendencies, in your own assessment? Defend your answer using both suttas' own language.

Identify one belief you hold that was formed by generalizing from a single vivid case you personally witnessed or experienced. Using this sutta's own pattern (grant the particular, question the universal), write half a page examining whether the generalization actually holds.

Option B — Teach the difference between the particular and the universal
