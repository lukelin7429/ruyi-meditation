Session 3 · The first foundation, in full

The Body as Ground

Mindfulness of the body — a steadier home for attention than the breath alone
Before class: Complete Session 2 first — this session assumes noting is at least familiar, even if it still feels a little effortful.
Part 1

Teaching

A bigger home than the nostrils

The breath is a fine anchor, but it's a narrow one — a small patch of sensation at the nose, or a little motion at the belly. This week widens the anchor to the whole body. In the traditional four foundations of mindfulness (satipaṭṭhāna), the body comes first, and for a practical reason: it's the most stable, least abstract object you have. Thoughts come and go in a flash. The body is simply here, the whole time, offering something to feel in almost every part of it, right now, without your having to produce anything.

The body scan

The technique this week is a body scan: moving attention slowly and deliberately through the body, region by region, simply noticing whatever is actually there — warmth, coolness, pressure, tingling, tightness, or sometimes nothing detectable at all. You are not trying to relax the body, fix anything, or make a sensation appear. You are only visiting each area and noticing its honest, present report, then moving to the next.

This connects directly to last week's noting. When the mind wanders off the body entirely — and it still will — note it exactly as before ("thinking," "planning") and simply resume the scan from wherever attention left off. Noting and the body scan are not two separate practices; noting is what you do the moment the body stops being where you are, and the scan is what you return to.

Why "no sensation" still counts

A very common experience the first few times: some areas — often the back, or the fingers, or the middle of the back of the head — report almost nothing at all. That absence is not a failure of the technique or a sign you're doing it wrong. Numbness and vagueness are simply what some areas of the body report under quiet attention, at least at first, and noticing "nothing much here" is itself an accurate, successful observation. The scan is training a habit of checking in, not a demand that every region produce a vivid result.

You're not trying to feel more. You're only trying to notice, honestly, whatever is already there.
Three ideas to carry out of today
  • The body is always available. Unlike a thought, it doesn't have to be produced — it's already there, the entire sit, offering something to notice.
  • Noting still applies. When attention leaves the body for a thought, note it as before, then simply resume the scan.
  • "Nothing much here" is a valid result. Numb or unclear areas are honest data, not evidence you're doing the scan wrong.
Part 2

Guided InstructionsA slow body scan, head to feet

Ten to fifteen minutes this week, seated as usual. Give each region a few unhurried breaths before moving on — this is not a race through the body.

A Slow Body Scan

Settle into your posture. Take one slow breath, longer on the way out, and let your eyes close or soften.

Bring attention to the top of the head, and simply notice whatever is there for a few breaths — pressure, temperature, or nothing distinct. Then let attention move to the face: forehead, eyes, jaw. Notice any tension without trying to release it — just notice.

Move down through the neck and shoulders, then slowly down each arm to the hands and fingers. Then the chest and belly, feeling them rise and fall with the breath. Then the back, the hips, and down each leg to the feet.

If the mind wanders off the body at any point, note it — "thinking," "planning," whatever fits — and simply pick the scan back up from wherever you left it. There's no need to start over.

Once you've reached the feet, let attention rest on the whole body at once for the final minute or two — the entire shape of it, sitting here, breathing.

Part 4

Common Obstacles

I can't feel anything in most of my body.

Completely normal, especially in the first week or two. Stay with each region for a few breaths regardless of whether anything registers, and simply note "unclear" or "nothing much" as an honest result. Clarity tends to increase gradually with repetition — it isn't something to force.

I fall asleep or get drowsy partway through the scan.

Common, since the scan is slower and more relaxing than watching the breath alone. Try sitting slightly more upright, opening your eyes partway if needed, or doing the scan a little faster on drowsy days. Falling asleep occasionally isn't a disaster — just note it in the log and continue the next day.

An area is uncomfortable or painful, not just neutral.

Notice it the same honest way as any other sensation — you don't need to fix it or move away immediately. But real physical pain is not something to grit your teeth through for the sake of the practice; if a sensation is sharp or concerning, simply shift your posture and continue. The scan is meant to build awareness, not tolerance for injury.

Should I combine the body scan with noting every single wandering thought, or just let the scan run?

Note it when you catch it, but don't go hunting for wandering thoughts during the scan — the body itself is the primary object this week. If you notice you've drifted, a quiet note and a return to the scan is enough; there's no need to interrupt the scan's rhythm more than that.

Part 5

Reflection

At the end of the week, look back over your log and sit with these — no need to write pages, a line or two each is enough.

  1. Which area of the body came through most clearly across the week, and which stayed the vaguest?
  2. Did the body scan feel calmer or more restless than last week's breath-and-noting practice? What was different about the experience?
  3. Did you notice any connection between what was happening in your day and where you held tension in the body?
  4. Compared to the breath alone, does having the whole body as an anchor change how it feels to sit down?
