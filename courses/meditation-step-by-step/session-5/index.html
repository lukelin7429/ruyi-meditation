Session 5 · Five patterns, five specific responses

The Five Hindrances

Desire, aversion, sloth, restlessness, doubt — and what to actually do when one shows up
Before class: Complete Session 4 first — recognizing pleasant, unpleasant, and neutral tone makes these five much easier to catch early.
Part 1

Teaching

Five familiar visitors, with names

Every obstacle a settled mind runs into during meditation, across every tradition, tends to fall into one of five recurring patterns. The tradition calls them the five hindrances (nīvaraṇa) — not because they're rare or exotic, but because they are exactly the ordinary interruptions you've almost certainly already met in every sit so far: wanting something else, not wanting what's here, feeling too heavy to continue, feeling too agitated to settle, or wondering if any of this is actually working. Naming them precisely is itself useful — a hindrance you can name is already a little less overwhelming than a vague, formless static.

1. Sensory Desire kāmacchanda
Wanting something other than what's here — a different sensation, a snack, a more comfortable position, the sit to be over. Response: name it plainly ("wanting"), and ask what's actually being sought underneath — usually just comfort or escape, both of which pass on their own if left unfed.
2. Ill Will vyāpāda
Aversion, irritation, or resistance — toward a sensation, a thought, the practice itself, or someone entirely unrelated who drifts into mind. Response: name it ("aversion"), and soften the posture and jaw slightly rather than bracing against it — resistance to the aversion usually intensifies it.
3. Sloth and Torpor thīna-middha
Heaviness, dullness, drowsiness — the mind going foggy or the body wanting to slump. Response: name it ("dull" or "drowsy"), then actively brighten: sit up straighter, open the eyes partway, or take three slightly deeper breaths.
4. Restlessness and Worry uddhacca-kukkucca
Agitation, an unsettled body, or a mind circling anxiously over something said or left undone. Response: name it ("restless"), and rather than fighting it, let attention rest more broadly on the whole body's stillness — the seat, the floor — rather than a single narrow point.
5. Doubt vicikicchā
Uncertainty about the practice, the teacher, or your own capacity — "is this working," "am I doing this right." Response: name it ("doubting"), and treat it exactly like any other passing mental event rather than a verdict to resolve mid-sit — most doubt is simply another hindrance, not a real argument that needs answering right now.

Not a personal flaw — a passing weather pattern

A hindrance arising is not evidence you're bad at meditation, and it isn't a permanent trait of your mind either. It's a temporary condition, the way weather is temporary — present now, gone later, likely to return again at some point, and none of that a verdict on you. The tradition's confidence about these five comes precisely from how universal and repeatable they are: everyone who has ever sat down to practice meets exactly these five, in roughly this range of disguises, again and again.

A hindrance you can name is a hindrance you're already halfway out of noticing — a passing state, not a permanent fact about you.
Three ideas to carry out of today
  • Five, not infinite. Almost every obstacle you'll meet in practice reduces to one of these five familiar patterns.
  • Each has its own specific response. Not one generic fix — desire, aversion, dullness, restlessness, and doubt each call for a different concrete move.
  • It's weather, not identity. A hindrance arising says nothing permanent about you — it's a passing state the whole tradition expects and has tools for.
Part 2

Guided InstructionsNaming the hindrance, applying its response

Ten to fifteen minutes, seated as usual. This week, when something pulls you off the breath or body, check it against the five before simply noting "thinking."

Naming the Hindrance

Settle into your posture, and take one slow breath, longer on the way out.

Rest attention on the breath or body, as in earlier sessions. When something pulls you away strongly enough to notice, pause for a moment and ask: is this desire, aversion, dullness, restlessness, or doubt?

Name whichever one fits, plainly — "wanting," "aversion," "dull," "restless," "doubting." If none of the five quite fit, a plain "thinking" is still completely fine.

Apply that hindrance's specific response briefly — soften for aversion, brighten for dullness, broaden attention for restlessness — then return to the breath or body.

Spend the last few minutes simply resting and noticing whichever hindrances arise on their own, without hunting for them.

Part 4

Common Obstacles

I have two or three hindrances at once and can't tell which one to name.

Name whichever feels strongest in that moment — precision isn't the goal, noticing is. If they're genuinely tangled together, "restless" or "unsettled" as a general label is fine; the categories are a tool for clarity, not a strict taxonomy to get exactly right.

Doubt keeps coming up about whether I'm doing any of this correctly.

That's doubt itself showing up as content — name it "doubting" just like any other hindrance, and let the question go until after the sit rather than trying to resolve it mid-practice. If it persists afterward, the FAQ sections from earlier sessions, or Begin to Meditate, are good places to check.

Naming the hindrance doesn't make it go away — it's still there.

That's expected, and it isn't the goal. The point of naming isn't to make the hindrance vanish on command — it's to keep you from being swept along by it unnoticed. A named hindrance that's still present is still real progress.

Some days I don't notice any of the five at all.

A genuinely settled sit with no strong hindrance present is a completely valid outcome — log it as such rather than searching for a hindrance that isn't there.

Part 5

Reflection

At the end of the week, look back over your log and sit with these — no need to write pages, a line or two each is enough.

  1. Which hindrance appeared most often this week? Does that match a pattern you already suspected about yourself, on or off the cushion?
  2. Did any hindrance's specific response — softening, brightening, broadening — actually help, or did a different approach work better for you?
  3. Was there a moment you caught a hindrance early, before it fully took over the sit? What did catching it early feel like, compared to noticing it late?
  4. Do any of these five show up in your daily life outside meditation, in a similar shape?
