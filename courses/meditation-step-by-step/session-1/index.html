Session 1 · The problem no single guide can solve for you

Building the Habit

Why daily beats long, and what to do on the days you don't want to sit
Before class: Read Begin to Meditate if you haven't sat before — this session assumes you already know the posture and the breath.
Part 1

Teaching

The guide isn't the problem

You can read a perfect explanation of posture and breath, sit once, feel something real — and then, quietly, never sit again. This is extremely common, and it isn't a failure of understanding. Nobody explained what to do about the second day. Or the tenth. Or the one where you're tired, behind on everything, and meditation is the first thing that feels optional.

That's what this session is actually about. Not technique — habit.

Daily beats long

Five minutes every day changes how your mind settles far more than fifty minutes once a week. A practice your body expects starts to arrive faster on its own — the mind settles sooner, the resistance is smaller, simply because it isn't a novelty being negotiated each time. A once-a-week hour, however sincere, never gets the chance to become familiar. If you only remember one sentence from today: consistency is doing almost all of the real work here — duration is a distant second.

The two-minute rule

Here is the actual tool for hard days. When you don't want to sit for ten minutes, don't skip it — sit for two. Two minutes, no exceptions, no negotiating. The point isn't the two minutes themselves; it's that the habit survives the day, unbroken. A short sit that happens beats a long sit that gets postponed into never happening. You can always sit longer once you're there — but the deal you make with yourself is only ever for two.

The question is never "will I feel like it." The question is only "did I sit today" — and two minutes still counts as yes.
Three ideas to carry out of today
  • The habit is the actual practice this week. Not depth, not stillness — just showing up, every day, on purpose.
  • Resistance is not a stop sign. The day you don't want to sit is very often exactly the day the practice has the most to offer you.
  • Two minutes is a real sit. It's not a consolation prize — it's the tool that keeps the habit alive through the days a longer sit won't happen.
Part 2

Guided InstructionsThe two-minute minimum sit

Use your regular ten-minute sit from Begin to Meditate on good days. On hard days, use this instead — read it once, then set a two-minute timer and go.

The Two-Minute Sit

Sit down. Take your posture — you don't have to get it perfect, just upright enough to stay awake.

Take one slow breath, a little longer on the way out than the way in.

For the rest of the two minutes, simply feel the breath moving. When your mind wanders — and it will, almost immediately — just notice that, and come back. That's the whole practice, compressed.

When the timer sounds, open your eyes. You're done. That counts.

Part 4

Common Obstacles

I missed a day. Is the habit ruined?

No single day ruins anything. What actually breaks a habit is missing twice in a row and treating that as proof it's not working. Just sit today — even two minutes — and the streak is alive again.

I genuinely don't have ten minutes some days.

That's exactly what the two-minute sit is for. It isn't a lesser version of the practice — it's the version designed specifically for days like that.

The two-minute sit feels too short to matter.

Its job isn't to produce deep stillness — it's to keep the habit unbroken. The depth comes back on the days you have more time. What it protects is continuity, and continuity is what this whole session is about.

How do I know if this week actually worked?

Count your log entries, not your calm. If most days have an entry — even a two-minute one — the week worked, regardless of how any individual sit felt.

Part 5

Reflection

At the end of the week, look back over your log and sit with these — no need to write pages, a line or two each is enough.

  1. Which days did you actually sit, and which did you skip? Is there a pattern — time of day, mood, what came before?
  2. How did the two-minute sits compare to the ten-minute ones? Did they feel like "less," or did they feel like something else?
  3. What almost made you skip a day, and what got you to sit anyway — or what won?
  4. If next week only asked one thing of you, what would actually help the habit hold?
