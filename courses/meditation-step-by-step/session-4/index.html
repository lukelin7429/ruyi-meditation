Session 4 · The second foundation

Feelings and the Space Around Them

Mindfulness of feeling-tone — pleasant, unpleasant, or neutral, underneath every sensation and thought
Before class: Complete Session 3 first — this session works with the same body you spent last week getting to know.
Part 1

Teaching

Not "emotions" — something more basic

When people hear "mindfulness of feeling," they usually think of emotions — anger, joy, grief, the big, story-laden states. That's not quite what this session is about. The traditional term is vedanā, and it names something much smaller and more basic than an emotion: the immediate pleasant, unpleasant, or neutral tone that accompanies every single sensation and thought, arising a fraction of a second before any story gets built on top of it. A sound is pleasant, unpleasant, or neutral before you've decided what it means. So is a thought, a sensation in the knee, a taste, a memory. Emotions are usually what get built afterward, out of a chain of these tiny tones plus the stories we attach to them.

The gap this practice opens up

Ordinarily, this tone and the reaction to it happen so fast they feel like a single event: an unpleasant sensation arrives, and aversion is somehow already there, as if they were the same thing. This week's practice is about slowing that down enough to see the gap — however brief — between the bare tone arriving and the reaction to it. Pleasant tone tends to pull toward wanting more; unpleasant tone tends to push toward getting rid of it; neutral tone tends to simply be overlooked, unnoticed, the majority of most people's actual moment-to-moment experience passing by unremarked. Naming the tone itself — just "pleasant," "unpleasant," or "neutral," nothing more elaborate — is what makes that gap visible.

Building on the body

This connects directly to last week: instead of scanning the body for its physical sensations alone, you'll now also note the tone that comes with each one. A tight shoulder might be simply unpleasant. A slow, easy breath might be pleasant. Long stretches of the body might register as neutral — and noticing "neutral" is just as much a success as noticing anything sharper. Most people are quite good at noticing strong pleasant or unpleasant tone and very poor at noticing neutral tone at all; part of this week's point is simply proving to yourself how much of experience actually falls there, unnoticed, most of the time.

The tone itself is not the problem. What usually causes trouble is everything built on top of it in the instant after — and that instant is exactly what this practice makes visible.
Three ideas to carry out of today
  • Vedanā is smaller than emotion. Just pleasant, unpleasant, or neutral — the raw tone underneath the story, not the story itself.
  • There's a gap. Between the tone arriving and the reaction to it, however brief — and naming the tone is what makes that gap visible.
  • Neutral counts too. Most experience is neutral and simply overlooked; noticing it plainly is a real part of this week's practice, not a lesser result.
Part 2

Guided InstructionsNaming the tone, pleasant / unpleasant / neutral

Ten to fifteen minutes, seated as usual. This week you're adding one more label on top of last week's noting — the felt tone, not just the content.

Naming the Tone

Settle into your posture, and take one slow breath, longer on the way out.

Rest attention on the breath, or let it move through the body as in last week's scan. For each sensation, thought, or sound that arises clearly enough to notice, ask — briefly, without dwelling — is this pleasant, unpleasant, or neutral?

Name it just that plainly: "pleasant," "unpleasant," "neutral." Not why, not what it means — just the bare tone, the way you noted "thinking" or "planning" two weeks ago.

Notice, too, what happens right after you name it — does the mind want to move toward a pleasant tone, or away from an unpleasant one? You don't need to stop that pull. Just notice it's there, then return to resting attention on the breath or body.

Spend the final few minutes simply resting, without deliberately hunting for tones — let them arise or not, and note only what shows up on its own.

Part 4

Common Obstacles

I keep confusing the tone with the whole emotion or story around it.

Very common at first — try to catch the very first, split-second flicker before the story builds, and just name that: "unpleasant," not "annoyed because of X." With practice the tone becomes easier to isolate from the story built on top of it.

Everything feels neutral, and I can't find anything sharper to note.

That's a completely valid week — neutral is the most common tone in ordinary experience, and noticing it plainly, rather than manufacturing something more dramatic, is exactly the point.

Naming an unpleasant tone seems to make the discomfort more intense, not less.

Try naming it even more plainly and briefly — a single quiet word, not a lingering examination. The goal is a light touch, glancing at the tone and returning to the anchor, not staying fixed on the unpleasant sensation.

How is this different from just noting thoughts, like in Session 2?

Session 2's noting named the content — "thinking," "planning." This week names the tone underneath any content at all, thought or sensation alike. They work together: you can note both the content and its tone in the same sit if that feels natural, or focus on just the tone this week.

Part 5

Reflection

At the end of the week, look back over your log and sit with these — no need to write pages, a line or two each is enough.

  1. Which tone did you notice most often across the week — and did that surprise you?
  2. Could you catch the gap between a tone arriving and your reaction to it, even briefly? What did that gap feel like?
  3. Did noticing "neutral" change how ordinary, uneventful moments of your day felt, even outside formal sitting?
  4. Was there a moment this week where naming the tone changed what you did next — a pause that wouldn't have been there otherwise?
