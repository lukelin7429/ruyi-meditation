# The Body, Too, Is a Place of Practice

Recently, I have begun paying more serious attention to restoring my health so that my breathing and energy can feel more settled during meditation. In the past, I tended to see meditation mainly as a practice of the mind. I thought that if I could concentrate and maintain enough determination, I should be able to sit peacefully. Gradually, however, I have realized that when the body is tired, tense, or out of balance, the mind cannot easily relax either.

This has led me to reflect on whether I have been carrying too many responsibilities. Whenever I see something that needs to be done, my instinct is to take care of it myself. Education, public service, website projects, Buddhist study, and plans for a future online Buddhist academy are all deeply meaningful to me. Yet one person has only so much time, energy, and strength. Even meaningful work can disturb our balance when we take on too much. I am therefore learning to ask others for help and to share some of my responsibilities.

I have also become aware of how much I overuse my mind. I enjoy learning and often spend long hours reading, thinking, and working. Once I sit down at the computer, I can easily lose track of time. The problem is not the work or the learning itself, but the lack of rest and transition. I need to change not only how I respond to physical discomfort, but also how I work. Important work should not become a reason to lose balance in the rest of my life.

To better understand my condition, I began reading about traditional Chinese medicine and consulting related information online. Its way of looking at health drew my attention to how prolonged thinking, mental tension, sleep, diet, and physical well-being may be interconnected rather than separate issues.

Traditional Chinese medicine and Buddhism are not the same system, but both have helped me appreciate the importance of balance and a holistic view. Buddhism teaches that every result arises from many causes and conditions. Traditional Chinese medicine also encourages us not to view one part of the body or one symptom in isolation. The condition of the body affects the mind, and tension in the mind can also be reflected in the body. Meditation, then, is not only a practice of the mind. It is also connected to how we work, rest, and live each day.

This experience has led me to reconsider the meaning of diligence in practice. I used to think that if something was worthwhile, I should do as much as possible, and that as long as I could keep going, I should continue carrying the responsibility. But exhausting ourselves is not the same as being diligent. True diligence should also include restraint, rest, cooperation, and awareness of our physical and mental condition.

We often speak of treating others with compassion, yet we can be surprisingly harsh toward our own bodies. When we are tired, we demand that the body continue working. When it becomes uncomfortable, we blame it for slowing us down. When meditation does not go smoothly, we assume that we are simply not trying hard enough. But the body accompanies us as we learn, work, practice, and serve others. It, too, deserves understanding and care.

I am now learning to entrust some responsibilities to others and to avoid working or thinking intensely for hours without a break. These changes may not look like profound spiritual practice, but perhaps they are exactly the practice I need right now. The body is not an obstacle to cultivation. Learning to listen to it, care for it, and find balance between diligence and rest is itself part of the path.
