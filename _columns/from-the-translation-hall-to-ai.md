# From the Translation Hall to AI: The Wisdom Buddhist Translation Still Cannot Do Without

Recently, I began experimenting with AI agents to help me read Mahayana Buddhist scriptures and translate some sutras and commentaries into English. To be honest, the results surprised me.

For a long time, working with a Buddhist text meant spending many hours searching for references, comparing different translations, clarifying technical terms, and consulting commentaries by Dharma teachers. It required not only language ability, but also a solid foundation in Buddhist thought. Now, AI can help read the text, identify structure, analyze terminology, compare possible renderings, and produce English that is far more natural than what we used to expect from “machine translation.”

More importantly, AI is no longer merely a tool for producing a rough first draft. Used well, it can search widely, organize related materials, compare existing translations, examine Buddhist vocabulary, analyze sentence structure, and propose several possible ways to render a passage. In a short time, it can show the translator a range of references that would once have taken days or weeks to gather.

This has made me think more seriously about a possibility: Can Ru-Yi Meditation Center use the tools of our time to translate Chinese Buddhist scriptures into English that is accurate, natural, and accessible to readers around the world? This is not only a technical possibility. It may become an important part of our bilingual Dharma work.

Yet Buddhist translation cannot be judged only by the power of the tool.

A sutra is not an ordinary piece of writing. It carries the meaning of the Buddha’s teaching and serves as a basis for listening, reflection, and practice. Translating a Buddhist scripture is not simply a matter of turning Chinese sentences into English sentences. The real challenge is to carry the doctrinal meaning, the spiritual context, the subtle differences among Buddhist terms, and the living tradition of interpretation into another language and culture.

This is especially important when we speak of Chinese Buddhist scriptures. Many Sanskrit originals of Buddhist texts have been lost. In such cases, the Chinese translation is not merely a “secondary source.” It is one of the main reasons the teaching has survived at all. Many Mahayana scriptures can still be read, studied, and practiced today because ancient translators rendered them into Chinese and preserved them within the East Asian Buddhist tradition.

For this reason, translating Chinese Buddhist scriptures into English is not just a matter of convenience for modern readers. It is a meaningful act of transmission. When the Sanskrit original is no longer available, the Chinese canon becomes an essential bridge to the teaching. If we can extend that bridge into English, people from many different countries and cultures may have a chance to encounter the compassion and wisdom of Mahayana Buddhism.

That is why this work must be approached with care.

When we look back at the great translation projects of ancient China, we see that Buddhist translation was never treated as a solitary task. During the Tang dynasty in particular, translation was often a major collective undertaking supported by the state and carried out by eminent monks, scholars, and literary editors. A translation hall included different roles: a chief translator, doctrinal examiners, scribes, literary polishers, proofreaders, and reviewers. Some focused on understanding the source text, some on doctrinal accuracy, some on literary clarity, and some on repeated checking.

That system reminds us that Buddhist translation requires more than language ability. It requires collective review, doctrinal judgment, and literary refinement. The ancient translation hall did not ask only, “Does this read smoothly?” It asked deeper questions: Is the meaning correct? Is it faithful to the scripture? Could this wording mislead future students of the Dharma?

Today, with AI, our tools have become astonishingly powerful. Materials that once required enormous human effort to locate can now be gathered quickly. Terms that once took a long time to compare can now be organized and analyzed almost immediately. English renderings that once required many rounds of drafting can now be generated in several versions for comparison and revision.

This is not a minor improvement. It is a major change in the translation process.

At the same time, even the most powerful AI cannot replace human aspiration, training, and judgment. The final question in Buddhist translation is not whether the English sounds elegant, but whether the Dharma has been faithfully conveyed. AI can offer many possible translations, but which one best carries the meaning of the text? Which English term is least likely to mislead readers? Which sentence sounds smooth but quietly weakens the depth of the teaching? These questions still require people who understand Buddhism, Chinese, and English, and who are willing to keep learning over time.

Therefore, I do not think we should treat AI as a threat. Nor should we treat it as an all-powerful replacement. A better direction is to build a modern version of the translation hall: using AI wisely while training people who can truly work with both the Dharma and the English language.

This is also one of my hopes for the future bilingual work of Ru-Yi Meditation Center. If Ru-Yi can use Chinese Buddhist scriptures as the foundation, combine AI tools with Dharma education, English training, and careful review, and gradually cultivate a younger generation who understands Buddhism and can express it in English, then Buddhist translation will no longer be only the work of a few specialists. It can become a long-term project of education and Dharma transmission.

Young people can learn scripture through this process. They can learn English, learn how to research, learn how to compare different translations, and learn how to approach the Dharma with humility and care. AI can help them enter the world of sources more quickly. But their real growth will come through repeated reading, discussion, review, and revision. That is how they gradually develop sensitivity to the meaning of the Dharma.

This kind of training matters even more than completing a single translation. A translation is one result; trained people are the deeper foundation. If Buddhist translation can become a way for the next generation to study the Dharma, strengthen their English, and learn to take responsibility for sharing the teaching, then AI will not merely be a tool for efficiency. It will become a condition for both education and Dharma work.

Some may ask: If we want English translations, why not translate directly from Sanskrit, Pali, or Tibetan? I do not see these paths as being in conflict. Study of the original languages is extremely important and deserves deep respect. At the same time, the Chinese Buddhist canon carries more than a thousand years of East Asian Buddhist study and practice. Many texts have survived precisely because of their Chinese translations. To translate from Chinese is not to lower the standard. It is to recognize the value of this living transmission and extend it into a wider world.

From the ancient translation hall to today’s AI, the tools and the times have changed. But the wisdom Buddhist translation cannot do without has not changed. The ancient translation halls depended on the vows of great practitioners, collective review, and deep cultivation. Today, we can build on that foundation with the power of AI, thoughtful education, and training in a global language.

I believe Ru-Yi Meditation Center’s bilingual website and Buddhist translation work are not simply about adding more English content. The deeper purpose is to allow the compassion and wisdom preserved in Chinese Buddhist scriptures to be seen, understood, and practiced by more people around the world.

AI can help us move faster. Education can help us move more steadily. The wisdom of the Dharma reminds us why we are walking this path in the first place.

May this effort become one small condition for bringing the Dharma to the world.
