*Reflections on Impermanence, the Five Aggregates, and the Quiet Courage of Detached Caring*

There is a paradox that sits at the heart of Buddhist practice: a deepening path can loosen even our tightest attachments to the people, roles, and endeavors we love most, yet this loosening is not unloving. In his introduction to Chapter I of *In the Buddha's Words*, Bhikkhu Bodhi explains how we construct a fragile sense of selfhood upon the five aggregates—form, feeling, perception, volitional formations, and consciousness—through two constant mental habits: identification ("this is me") and appropriation ("this is mine"). In *Anxiety Due to Change* (SN 22:7), the Buddha illustrates why this construction inevitably breeds suffering. When an uninstructed person regards an aggregate as self, any natural alteration in that aggregate shakes the foundation of their identity, leaving the mind obsessed, distressed, and anxious.

For over a year, one of the defining roles I claimed as "me" and "mine" was serving as the anchor and host of a Vietnamese-language news show. Working closely alongside producers and creative teams, I traveled across the globe documenting stories and helping build the network into a transglobal platform. Broadcasting in both Vietnamese and English, I poured my energy, voice, and heart into connecting with communities around the world. Without realizing it, I wove that professional mission directly into my five aggregates. The physical travel and on-camera presence became tied to form; the excitement of live broadcasting and global connection fed feeling; my self-image as a bilingual bridge-builder solidified in perception; and my relentless drive to shape the network's vision operated through volitional formations (*saṅkhāra*). Through identification, I was no longer simply hosting a program—I was the anchor of this global bridge. Through appropriation, the show, the relationships, and the shared momentum felt unmistakably mine.

When the producer changed the direction of the news network, my mind immediately registered the change as a personal threat. Because I had fused my sense of self with that role, the shift did not feel like an ordinary evolution in media production; it felt like an erasure of who I was. Anxiety surfaced first as a subtle tightening in my chest, followed by relentless mental rumination. My mind replayed conversations, scrutinized decisions, and resisted the reality that something I had poured so much life into was transforming beyond my control. In the language of SN 22:7, because I regarded those aggregates—my creative formations, my public role, my social perceptions—as self, their alteration left my mind agitated and unsettled. It required a profound internal shift to step back, breathe, and recognize that the suffering I felt was not caused merely by the producer's new direction for the network, but by my own clinging to permanence in a conditioned, ever-changing world.

Walking through that transition clarified the vital difference between attachment and genuine love. Attachment is transactional and self-referential; it grasps at a person, project, or outcome and demands that it remain fixed so that my ego feels secure. Love, when paired with wisdom, is spacious and non-possessive. In daily life, detached caring looks like showing up with complete sincerity, generosity, and excellence while releasing ownership over how things unfold. When I practiced detached caring during that transition—and as I apply it now in my teaching and creative work—I could still honor the producers, appreciate the global viewers we reached, and take pride in the integrity of the work we accomplished, without needing the show to remain mine forever.

To an uninstructed person, however, this kind of equanimity is easily mistaken for coldness or indifference. In everyday culture, we are conditioned to equate how intensely we worry, fight, or hold on with how deeply we care. When someone does not react to loss or change with visible panic, bitterness, or territorial defensiveness, observers often assume they never truly valued the relationship or the work. Yet the Buddha's teaching reveals the opposite: when we stop draining our energy into anxious self-protection, our care actually becomes cleaner, steadier, and more compassionate, because it is no longer clouded by fear of personal loss.

Even with the clarity that experience gave me, I must be honest about where I still feel the pull of "mine." Today, as I narrow down my book manuscript for final publication, I notice how easily identification and appropriation re-emerge in subtler forms—wanting the words, the structure, and the eventual reception of the book to validate my years of effort. The difference now is that when that familiar tug of "mine" arises, I recognize it sooner. Instead of building a fortress around what I love, I am learning to hold it with open hands—caring wholeheartedly while trusting the natural unfolding of impermanence.
