This November, during a group practice session at Ru-Yi Meditation Center, a fellow practitioner shared an experience she had while taking part in *zhunian*, the practice of chanting the Buddha's name beside someone who has just died. That day, she and a group of dharma brothers and sisters went to the home of the deceased. When they first walked in, she noticed that the face in the memorial photo looked troubled and sorrowful. After two hours of chanting, she stepped out and looked at the photo again, and the face now seemed gentler, no longer weighed down by sorrow. What struck everyone even more was that on the ride home, several others who had chanted with her said they, too, had noticed a change in the face in the photo.

Someone later asked our teacher how such a thing could happen. Our teacher explained it from the perspective of Yogācāra, the Consciousness-Only school. Because beings experience different karmic results, they are born with different bodies, senses, and minds, and so they do not necessarily have the same experience even when facing the same situation. Our teacher gave the example of water: humans see water; fish take water as their home; the gods see it as pure and radiant lapis lazuli; and hungry ghosts may see only pus and blood.

Buddhism often calls this "one water, four views." A verse in Asaṅga's *Compendium of the Great Vehicle* (*Mahāyānasaṃgraha*) says: "Ghosts, animals, humans, and gods, each according to what befits them, see the same thing with different minds; therefore objects are held to be not truly real." Faced with the same thing, gods, humans, fish, and hungry ghosts encounter entirely different worlds, each shaped by their own karma and its results. If water had only one fixed and unchanging form, why would different beings experience it so differently? What Yogācāra wants to point out is precisely this: the world we experience has never been separate from the consciousness that knows it.

We can see similar examples in everyday life. Faced with excrement, people usually find it filthy and foul-smelling and want to get away from it as quickly as possible, while certain flies and insects are drawn to it as food or as a place to live. This has to do with the bodies, senses, and survival needs of different species. From the Yogācāra point of view, it also shows how beings, by way of different karma, receive different bodies as karmic results and therefore come to know the world in different ways. Every form of life experiences the world through the conditions of its own body and mind.

Yogācāra calls the eighth consciousness the *ālaya-vijñāna*, or storehouse consciousness, because it holds the seeds left behind by our past actions of body, speech, and mind. It is also called the "consciousness of karmic maturation" (*vipāka-vijñāna*), because it is itself the result brought about by past karma. When conditions ripen, these seeds give rise to their respective results. When beings are born, they receive not only different bodies as karmic results but also different senses, habits, and ways of knowing the world. So humans have the world that humans see, fish have the world that fish see, and hungry ghosts and gods each live in realms presented by their own karma and consciousness.

Even among human beings, the seeds and habitual tendencies each person carries are not entirely the same, so we still react differently to the same event. One person sees a rainy day and feels gloomy; a farmer sees the rain and may feel glad. One person hears a remark as a kind reminder; another hears the very same words as criticism. Though the outer circumstances may be much the same, the world that appears within each mind can be very different.

Yogācāra goes further and divides the act of knowing into a "seeing aspect" (*darśana-bhāga*) and an "image aspect" (*nimitta-bhāga*). Put simply, the seeing aspect is consciousness's ability to see and know, while the image aspect is the appearance a thing takes on within consciousness. We assume that we are directly seeing a completely objective world, but what we actually experience is the image aspect that consciousness itself has transformed into appearance. The Faxiang school also speaks of the "underlying basis" and the "reflected image": what everyone faces together is the underlying basis, while what arises in each person's mind is that person's own reflected image. The eye consciousness meets the shapes and colors of the photo, and then the sixth, mental consciousness recognizes and judges them according to memory, emotion, and habit, so that we perceive the person in the photo as sorrowful, peaceful, or wearing some other expression.

Returning to the photo of the deceased, we do not need to rush to decide whether the photo itself underwent some physical change. Light, angle, viewing distance, and memory can all affect our judgment. But one thing is certain: after two hours of chanting the Buddha's name, the people looking at the photo were no longer the same people they had been before. When they first entered the home and faced death, their minds may have carried tension, grief, and unease. Through sustained chanting, their minds gradually settled, and blessings and compassion arose. The original purpose of *zhunian* is to help the dying or deceased person keep right mindfulness and recite the Buddha's name single-mindedly; in the process, the minds of those who chant also grow calm. The photo, as the underlying basis, may not have changed at all, but when the mind that sees has changed, the image that appears in it may change as well.

As for why so many people had a similar impression, it is not hard to understand. The dharma brothers and sisters who chanted together were in the same setting, recited the Buddha's name together, and together went through the shift from unease to calm. With similar states of mind, they may well have felt similarly about the photo. Moreover, they only began talking about it with one another on the way home, and each person's description may have colored the others' impressions. So even if many people had the same impression, that does not prove the photo changed physically. What Yogācāra really asks us to observe is how consciousness takes part in every single experience.

The consciousness of ordinary beings is conditioned by karmic seeds, habitual tendencies, and attachment to self, so we easily take the world we see as the one and only truth. Practice means continually cultivating wholesome seeds and reducing greed, anger, and delusion, so that defiled consciousness is gradually transformed. Yogācāra calls the fundamental transformation of what our life rests on "the transformation of the basis" (*āśraya-parāvṛtti*). This transformation is complete only in Buddhahood, and daily practice is a matter of moving toward it, little by little. As attachment lessens and wisdom and compassion grow, the way we see ourselves, others, and the world changes too.

Did the photo of the deceased really change that day? Perhaps we can never prove it. But after that session of chanting, the minds of those dharma brothers and sisters may truly have become more settled and gentle. When consciousness changes, the world that appears in the mind is no longer quite the same. Perhaps this is where Yogācāra touches daily life most closely: we think we are simply looking at the world, yet every act of looking carries our own karma, habits, and mind.
