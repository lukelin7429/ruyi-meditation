# Practicing Generosity with Wisdom

Sometimes, while helping someone, a quiet question rises in the back of my mind: Am I being used?

It is not always an easy question to face. Someone asks for help. At first, it sounds like a sincere request. But little by little, you begin to feel that your time, your skills, and your experience are being treated as tools for someone else's convenience. You are no longer a friend, a collaborator, or even a fellow human being. You are simply useful.

That feeling can be painful. But it is also worth examining carefully.

Not every act of service falls neatly into the categories of "being used" or "not being used." Sometimes, in helping another person, we also grow. We learn a new skill. We gain experience. We sharpen our abilities. We meet good people. We build positive connections. In those cases, helping someone is not a loss. It is mutual benefit. The other person receives support, and we become better through the process.

Looking back, much of my own growth has come through service. In order to help others well, I had to keep learning. I learned how to edit videos, build websites, organize information, promote meaningful projects, and communicate ideas more clearly. What began as helping others often became training for myself. Through giving, I was also being shaped.

From a Buddhist perspective, service is a form of generosity. Giving is not limited to money or material goods. We can give our time. We can give our attention. We can give our skills. We can give guidance. We can give encouragement. We can give someone a sense of safety.

For anyone who wishes to walk the Bodhisattva path, generosity is not optional. It is one of the basic practices. Compassion cannot remain only as a beautiful idea. It has to become action. Serving others is one of the ways we turn compassion into something real.

But generosity also needs wisdom.

Over the years, I have met people who did not truly value what was being given. They were not interested in friendship, gratitude, or mutual growth. They simply wanted access to free labor, free expertise, and free time. When giving becomes one-sided in this way, it can leave a person feeling drained and disrespected.

That does not mean we should only help when we receive something in return. The Bodhisattva path is not a business transaction. If a person is sincere, and if the work genuinely benefits others, then even when there is no personal gain, the service may still be meaningful.

But if a request does not help others, does not help us grow, and only consumes our energy while encouraging someone else's greed or dependency, then it may be wiser to say no.

Saying no is not always a lack of compassion. Sometimes it is the most responsible choice. It protects our own limited time and energy. It may also help the other person stop relying on others in unhealthy ways.

In Buddhism, we speak of the Six Perfections: generosity, morality, patience, diligence, meditation, and wisdom. Generosity without wisdom can easily become blind giving. A kind heart without discernment may not truly help anyone. It may exhaust the giver, and it may prevent the receiver from learning responsibility.

After more than twenty years of serving in different ways, my understanding of generosity has changed. In the past, whenever someone asked for help, my first instinct was to say yes. I wanted to be useful. I wanted to support others. I wanted to contribute.

Now I understand that sustainable generosity requires discernment.

Our time is limited. Our energy is limited. Our abilities are limited. Because of that, we should use them where they can truly do good: with people who are sincere, with projects that are meaningful, and with causes that genuinely benefit living beings.

Compassion does not mean allowing ourselves to be endlessly consumed. True compassion sees clearly. It gives with an open heart, but also with a wise mind. It asks not only, "Can I help?" but also, "Will this help lead to something wholesome?"

I still want to serve. I still want to use whatever ability I have for the Dharma, for education, and for public good. But I also hope to practice a wiser form of giving. I want to offer my time to people who value it, to work that matters, and to relationships where growth can happen on both sides.

Generosity needs compassion, but it also needs wisdom.

When giving is guided by both, it is no longer merely an act of service. It becomes a path of practice, one that can bring light not only to others, but also to our own heart.
