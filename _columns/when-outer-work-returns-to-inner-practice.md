# When Outer Work Returns to Inner Practice

## Generosity Is the Beginning, Not the Destination

I have always been an introvert.

By temperament, I would probably have chosen a quiet life: reading, thinking, and observing my own mind in stillness. I have never been someone who naturally seeks crowds, attention, or public roles. If I had followed only my personality, I might have stayed mostly within the world of books, contemplation, and solitary practice.

But after encountering the Dharma, I gradually came to understand that the bodhisattva path is not a path of seeking purity for oneself alone. It is not a path of avoiding the suffering and confusion of sentient beings. The bodhisattva path is practiced among people, in the places where help is needed, and in the very situations that challenge our habits, attachments, and limitations.

Because of this, although I am introverted by nature, I stepped into volunteer work and educational service. In the early years, I offered free English classes at Minghang Temple in Zhutang, Changhua. I also helped organize cultural activities, art events, and competitions there. Later, I became involved in serving many schools, promoting English and bilingual education, and helping schools build bilingual websites.

All of this was part of my learning to walk the bodhisattva path.

Over the years, I have been deeply grateful for these opportunities. To serve children, schools, local communities, and education has been an important part of my life and practice. On the surface, it may have looked as if I was giving my time, skills, and resources to others. But in truth, sentient beings were giving me the opportunity to practice generosity, to learn responsibility, and to loosen the habit of living only for myself.

Yet after walking this path for many years, I have also become increasingly clear about one thing: generosity is only the first of the six paramitas.

Generosity is essential. It helps us let go of stinginess and teaches us to share our time, abilities, resources, and care. But if we remain only at the level of outward giving, activities, accomplishments, and recognition, we have not yet reached the deeper aim of Buddhist practice.

The bodhisattva path is not merely about being kind-hearted or keeping oneself endlessly busy with one project after another. True practice must continue beyond generosity.

The six paramitas begin with generosity, but they continue through ethical discipline, patience, diligence, meditative concentration, and finally prajna, wisdom. In other words, bodhisattva practice cannot remain only in outward busyness and visible service. While benefiting others, our own mind must also become more still, our afflictions fewer, our compassion deeper, and our wisdom clearer.

So when I say that outward work must return to inner practice, I do not mean that the work I have done in the past no longer matters. I do not mean that volunteer service or educational work should simply be abandoned. And I certainly do not mean that I no longer wish to give or to serve others.

What I mean is that, at this stage of my life, my task must begin to shift.

In the past, I devoted much of my time to outward service: activities, classes, schools, websites, talks, and educational projects. These efforts all had their meaning and their conditions. But now I need to give myself more time for sitting meditation, more time for studying the scriptures, and more time to return to the foundation of the Dharma. I need to cultivate meditative concentration, and I need to cultivate prajna wisdom.

If a person only keeps giving outwardly but does not return inwardly to practice calm abiding, insight, meditation, and wisdom, then even giving can slowly become another form of attachment. One may become attached to results, attached to being needed, attached to having things done in one's own way. These are not the deepest spirit of the bodhisattva path.

True bodhisattva practice should enter the world with compassion, observe the world with prajna, and gather the mind through meditative concentration.

Therefore, in the days ahead, I hope to let my outward work return more deeply to inner practice. This is not a departure from sentient beings, but a wish to benefit them through deeper practice. It is not a refusal to serve, but a hope that service will no longer come merely from busyness, but from a mind that is more purified, more spacious, and more guided by wisdom.

I still cherish all the causes and conditions of the past, and I am grateful to everyone who has walked with me along the way. But I must also be honest with myself: life is limited, and practice cannot always be postponed.

If meditation, scripture study, and hearing and contemplating the Dharma are always placed at the end of the list, if I keep telling myself that I will practice only after everything else is finished, then perhaps this whole life will pass in busyness.

So this essay is both an explanation to others and a reminder to myself.

I am still willing to give. I am still willing to serve when the right conditions arise. But from now on, I must reserve more time for meditation, the scriptures, and the actual practice of the Dharma. Generosity is the beginning, but meditative concentration and prajna are the direction I now need to walk toward more seriously.

Outward work has its causes and conditions. Inner practice has its own lesson.

After more than twenty years, I have slowly come to understand that what matters most is not how many things I have done, but whether the Dharma has transformed my mind while doing them. What matters is not how many people I have served, but whether, in serving sentient beings, I have reduced self-attachment and increased compassion and wisdom.

When outward work returns to inner practice, it is not a withdrawal. It is a return to deeper practice.

May I have less distraction and more meditative concentration. Less attachment and more prajna. Less outward chasing and more inward contemplation.

Perhaps this is the most honest lesson for me at this stage of life.
