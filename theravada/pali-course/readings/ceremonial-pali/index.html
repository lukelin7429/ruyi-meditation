First Readings: Ceremonial Pāli

The reading track opens with the most widely recited Pāli in the world — text every practicing Theravādin Buddhist has heard or spoken, in Sri Lanka, Myanmar, Thailand, Cambodia, and Laos alike. None of it is any single author's property; all of it is public domain, ancient, and common to the whole tradition. Unlike Part I's first pass at these lines, you can now analyze every word.

Reading 1 of the Reading Track Requires: Parts I & II of the Pāli Course Source: traditional Theravādin liturgy, public domain

The Homage, Once More — Now Fully Readable

Every formal recitation opens with the verse Lesson 4 taught you to sound out and Lesson 18 taught you to fully parse: chanted three times before any other text.

Pali Source

Namo tassa bhagavato arahato sammāsambuddhassa. (×3)

Ru-Yi Translation

Homage to Him, the Blessed One, the Worthy One, the Perfectly Self-Awakened One. (recited three times)

Tisaraṇa — The Three Refuges

The formula by which a person formally identifies as a Buddhist — going for refuge to the Three Jewels — recited three times, each time counting itself explicitly.

Pali Source

Buddhaṃ saraṇaṃ gacchāmi.
Dhammaṃ saraṇaṃ gacchāmi.
Saṅghaṃ saraṇaṃ gacchāmi.

Dutiyampi buddhaṃ saraṇaṃ gacchāmi.
Dutiyampi dhammaṃ saraṇaṃ gacchāmi.
Dutiyampi saṅghaṃ saraṇaṃ gacchāmi.

Tatiyampi buddhaṃ saraṇaṃ gacchāmi.
Tatiyampi dhammaṃ saraṇaṃ gacchāmi.
Tatiyampi saṅghaṃ saraṇaṃ gacchāmi.

Ru-Yi Translation

I go to the Buddha for refuge.
I go to the Dhamma for refuge.
I go to the Saṅgha for refuge.

For the second time, I go to the Buddha for refuge.
For the second time, I go to the Dhamma for refuge.
For the second time, I go to the Saṅgha for refuge.

For the third time, I go to the Buddha for refuge.
For the third time, I go to the Dhamma for refuge.
For the third time, I go to the Saṅgha for refuge.

Reading note — Ru-Yi

"Buddhaṃ saraṇaṃ gacchāmi" holds a small grammatical surprise: gacchati ("goes," Lesson 11's regular pattern) here takes two accusatives at once — buddhaṃ and saraṇaṃ are both in the accusative case from Lesson 6, not because there are two objects, but because this specific idiom, "go to X as a refuge," treats the destination and the role it's going to as a single double-accusative unit. This isn't a pattern the earlier lessons taught in the abstract, because it belongs to this one idiom rather than to a general rule — exactly the kind of thing a reading track is for.

Dutiyampi and tatiyampi are themselves small sandhi facts from Lesson 15's family of rules: dutiyaṃ ("second[ly]," an accusative used adverbially) plus api ("also, even") contract their shared boundary — niggahīta before a vowel — into dutiyampi. Tatiyampi is built the same way from tatiyaṃ, "third[ly]."

Pañcasīla — The Five Precepts

The five training precepts undertaken by lay Buddhists, each stated in an identical grammatical frame: an ablative naming what is abstained from, followed by the fixed phrase veramaṇī sikkhāpadaṃ samādiyāmi, "I undertake the training precept of abstinence."

Pali Source

Pāṇātipātā veramaṇī sikkhāpadaṃ samādiyāmi.
Adinnādānā veramaṇī sikkhāpadaṃ samādiyāmi.
Kāmesumicchācārā veramaṇī sikkhāpadaṃ samādiyāmi.
Musāvādā veramaṇī sikkhāpadaṃ samādiyāmi.
Surāmerayamajjapamādaṭṭhānā veramaṇī sikkhāpadaṃ samādiyāmi.

Ru-Yi Translation

I undertake the training precept of abstaining from killing living beings.
I undertake the training precept of abstaining from taking what is not given.
I undertake the training precept of abstaining from sexual misconduct.
I undertake the training precept of abstaining from false speech.
I undertake the training precept of abstaining from the basis of heedlessness that is liquor, wine, and intoxicants.

Reading note — Ru-Yi

Every one of the five opening words is an ablative (Lesson 5: "from, source, cause") — grammatically, "away from which one abstains." And veramaṇī sikkhāpadaṃ, though conventionally printed as two words, functions as one Lesson 16-style tappurisa compound: "the training-precept OF abstinence." That's why only the final member, sikkhāpadaṃ, carries the accusative ending that samādiyāmi ("I undertake") needs for its object — veramaṇī appears in its bare compounding form, exactly as Lesson 16 taught you every non-final compound member does.

The fifth precept's opening word is this reading's real payoff: Surāmerayamajjapamādaṭṭhānā breaks down as a Lesson 17-style dvanda listing three near-synonymous intoxicants — surā (a rice liquor), meraya (a fermented liquor), majja (intoxicants generally) — followed by a Lesson 16-style tappurisa, pamādaṭṭhāna, "basis of heedlessness" (pamāda + ṭhāna). The whole compound then takes the same ablative ending, -ā, as the other four precepts. One word, two different compound types nested together, and the same case ending you've now seen five times in a row — this is what "grammar before vocabulary" was ultimately for.

Key vocabulary — for Pāli learners
pāṇātipāta

killing living beings — pāṇa ("living being") + atipāta ("attack, onslaught"), itself a tappurisa: "an attack ON living beings."

adinnādāna

taking what is not given — a- ("not") + dinna ("given," the past participle of dadāti, "gives" — compare Lesson 14's diṭṭha, kata) + ādāna ("taking").

musāvāda

false speech — musā ("falsely") + vāda ("speech, statement"), from the same root as Lesson 12's vadati/avoca.

samādiyāmi
v.

"I undertake, I take upon myself" — 1st singular present, saṃ- + ādiyati, exactly the regular pattern Lesson 11 taught.
