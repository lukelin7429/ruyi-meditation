Sāriputta's Refusal

Sāriputta, doubled over with stomach pain, is finally brought the one food that would ease it — porridge his friend Moggallāna secured through a chain of events neither of them fully understood. Tracing that chain back to a deity's meddling, Sāriputta refuses it outright, at the cost of forty-five more years of the same illness. This reading's first excerpt is the ordinary dialogue that sets the story up; the second is the refusal itself.

Reading 6 of the Reading Track Requires: Parts I & II of the Pāli Course Source: Vism I, Sīlaniddesa §44 (VRI/CST numbering)

Excerpt 1: Bringing the Porridge

Pali Source

Mahāmoggallānatthero pātova āgantvā "āvuso, yāva ahaṃ piṇḍāya caritvā āgacchāmi, tāva idheva hohī"ti vatvā gāmaṃ pāvisi. Te manussā paccuggantvā therassa pattaṃ gahetvā vuttappakārassa pāyāsassa pūretvā adaṃsu. Thero gamanākāraṃ dassesi. Te "bhuñjatha, bhante, tumhe, aparampi dassāmā"ti theraṃ bhojetvā puna pattapūraṃ adaṃsu. Thero gantvā "handāvuso sāriputta, paribhuñjā"ti upanāmesi.

Ru-Yi Translation

The Elder Mahāmoggallāna, coming early in the morning, said, "Friend, stay right here until I go on almsround and return," and entered the village. Those people, coming out to meet him, took his bowl, filled it with the porridge of the kind mentioned, and gave it to him. The elder made as if to leave. They said, "Please eat too, venerable sir — we will give more besides," fed him, and gave him another bowlful. The elder, going back, offered it, saying, "Here, friend Sāriputta, partake of this."

hohi
Imperative (Lesson 13), 2nd singular of hoti, "be! — stay!" The first time this course has shown you an imperative inside ordinary dialogue rather than a fixed ceremonial formula like ehi bhikkhu.
bhuñjatha
Imperative, 2nd plural, "eat! (please, all of you)" — spelled identically to Lesson 11's present-tense 2nd plural, exactly the overlap Lesson 13 flagged. Only the polite, address-to-a-monk context marks this as a request rather than a plain present-tense statement.
paribhuñja
Imperative, 2nd singular again, "partake!" — the bare-stem imperative pattern from Lesson 13, now confirmed a second time in the same short passage.
caritvā, gahetvā, pūretvā, gantvā
Four more absolutives (Lesson 14) — "having walked... having taken... having filled... having gone" — still doing the same chaining work you first saw in Reading 3.

Excerpt 2: The Refusal

Pali Source

Theropi taṃ disvā "atimanāpo pāyāso, kathaṃ nu kho uppanno"ti cintento tassa uppattimūlaṃ disvā āha — "āvuso moggallāna, aparibhogāraho piṇḍapāto"ti.

"Vacīviññattivipphārā, uppannaṃ madhupāyasaṃ;
sace bhutto bhaveyyāhaṃ, sājīvo garahito mama.

Ru-Yi Translation

That elder, too, seeing it and thinking, "This porridge is exceedingly delightful — how did it come about, I wonder?", perceived the root of its origin and said, "Friend Moggallāna, this almsfood is not fit to be eaten."

"This sweet porridge, arisen from the spreading effect of a verbal intimation — if I had eaten it, my livelihood would deserve blame.

cintento
Present participle (Lesson 14), masculine nominative singular, "thinking" — agreeing with thero, exactly Lesson 14's original -anto pattern.
bhutto bhaveyyāhaṃ
bhutto (past participle of bhuñjati, "eaten") + bhaveyyaṃ (optative, Lesson 13, 1st singular of hoti/bhavati) + ahaṃ (Lesson 9) — a periphrastic "if I had eaten," and the first 1st-person optative this reading track has shown you.
Reading note — Ru-Yi

Bhaveyyāhaṃ is worth slowing down on: bhaveyyaṃ ends in niggahīta, ahaṃ begins with a vowel, and the two don't simply elide the way Reading 5's sace+ahaṃ did — the niggahīta drops and the two adjacent short a-vowels merge into one long ā, giving bhaveyya+ahaṃ → bhaveyyāhaṃ. This is a third sandhi outcome alongside Lesson 15's elision and Reading 5's reverse-direction elision: sometimes, instead of one vowel simply disappearing, two short vowels fuse into a single long one. The underlying instinct is the same throughout — Pāli does not tolerate two vowels sitting side by side across a word boundary — but the specific repair varies with which sounds are actually in contact.

This is also the reading track's first sace + optative combining with a 1st-person subject rather than the 3rd-person examples in Readings 4 and 5 — proof that the pattern generalizes across all persons, not just "he/she/it."
