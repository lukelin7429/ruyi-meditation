Lesson 2 of the Pāli Course · Consonants I

Place and Manner

Pāli's 33 consonants aren't a random list to memorize — they're organized into a tight, five-by-five grid by two questions: where in the mouth the sound is made (its place), and how it's made there (its manner). Learn the grid once and every consonant's behavior — including which nasal can appear next to which stop — becomes predictable instead of arbitrary.

Level: absolute beginner Prerequisite: Lesson 1 Focus: place of articulation

Five Places, One Grid

Say the English words "key" and "tea" slowly and notice where your tongue touches. For "key," the back of your tongue rises to meet the soft back part of the roof of your mouth. For "tea," the tip of your tongue touches further forward. Pāli consonants are classified by exactly this kind of observation — where in the vocal tract the airflow gets blocked — and it recognizes five distinct places, arranged from back to front:

Velar (back of the throat)

k · kh · g · gh · ṅ

Back of the tongue against the soft palate — the same spot as English "k" in "key" or "g" in "go."

Palatal (hard palate)

c · ch · j · jh · ñ

Middle of the tongue against the hard palate — close to English "ch" in "church" and "j" in "judge."

Retroflex (curled back)

ṭ · ṭh · ḍ · ḍh · ṇ

Tip of the tongue curled back to touch the roof of the mouth, behind the ridge. English has no true equivalent — the closest most English speakers can find is the "t"/"d" in American "party," "hardy."

Dental (upper teeth)

t · th · d · dh · n

Tip of the tongue against the back of the upper front teeth — noticeably further forward than English "t"/"d," which actually touch the ridge just behind the teeth.

Labial (lips)

p · ph · b · bh · m

Both lips together — identical to English "p," "b," and "m."

The Full Grid

Each of the five places produces the same five-member family: an unaspirated voiceless stop, an aspirated voiceless stop, an unaspirated voiced stop, an aspirated voiced stop, and a nasal. The aspirated members (marked with an "h") are this lesson's blind spot on purpose — Lesson 3 gives aspiration the full, dedicated treatment it needs. For now, use this table as a map of the whole territory, and focus on locating each plain, unaspirated letter by place.

Outside this grid sit eight more consonants that don't belong to any of the five families: the semivowels y, r, l, ḷ (a retroflex "l," rarer, mostly from older forms surfacing after certain sandhi), and v; the sibilant s; the aspirate h; and the niggahīta ṃ, which gets its own section in Lesson 3.

Voiced vs. Voiceless: The One Manner Contrast You Already Know

Put two fingers on your throat and say "sss" then "zzz." You'll feel the buzz of your vocal cords switch on for "zzz" — that buzz is voicing, and English already uses it constantly to keep words apart: "pin" vs. "bin," "tin" vs. "din," "cot" vs. "god." Pāli uses exactly the same contrast, at every one of the five places, and it is worth noticing consciously rather than taking it for granted, because Lesson 3's aspiration contrast will ask you to combine it with a second, less familiar feature.

kamma
voiceless velar — "action, deed" (as in kammapatha, "course of action")
gamana
voiced velar — "going, walking"
taṇhā
voiceless dental — "craving," the second noble truth's cause of suffering
dāna
voiced dental — "giving," the first of the ten pāramī

Retroflex vs. Dental: The Contrast English Doesn't Make

English speakers instinctively map both Pāli t/d (dental) and ṭ/ḍ (retroflex) onto their own single "t"/"d," because English draws no distinction here at all. In Pāli, the two are as different as "p" and "k" are in English — a genuinely separate sound, not a stylistic variant.

Two extremely common words, both safe to memorize as fixed reference points before you can reliably feel the tongue position yourself:

citta
dental t — "mind, consciousness"
aṭṭha
retroflex ṭṭh — "eight," as in ariya aṭṭhaṅgika magga, the Noble Eightfold Path
nibbāna
dental n — the third noble truth, the cessation of suffering
vaṇṇa
retroflex ṇṇ — "color, appearance, praise"
Reading note — Ru-Yi

Once you can hold the retroflex/dental distinction in your ear, a word like daṇḍa ("stick, rod, punishment" — the word that opens the Dhammapada's chapter on violence) becomes a small pronunciation exercise all by itself: dental d to open the word, retroflex ṇḍ to close it. Reading Pāli accurately is largely built from noticing dozens of small contrasts exactly like this one, held consistently, rather than from any single hard rule.

Practice: Sorting by Place

For each word, name the place of articulation of the bold consonant, using the five categories from this lesson (velar, palatal, retroflex, dental, labial).

  1. saṅgha — the bold consonant is which place?
  2. cetanā — volition; which place?
  3. vaṭṭa — the round of rebirth; which place?
  4. paññā — wisdom; which place?
  5. bhagavato — of the Blessed One; which place is this semivowel closest to?
Check your answers

saṅgha — velar (g is produced at the same place as k, ṅ, gh, kh).

cetanā — palatal (c is produced at the same place as j, ch, jh, ñ).

vaṭṭa — retroflex (ṭ is produced at the same place as ḍ, ṇ, ṭh, ḍh).

paññā — palatal (ñ is the palatal nasal, same place as c/ch/j/jh).

bhagavato — labial (v is formed with the lips/teeth close to where p/b/m are made; traditional grammar groups it with the labial series).
