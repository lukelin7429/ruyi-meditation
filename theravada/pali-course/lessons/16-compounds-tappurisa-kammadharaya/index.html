Lesson 16 of the Pāli Course · Compounds I

Tappurisa & Kammadhāraya

Pāli fuses words together far more freely than English does, and long compounds are one of the biggest visual obstacles for a new reader — a single "word" can be a whole clause's worth of meaning glued into one unbroken string. This lesson gives you the two most common compound types and a method for pulling them apart.

Level: beginner Prerequisite: Lesson 15 Part II · Lesson 12

Tappurisa: The "Of, By, In, For..." Compound

In a tappurisa compound, the first member stands in an implied oblique-case relationship (genitive, instrumental, locative, and so on — Lesson 5's cases) to the second, the way English "battlefield" means a field of battle, not a field that is battle. Pāli compounds drop the case ending that relationship would otherwise require; you have to supply it yourself when unpacking the compound.

kamma + ṭhāna = kammaṭṭhāna
"action" + "place/basis" → "basis OF action," i.e. a meditation subject — the very first specialized vocabulary word this course taught you, back in Lesson 1, without yet being able to explain its structure
rāja + putta = rājaputta
"king" + "son" → "son OF a king," a prince — the genitive relationship is implied, not spelled with an ending
Reading note — Ru-Yi

The title of the Buddha's first sermon is itself a nested tappurisa: Dhammacakkappavattana breaks down as dhamma + cakka + pavattana — "teaching" + "wheel" + "setting in motion," i.e. "the setting in motion of the wheel of the teaching." Dhammacakka ("wheel of the Dhamma") is itself a smaller tappurisa nested inside the larger one — exactly the kind of layered structure you'll learn to peel apart one level at a time, outermost member first.

Kammadhāraya: The Descriptive Compound

In a kammadhāraya compound, the first member is an adjective (or a noun standing in apposition) directly describing the second — no implied case relationship, just "an X that is Y," the way English "bluebird" means a bird that is blue, not a bird of blue.

kalyāṇa + mitta = kalyāṇamitta
"good, beautiful" + "friend" → "good friend" — the standard Buddhist term for a spiritual friend or mentor
mahā + purisa = mahāpurisa
"great" + "man/person" → "great man" — a standard epithet, e.g. for the Buddha

A Method for Breaking Compounds Apart

  1. Scan for recognizable whole words inside the string — vocabulary you already know is your best guide to where one member ends and the next begins.
  2. Check the boundary for sandhi (Lesson 15): a doubled consonant or an unexpected vowel change often marks exactly where two members were joined.
  3. Ask what relationship makes sense between the pieces: description (kammadhāraya) or an implied case (tappurisa)? Meaning, not the spelling, tells you which.
  4. Translate outermost-in for nested compounds, the way Dhammacakkappavattana above resolved dhammacakka as one unit before attaching pavattana.

Practice: Breaking Down Compounds

  1. lokanātha — loka ("world") + nātha ("protector, refuge")
  2. mahāsatta — mahā ("great") + satta ("being")
  3. dhammacakka — dhamma ("teaching") + cakka ("wheel")
Check your answers

1. Tappurisa — "protector OF the world" (genitive relationship).

2. Kammadhāraya — "great being," a being that IS great (description, no implied case).

3. Tappurisa — "wheel OF the teaching" (genitive relationship).
