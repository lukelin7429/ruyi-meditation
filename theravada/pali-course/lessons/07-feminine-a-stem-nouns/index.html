Lesson 7 of the Pāli Course · Nouns

Feminine ā-stem Nouns

Where Lesson 6 covered nouns ending in short -a, this lesson covers Pāli's main feminine class: nouns ending in long -ā. We'll use paññā, "wisdom," throughout — a word you've already met several times in this course.

Level: beginner Prerequisite: Lesson 6 Part II · Lesson 3

Five Cases, One Ending

The highlighted rows above are not a display error: in the singular, instrumental, dative, ablative, genitive, and locative all collapse onto the single form paññāya. Where the masculine a-stem paradigm in Lesson 6 merged pairs of cases, the feminine ā-stem paradigm merges five of them at once. In practice this is far less confusing than it sounds, for the same reason a small English vocabulary of context words works fine in speech: the surrounding sentence — a preposition-like postposition, another noun's case, or simply what makes sense — carries the rest of the disambiguating work that the ending itself no longer can.

This is also genuinely common to encounter, not a rare edge case: paññāya is the exact form behind the doctrinally central phrase paññāya vimutto, "liberated through wisdom" (as opposed to liberation reached primarily through concentration) — an instrumental use of precisely this ending.

Reading note — Ru-Yi

Notice the vocative singular, paññe, breaks the pattern in its own way: it isn't the bare stem the way masculine buddha was in Lesson 6, but a short -e replacing the long -ā entirely. Vocative forms across Pāli's noun classes are consistently the least predictable case from the rest of the paradigm — worth simply memorizing case by case as you meet them, rather than trying to derive them from a single master rule.

Practice: Producing the Form

  1. "___ uppajjati" — wisdom arises. (subject)
  2. "___ vimutto" — liberated through wisdom. (means)
  3. "he ___" — O wisdom (direct address — an unusual but grammatically valid sentence to test the form)
  4. "idaṃ ___ phalaṃ" — this is the fruit of wisdom. (possession)
Check your answers

1. Paññā — nominative singular.

2. Paññāya — instrumental singular.

3. Paññe — vocative singular, the one irregular form in this paradigm.

4. Paññāya — genitive singular, identical in form to the instrumental you gave in question 2. This is the five-way convergence from earlier in this lesson, now showing up in your own production, not just in a table.

Continuing From Here

With Lessons 6 and 7, you can now decline the two largest noun classes in the language. Lesson 8 turns to two smaller but still frequent classes — masculine nouns ending in -i and -u — using muni ("sage") and bhikkhu ("monk") as models, and is a lighter lesson by design: these classes show more variation between texts than the two you've just learned, so the goal is recognizing their most common forms on sight rather than production drilling.
