Lesson 3 of the Pāli Course · Consonants II

Aspiration, Nasals & Niggahīta

This lesson finishes the consonant grid from Lesson 2 with its hardest column for English speakers — aspiration — then adds the five nasals and one sound that belongs to no place at all: the niggahīta ṃ. By the end of this lesson you will have met every consonant Pāli has.

Level: absolute beginner Prerequisite: Lesson 2 Focus: aspiration, nasals, ṃ

You Already Make This Sound — You Just Don't Notice It

Hold the back of your hand an inch from your mouth and say the English word "pin." You'll feel a small puff of air hit your hand right as the "p" releases. Now say "spin" the same way. The puff is gone — the "p" in "spin" is a completely different sound from the "p" in "pin," articulated identically but released without that burst of breath. English has both sounds, in fact uses them in a fully predictable pattern (aspirated at the start of a stressed syllable, unaspirated after "s"), and simply never lets the difference change a word's meaning, so English speakers grow up without ever consciously hearing it.

Pāli has exactly the same two sounds — call them "aspirated" (with the puff) and "unaspirated" (without it) — but treats the difference the way English treats voicing: as a genuine, word-changing contrast, at all five places from Lesson 2. Spelled, the aspirated member of each pair simply adds an "h" after the base letter: k/kh, g/gh, c/ch, j/jh, ṭ/ṭh, ḍ/ḍh, t/th, d/dh, p/ph, b/bh.

Try this

Hold your hand in front of your mouth again and say these two words slowly: kamma, then khanti. You should feel no puff on the first "k," and a clear puff on the "kh." This is the entire skill this lesson is asking you to build — not a new mouth position, just noticing and controlling a puff of air you already produce every day in English without meaning to.

One Pair Per Place

Five clean examples, one per place, all drawn from vocabulary you'll see again in this course's readings from our own Visuddhimagga translation:

kamma
velar, unaspirated — "action, deed"
khanti
velar, aspirated — "patience," one of the five kinds of restraint in Sīlaniddesa
cetanā
palatal, unaspirated — "volition," the first term in Sīlaniddesa's fourfold definition of virtue
chanda
palatal, aspirated — "desire, wish," one of the four bases of success (iddhipāda)
kammaṭṭhāna
retroflex, aspirated — "meditation subject" (literally "place/basis of action")
taṇhā vs. thera
dental family, unaspirated vs. aspirated — "craving" vs. "elder monk"
paññā vs. phala
labial, unaspirated vs. aspirated — "wisdom" vs. "fruit, result"

Notice that khanti and cetanā are not incidental examples — both are technical terms straight out of the passages we've already translated on the Sīlaniddesa page. This is exactly the point of building this course alongside a live translation project: the vocabulary you drill here is vocabulary you will meet again, doing real work, not invented for the exercise and then discarded.

The Five Nasals

Each of the five places in Lesson 2's grid has its own nasal — a sound made by blocking the mouth at that place while air escapes through the nose. English only distinguishes three nasals in spelling (m, n, and the "ng" sound written with two letters), so two of Pāli's five nasals will be genuinely new symbols even though the sounds themselves are familiar.

NasalPlaceNotes
ṅvelaridentical to English "ng" in "sing" — in Pāli spelling it appears only immediately before a velar stop, as in saṅgha, saṅkhāra
ñpalatalclose to the "ny" in Spanish "señor" — appears doubled or before palatal stops, as in paññā, ñāṇa
ṇretroflexthe retroflex counterpart to n — vaṇṇa, kaṇha
ndentalordinary English "n" — nibbāna, dāna
mlabialordinary English "m" — dhamma, kamma

Because ṅ and, mostly, ñ only ever show up glued to a stop from their own place, you rarely need to hunt for them independently — seeing ṅg or ṅk together is itself the signal that you're looking at the velar nasal, not a separate decision to make.

Niggahīta: The Sound With No Place

Every consonant so far has belonged to one of the five places. The niggahīta, written ṃ, belongs to none of them — it is a pure nasalization, historically closer to the "ng" at the end of French "bon" than to any single consonant closure. It occurs only at the end of a syllable, most often at the end of a word (evaṃ, "thus"; dhammaṃ, "the teaching," as a direct object; ahaṃ, "I").

When another word or another element follows directly, spoken Pāli typically assimilates the niggahīta to the place of whatever consonant comes next — and this shows up directly in spelling, not just in pronunciation. The prefix saṃ- ("together, thoroughly, well") is a niggahīta itself, and when it attaches to a word beginning with a labial consonant, it is written as a plain m instead:

saṃ + buddha → sambuddha
"together/completely" + "awakened" → "the Perfectly Awakened One" — the niggahīta becomes m before the labial b
saṃ + pajāna → sampajāna
"together/completely" + "knowing" → "clearly comprehending" — same shift, before the labial p

You will see this exact word, sambuddhassa, in the very next lesson.

Practice: Aspirated or Not?

Say each word aloud with your hand in front of your mouth (or silently trace the puff-of-air test in your mind), then decide: does the bold consonant carry aspiration?

  1. bhagavato — of the Blessed One
  2. citta — mind
  3. saṅgha — the community (focus on the second consonant, gh)
  4. vīriya — energy, effort
  5. dhamma — the teaching (focus on the m)
Check your answers

bhagavato — aspirated (bh is the voiced aspirated labial stop).

citta — not aspirated (tt is the plain dental stop, geminated).

saṅgha — aspirated (gh is the voiced aspirated velar stop; the ṅ before it is simply the velar nasal, not itself aspirated).

vīriya — not applicable — r is a semivowel, outside the aspirated/unaspirated system entirely. (If this tripped you up, that's useful: aspiration only applies to the 25 stops in Lesson 2's grid, never to y, r, l, v, s, or h.)

dhamma — not aspirated (the m itself is plain; note that the word's own first consonant, dh, is aspirated — worth noticing on a second pass).

Continuing From Here

You have now met every sound in the Pāli inventory: eight vowels with phonemic length, twenty-five grid consonants organized by place and by voicing/aspiration, five nasals, four semivowels plus the retroflex ḷ, the sibilant s, the aspirate h, and the placeless niggahīta ṃ. Lesson 4 puts the whole system to work at once, reading a complete line of Pāli aloud — the verse of homage that opens every Theravādin recitation.
