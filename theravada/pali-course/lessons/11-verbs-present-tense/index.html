Lesson 11 of the Pāli Course · Verbs

Verbs I: The Present Tense

Nouns and pronouns tell you who and what. Verbs tell you what's happening — and in Pāli, a verb's ending alone tells you who's doing it, which means a complete sentence can be a single word. This lesson gives you the present tense, using passati, "sees," the verb Lesson 10 promised you.

Level: beginner Prerequisite: Lesson 10 Part II · Lesson 7

Person and Number Live on the Verb

Just as Lesson 5 showed that a noun's ending, not its position, carries its grammatical role, a Pāli verb's ending carries the subject's person (I / you / he-she-it) and number (singular / plural) built in. One direct consequence: since passāmi already means "I see" all by itself, the pronoun ahaṃ from Lesson 9 is grammatically optional — Pāli sentences drop the subject pronoun constantly, adding it only for emphasis or clarity, the same way Spanish says "veo" rather than "yo veo." Reading Pāli therefore means learning to find the subject inside the verb ending itself, not just from a separate word nearby.

Dictionaries always cite a Pāli verb in its third person singular form — "he/she/it [verb]s" — because that is the form you meet stripped bare of any of the assumptions the other five forms make. Passati, "he/she/it sees," is therefore the form you'll always look up, even though "I see" or "they see" may be what a given sentence actually needs.

The Present Tense of passati, "to see"

Strip the citation form's -ti to get the present stem, passa-, then add one of six personal endings. This exact pattern — stem ending in -a, then -ati/-asi/-āmi singular and -anti/-atha/-āma plural — covers the largest class of ordinary Pāli verbs, including vadati ("speaks," which you already used correctly in Lesson 9's ahaṃ vadāmi) and hundreds of others.

PersonSingularPlural
1st ("I" / "we")passāmiI seepassāmawe see
2nd ("you")passasiyou seepassathayou (all) see
3rd ("he/she/it/they")passatihe/she/it seespassantithey see

Pāli grammars traditionally list these in the opposite order — third person first, then second, then first — because that is the order the language's own indigenous grammatical tradition (going back to the same Sanskrit framework behind Pāṇini's grammar) always uses. You'll see paradigms printed that way elsewhere; the content is identical regardless of which order they're listed in.

hoti, "Is / Becomes": The Verb You'll See on Every Page

Pāli's ordinary verb "to be" follows the same six-ending pattern as passati, just with stem-vowel -o- in place of -a- throughout — a small, learnable variation rather than a new system.

PersonSingularPlural
1sthomiI am/becomehomawe are/become
2ndhosiyou are/becomehothayou (all) are/become
3rdhotihe/she/it is/becomeshontithey are/become
Reading note — Ru-Yi

You've already silently read past several forms of this verb without them being named: hoti itself opened Sīlaniddesa's account of restraint ("iminā pātimokkhasaṃvarena upeto hoti," "he is possessed of this restraint by the Pātimokkha," Lesson 10's neighboring text), and it is simply the single most frequent verb in the entire Canon. Worth memorizing this table before any other verb besides passati.

A Note on deseti, "Teaches"

Lesson 6 used buddho dhammaṃ deseti, "the Buddha teaches the Dhamma," without commenting on its verb. You may now notice deseti doesn't match the passati pattern above — and it shouldn't: it belongs to a different, causative-flavored verb class (built by adding -e- to a root, roughly "to make known" from "to point out"), which this course covers on its own later. For now, simply recognize that not every verb ending in -eti is irregular in some unpredictable way — it's a whole separate, equally regular class, just not this lesson's class.

Simple Negation: na

Place the indeclinable na, "not," directly before the verb to negate a sentence — no change to the verb's own ending is needed.

So dhammaṃ na passati.
"He does not see the Dhamma."

Your First Original Sentences

Every word below has already appeared somewhere in this course. Read each sentence using only what you now know.

Buddho dhammaṃ deseti.
"The Buddha teaches the Dhamma." (Lesson 6's example, now fully readable — subject in the nominative, object in the accusative, verb agreeing with the subject)
Bhikkhū dhammaṃ passanti.
"The monks see the Dhamma." (i-stem/u-stem nominative plural from Lesson 8, agreeing with a third-person plural verb)
Ahaṃ taṃ na passāmi.
"I do not see him/it." (personal pronoun subject stated for emphasis, demonstrative object, negated verb)

Practice: Building and Reading Sentences

  1. Translate into Pāli: "The monk speaks." (use vadati)
  2. Translate into Pāli: "We do not see the path." (use maggaṃ, the accusative of magga from Lesson 6)
  3. Translate into English: Te bhikkhū dhammaṃ vadanti.
  4. Translate into English: Ahaṃ Buddho na homi.
Check your answers

1. Bhikkhu vadati.

2. Mayaṃ maggaṃ na passāma.

3. "Those monks speak the Dhamma" / "...teach the Dhamma."

4. "I am not the Buddha." — a genuinely humble and grammatically ordinary sentence any Pāli speaker could say.
