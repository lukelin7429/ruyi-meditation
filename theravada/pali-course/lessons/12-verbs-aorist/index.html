Verbs II: The Aorist

Every sutta's narrative frame — "the Blessed One said," "he saw," "she understood" — is carried by this tense. Unlike the present tense in Lesson 11, the aorist has no single productive paradigm you can apply to any verb; it is closer to English's irregular past tenses ("go/went," "see/saw") than to the regular "-ed." This lesson teaches it the way you'd actually need it as a reader: a short, extremely high-frequency vocabulary to recognize on sight.

Level: beginner Prerequisite: Lesson 11 Part II · Lesson 8

Why This Lesson Looks Different

Lessons 6 through 11 each gave you one paradigm and told you it covered a huge share of ordinary words. The aorist doesn't work that way: Pāli built it historically from several different formation patterns at once (grammarians distinguish a "root aorist," an "s-aorist," and others), and a great many of the verbs you'll actually read — including some of the most common ones — form their aorist from a different root entirely than their present tense does, the way English "go" suppletes to "went" instead of "goed."

Rather than presenting a six-person paradigm that would misrepresent how regular this system actually is, this lesson gives you the aorist forms that do almost all of the narrative work in real texts: the third person, singular and plural — because narration is told about someone, not by them. First- and second-person aorist forms exist and are learnable, but you'll pick them up naturally, verb by verb, as you meet them in direct speech.

Notice that vadati has two common aorists, āha and avoca, both simply meaning "said" — real languages tolerate this kind of redundancy, and Pāli narrative prose uses both constantly, often in the very same passage.

Reading note — Ru-Yi

One of the most frequent sentence openings in the entire Sutta Piṭaka is built from exactly this vocabulary: "Atha kho bhagavā etadavoca" — "Then the Blessed One said this." Atha kho is a fixed narrative-transition phrase ("then, now"), bhagavā is the nominative you learned in Lesson 6's discussion of bhagavato, etaṃ is the neuter accusative of the demonstrative from Lesson 9 ("this"), and avoca is this lesson's aorist. You can now recognize, on sight, the single most common way any discourse in the Canon announces that the Buddha is about to speak.

Reading Practice: Aorist in Context

Atha kho bhagavā etadavoca.
"Then the Blessed One said this."
So dhammaṃ addasa.
"He saw the Dhamma." (compare Lesson 11's present-tense passati — same verb, different tense)
Bhikkhū taṃ akaṃsu.
"The monks did that."
