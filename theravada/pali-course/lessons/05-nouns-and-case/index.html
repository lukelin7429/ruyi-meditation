Nouns and the Idea of Case

Part I taught you to read Pāli's sounds correctly. Part II teaches you what the words are doing. This lesson introduces the single idea that everything else in Pāli grammar is built on: a noun's ending, not its position in the sentence, tells you its grammatical job.

Level: beginner Prerequisite: Lesson 4 (Part I complete) Part II · Lesson 1 of ~14

Word Order vs. Word Endings

English mostly shows what a noun is doing in a sentence by where it stands and what preposition, if any, comes before it: "the monk gives a robe to the teacher" and "the teacher gives a robe to the monk" are built from identical words, but swapping "monk" and "teacher" completely reverses who receives what, because English relies on position and prepositions to carry that information.

Pāli, like Latin, Sanskrit, Russian, or German, instead changes the ending of the noun itself to show its job. "Monk," "to a monk," and "by a monk" are three different words in Pāli — bhikkhu, bhikkhuno, bhikkhunā — regardless of where any of them sits in the sentence. This has a direct, useful consequence: because the ending already tells you the grammatical role, Pāli word order is comparatively free, and a translator (or a poet arranging a verse's meter) can move words around far more than English allows without creating any ambiguity about who is doing what to whom.

This ending-driven system is called declension, and each grammatical role a noun's ending can mark is called a case. Pāli has eight of them.

The Eight Cases

Every Pāli noun, in both singular and plural, can appear in any of these eight cases. You are not memorizing eight arbitrary labels — each one corresponds to a job English speakers already recognize, just marked differently.

CaseCore jobRough English equivalent
Nominativepaṭhamā, "first"the subject"the monk teaches"
Accusativedutiyā, "second"the direct object; also motion toward"sees the monk"; "goes to the village"
Instrumentaltatiyā, "third"the means or accompaniment"by/with the monk"
Dativecatutthī, "fourth"the recipient or purpose"to/for the monk"
Ablativepañcamī, "fifth"the source, cause, or point of comparison"from the village"
Genitivechaṭṭhī, "sixth"possession or relation"of the monk," "the monk's"
Locativesattamī, "seventh"location, time, or circumstance"in/on/at the village"
Vocativeāmantana, "address"direct address"O monk!"

Two further facts round out the system. Number works the way you'd expect: singular and plural only, nothing in between. Gender is grammatical rather than biological — every noun is masculine, feminine, or neuter regardless of what it refers to, so citta ("mind") and kamma ("action") are both neuter, and gender will matter starting in the next few lessons because the same case can be spelled differently depending on a noun's gender and its final stem vowel.

Cashing In Lesson 4's Preview

Lesson 4 ended by pointing out that tassa, bhagavato, arahato, and sammāsambuddhassa all fill the same grammatical slot — the dative object of namo, "homage" — despite ending differently on the surface. You now have the vocabulary to state this precisely: all four words are in the dative case, singular number. They simply belong to different declension classes (an irregular -ant stem for bhagavato and arahato, versus the ordinary class behind tassa and sammāsambuddhassa), and different classes spell the same case differently — the way English marks the same "more than one" idea differently in "cats," "dishes," and "children," without any of those endings being wrong.

Reading note — Ru-Yi

This is also why memorizing "the dative ending is -assa" would already be too narrow a rule after only Lesson 4 — it happens to be right for two of those four words and wrong for the other two. Lesson 6 gives you the first full, reliable declension class to build on: the single largest noun class in the language, covering most masculine nouns and, with small adjustments, every neuter noun as well.

Practice: Naming the Case

For each English sentence, name which of the eight cases the bold phrase would need to be in if the sentence were Pāli. You don't need Pāli vocabulary for this — just match the job to the case from the table above.

  1. The monk teaches the Dhamma.
  2. She sees the monk.
  3. He walks with patience.
  4. She gave a robe to the monk.
  5. He returned from the village.
  6. This is the robe of the monk.
  7. The Buddha sat in the forest.
  8. O monk, listen well.
Check your answers

1. Nominative — subject of "teaches."

2. Accusative — direct object of "sees."

3. Instrumental — the means/manner.

4. Dative — the recipient.

5. Ablative — the source of motion.

6. Genitive — possession.

7. Locative — location.

8. Vocative — direct address.
