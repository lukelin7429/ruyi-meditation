Chapter 9 · Kammaṭṭhānasaṅgaha · Complete · Final Chapter

From Theory to the Path Itself

This page carries Ru-Yi's complete translation of the Abhidhammattha Saṅgaha's ninth and final chapter, Kammaṭṭhānasaṅgahavibhāga ("The Compendium of Meditation Subjects") — where eight chapters of theory turn at last to practice. Forty subjects of calm meditation are matched to six temperaments and traced to whichever jhāna each can produce; then the seven stages of purification carry insight from the bare discernment of mind-and-matter through ten insight-knowledges to the moment of the supramundane path, and the chapter closes with the four kinds of noble person this path produces. Translated in full, with the Pāli alongside our English, closing with the chapter's own colophon — and with it, the whole nine-chapter Saṅgaha.

Status: chapter 9 complete — all 9 chapters now complete Translator: Ru-Yi Meditation Center Source: PTS (Saddhātissa) edition, cross-checked VRI/CST Author: Ācariya Anuruddha, date uncertain (c. 8th–12th c. CE)

Translation Method

The Pāli source is the Pali Text Society's critical edition of the Abhidhammattha Saṅgaha, edited by Ven. Hammalawa Saddhātissa, cross-checked against the Chaṭṭha Saṅgāyana (Sixth Council) romanized text maintained by the Vipassana Research Institute (VRI, tipitaka.org) for internal consistency.

The English translation is Ru-Yi's own, made directly from the Pāli. No existing English translation is consulted, paraphrased, or used as a guide for wording or structure — including Bhikkhu Bodhi's copyrighted A Comprehensive Manual of Abhidhamma, the standard modern edition of this text. That volume's charts for this chapter (Tables 9.1–9.3), including the 40-subjects and 7-stages summary tables, are editorial apparatus, not part of Anuruddha's root text — several drawn, per its own editorial note, from Ven. Nārada Mahāthera's earlier Manual. This page builds its own plain summary tables directly from the root text's own enumerations.

Reading notes are grounded only in the chapter's own internal structure and its cross-references to earlier chapters, and in classifications that are standard, uncontested Abhidhamma doctrine across the tradition. Each translated passage also carries a short vocabulary list for readers learning Pāli alongside the text.

Two Kinds of Development

The final chapter opens by announcing its two subjects — the same distinction between calm and insight this whole translation has referenced since Chapter 4's account of the absorption process.

Pali Source

Samathavipassanānaṃ bhāvanānam ito paraṃ
Kammaṭṭhānaṃ pavakkhāmi duvidham pi yathākkamaṃ.

Ru-Yi Translation

From here on I shall speak, in due order, of the meditation subjects for the two kinds of development — of calm and of insight.

Key vocabulary — for Pāli learners
kammaṭṭhānant.

meditation subject — literally "field of action, workplace." kamma ("action, work") + ṭhāna ("place, standing").

samatha / vipassanā

calm and insight — samatha (from sam-, "to become peaceful") is one-pointedness developed to the level of jhāna; vipassanā ("seeing in diverse ways") is the direct meditative perception of impermanence, suffering, and non-self, unique to the Buddha's teaching.

Calm — The Forty Subjects

The compendium of calm opens by naming all forty meditation subjects at once, sorted into seven traditional categories.

Pali Source

Tattha samathasangahe tāva dasa kasiṇāni, dasa asubhā, dasa anussatiyo, catasso appamaññāyo, ekā saññā, ekaṃ vavatthānaṃ, cattāro āruppā cā ti sattavidhena samathakammaṭṭhānasangaho.

Ru-Yi Translation

Therein, in the compendium of calm, first: ten kasiṇas, ten kinds of foulness, ten recollections, four illimitables, one perception, one analysis, and four immaterial states — thus the compendium of calm-meditation subjects is sevenfold.

Pali Source

Kathaṃ? Paṭhavīkasiṇaṃ, āpokasiṇaṃ, tejokasiṇaṃ, vāyokasiṇaṃ, nīlakasiṇaṃ, pītakasiṇaṃ, lohitakasiṇaṃ, odātakasiṇaṃ, ākāsakasiṇaṃ, ālokakasiṇañ cā ti imāni dasa kasiṇāni nāma.

Uddhumātakaṃ, vinīlakaṃ, vipubbakaṃ, vicchiddakaṃ, vikkhāyitakaṃ, vikkhittakaṃ, hatavikkhittakaṃ, lohitakaṃ, puḷavakaṃ, aṭṭhikañ cā ti ime dasa asubhā nāma.

Ru-Yi Translation

How? The ten kasiṇas are: the earth-, water-, fire-, air-, blue-, yellow-, red-, white-, space-, and light-kasiṇa.

The ten kinds of foulness are: the bloated, the livid, the festering, the cut-up, the gnawed, the scattered, the hacked-and-scattered, the bleeding, the worm-infested, and the skeleton.

Pali Source

Buddhānussati, dhammānussati, sanghānussati, sīlānussati, cāgānussati, devatānussati, upasamānussati, maraṇānussati, kāyagatāsati, ānāpānasati cā ti imā dasa anussatiyo nāma.

Mettā, karuṇā, muditā, upekkhā cā ti imā catasso appamaññāyo nāma, brahmavihārā ti pi pavuccanti.

Āhāre paṭikkūlasaññā ekā saññā nāma. Catudhātuvavatthānaṃ ekaṃ vavatthānaṃ nāma.

Ākāsānañcāyatanādayo cattāro āruppā nāmā ti sabbathā pi samathaniddese cattāḷīsa kammaṭṭhānāni bhavanti.

Ru-Yi Translation

The ten recollections are: of the Buddha, the Dhamma, the Saṅgha, virtue, generosity, the deities, peace, death, the body, and the breath.

Loving-kindness, compassion, appreciative joy, and equanimity are called the four illimitables, also spoken of as the divine abodes.

The single perception is the perception of the repulsiveness of food. The single analysis is the analysis of the four elements.

The base of infinite space and so on are the four immaterial states. Thus, altogether, in the exposition of calm there are forty subjects of meditation.

Key vocabulary — for Pāli learners
kasiṇant.

a "totality" device — a physical disk or object (of earth, a colour, etc.) contemplated until its mental image can be expanded without limit; from a root meaning "whole, entire."

asubhant.

foulness — the ten stages of a decaying corpse, an especially potent antidote to sensual lust, already met at the very outset of the Visuddhimagga's own concentration section.

brahmavihāra

divine abode — the four illimitables, so named because they are the mental dwelling-places of the Brahmā deities; the same four cetasika-level qualities discussed in Chapter 2 (loving-kindness as a mode of non-hatred, equanimity as a mode of neutrality of mind).

Temperaments, Stages, and Signs

Before matching subjects to meditators, the chapter names the six temperaments a practitioner may have, the three stages every meditative development passes through, and the three signs that mark its progress.

Pali Source

Rāgacaritā, dosacaritā, mohacaritā, saddhācaritā, buddhicaritā, vitakkacaritā cā ti chabbidhena caritasangaho.

Ru-Yi Translation

The compendium of temperaments is sixfold: the lustful, the hateful, the deluded, the faithful, the intellectual, and the discursive.

Pali Source

Parikammabhāvanā, upacārabhāvanā, appanābhāvanā cā ti tisso bhāvanā.

Parikammanimittaṃ, uggahanimittaṃ, paṭibhāganimittañ cā ti tīṇi nimittāni ca veditabbāni.

Ru-Yi Translation

The three developments are: preliminary development, access development, and absorption development.

The three signs should be understood: the preliminary sign, the learning sign, and the counterpart sign.

Key vocabulary — for Pāli learners
caritant.

temperament — a person's natural character, determined by the kamma that produced their rebirth-linking consciousness; lustful pairs with faithful, hateful with intellectual, deluded with discursive, since each pair shares a structural resemblance despite differing in ethical quality.

paṭibhāganimitta

the counterpart sign — a purified mental replica of the meditation object, "appearing as if breaking out from the learning sign, a hundred times more purified, like the moon's disk emerging from behind a cloud"; its arising marks the shift from preliminary to access development.

Suitability and Jhāna Level

The chapter now matches the forty subjects to the six temperaments, then sorts them by which stage of development — and, for the thirty capable of it, which jhāna — they can actually produce.

Pali Source

Caritāsu pana dasa asubhā kāyagatāsatisaṅkhātā koṭṭhāsabhāvanā ca rāgacaritassa sappāyā. Catasso appamaññāyo nīlādīni ca cattāri kasiṇāni dosacaritassa. Ānāpānaṃ mohacaritassa vitakkacaritassa ca. Buddhānussati ādayo cha saddhācaritassa. Maraṇa-upasama-saññā-vavatthānāni buddhicaritassa. Sesāni pana sabbāni pi kammaṭṭhānāni sabbesam pi sappāyāni.

Ru-Yi Translation

Among temperaments, the ten kinds of foulness and mindfulness of the body's parts are suitable for the lustful. The four illimitables and the four colour kasiṇas, for the hateful. Mindfulness of breathing, for the deluded and the discursive. The six recollections beginning with the Buddha, for the faithful. [Recollection of] death, of peace, [the] perception [of loathsomeness in food], and [the] analysis [of the elements], for the intellectual. All the remaining subjects are suitable for everyone.

Pali Source

Bhāvanāsu pana sabbatthā pi parikammabhāvanā labbhat'eva. Buddhānussati ādisu aṭṭhasu saññā-vavatthānesu cā ti dasasu kammaṭṭhānesu upacārabhāvanā va sampajjati, natthi appanā. Sesesu pana samatiṃsa kammaṭṭhānesu appanābhāvanā pi sampajjati.

Tatthā pi dasa kasiṇāni ānāpānañ ca pañcakajjhānikāni. Dasa asubhā kāyagatāsati ca paṭhamajjhānikā. Mettādayo tayo catukkajjhānikā. Upekkhā pañcamajjhānikā. Iti chabbīsati rūpāvacarajjhānikāni kammaṭṭhānāni. Cattāro pana āruppā arūpajjhānikā.

Ru-Yi Translation

Preliminary development is obtained in all [forty]. In ten subjects — the eight recollections beginning with the Buddha, the perception, and the analysis — only access development is accomplished, not absorption. In the remaining thirty subjects, absorption development is also accomplished.

Among these: the ten kasiṇas and mindfulness of breathing produce all five jhānas. The ten kinds of foulness and mindfulness of the body produce only the first jhāna. The first three illimitables produce four jhānas. Equanimity produces only the fifth jhāna. Thus these twenty-six subjects produce the fine-material jhānas; and the four immaterial states, the immaterial jhānas.

Key vocabulary — for Pāli learners
sappāyaadj.

suitable, beneficial — this section's own word for the match between a meditation subject and a temperament; the same term the text uses again when noting that a wide kasiṇa suits the deluded and a small one the discursive.

Reading note — Ru-Yi

Ten subjects cap out at access concentration and can never reach absorption, and the reason given is structural, not a limitation of the practitioner: the eight recollections and the two analytical subjects all require reflecting on many distinct qualities or components at once, which keeps initial application (vitakka) too active and discursive to settle into the one-pointed fixity absorption demands. This matches exactly what Chapter 1 already established about jhāna's own internal logic — one-pointedness deepens only as the mind narrows its engagement with the object, and a subject built from multiplicity cannot narrow that far.

Insight — The Seven Purifications

The chapter turns from calm to insight, opening with the seven stages of purification that structure the entire path — the same "sixfold purification" already referenced in Chapter 7's account of the thirty-seven requisites of enlightenment.

Pali Source

Vipassanākammaṭṭhāne pana sīlavisuddhi, cittavisuddhi, diṭṭhivisuddhi, kankhāvitaraṇavisuddhi, maggāmaggañāṇadassanavisuddhi, paṭipadāñāṇadassanavisuddhi, ñāṇadassanavisuddhi cā ti sattavidhena visuddhisangaho.

Ru-Yi Translation

In the meditation subjects of insight, the compendium of purifications is sevenfold: purification of virtue, purification of mind, purification of view, purification by overcoming doubt, purification by knowledge and vision of what is path and not-path, purification by knowledge and vision of the way, and purification by knowledge and vision.

Pali Source

Aniccalakkhaṇaṃ, dukkhalakkhaṇaṃ, anattalakkhaṇañ cā ti tīṇi lakkhaṇāni. Aniccānupassanā, dukkhānupassanā, anattānupassanā cā ti tisso anupassanā.

(1) Sammasanañāṇaṃ, (2) udayabbayañāṇaṃ, (3) bhangañāṇaṃ, (4) bhayañāṇaṃ, (5) ādīnavañāṇaṃ, (6) nibbidāñāṇaṃ, (7) muñcitukamyatāñāṇaṃ, (8) paṭisankhāñāṇaṃ, (9) sankhār'upekkhāñāṇaṃ, (10) anulomañāṇañ cā ti dasa vipassanāñāṇāni.

Suññato vimokkho, animitto vimokkho, appaṇihito vimokkho cā ti tayo vimokkhā. Suññatānupassanā, animittānupassanā, appaṇihitānupassanā cā ti tīṇi vimokkhamukhāni ca veditabbāni.

Ru-Yi Translation

There are three characteristics: impermanence, suffering, and non-self. There are three contemplations: of impermanence, of suffering, and of non-self.

There are ten insight-knowledges: (1) knowledge of comprehension, (2) knowledge of rise and fall, (3) knowledge of dissolution, (4) knowledge of the fearful, (5) knowledge of danger, (6) knowledge of disenchantment, (7) knowledge of desire for deliverance, (8) knowledge of reflective contemplation, (9) knowledge of equanimity towards formations, and (10) knowledge of conformity.

There are three emancipations: the void, the signless, and the desireless. And three doors to emancipation should be understood: contemplation of the void, of the signless, and of the desireless.

Key vocabulary — for Pāli learners
visuddhif.

purification — each of the seven is a support (paccaya, in the Chapter 8 sense) for the one that follows; the first corresponds to the path's virtue factor, the second to its concentration, the last five to its wisdom.

udayabbaya

rise and fall — the second insight-knowledge, and the one whose "tender" and "mature" phases are separated by an entire purification: its tender phase belongs to the fifth purification, its mature phase to the sixth.

The First Two Purifications

Purification of virtue is defined by the fourfold code of a monk's conduct; purification of mind, by whichever of two paths — through calm first, or straight into insight — a meditator takes to reach it.

Pali Source

Kathaṃ? Pātimokkhasaṃvarasīlaṃ, indriyasaṃvarasīlaṃ, ājīvapārisuddhisīlaṃ, paccayasannissitasīlañ cā ti catupārisuddhisīlaṃ sīlavisuddhi nāma.

Upacārasamādhi, appanāsamādhi cā ti duvidho pi samādhi cittavisuddhi nāma.

Ru-Yi Translation

How? Restraint according to the Pātimokkha, restraint of the sense faculties, purity of livelihood, and [virtue] connected with the use of requisites: thus the fourfold purified virtue is called purification of virtue.

Access concentration and absorption concentration, both: this twofold concentration is called purification of mind.

Key vocabulary — for Pāli learners
samathayāna / suddhavipassanāyāna

the vehicle of calm / the vehicle of pure insight — two recognized routes to purification of mind: one develops jhāna first as a basis, the other (the "dry-insight" approach) reaches an equivalent concentration, khaṇikasamādhi, "momentary concentration," directly through sustained mindfulness of changing experience.

Purification of View, and by Overcoming Doubt

The third and fourth purifications are the ground floor of insight proper: first discerning mind-and-matter as they intrinsically are, then discerning what conditions them.

Pali Source

Lakkhaṇa-rasa-paccupaṭṭhāna-padaṭṭhāna-vasena nāmarūpapariggaho diṭṭhivisuddhi nāma.

Tesam eva ca nāmarūpānaṃ paccayapariggaho kankhāvitaraṇavisuddhi nāma.

Ru-Yi Translation

The discernment of mind-and-matter by way of characteristic, function, manifestation, and proximate cause is called purification of view.

The discernment of the conditions of that same mind-and-matter is called purification by overcoming doubt.

Key vocabulary — for Pāli learners
lakkhaṇa-rasa-paccupaṭṭhāna-padaṭṭhāna

the same fourfold defining device met at the very start of this translation, in Chapter 1's analysis of citta itself — now turned into the actual method a meditator uses to discern mind-and-matter directly.

nāmarūpavavatthānañāṇa

another name for purification of view: "the analytical knowledge of mind-and-matter" — it purifies specifically the wrong view of a permanent self, by revealing personality as a compound of interdependent factors with no controller behind them.

Comprehension, and the Ten Imperfections of Insight

Having discerned conditions, the meditator groups the conditioned formations and comprehends them by the three characteristics — but ten remarkable experiences can now arise that must themselves be recognized as obstacles, not attainments.

Pali Source

Obhāso pīti passaddhi adhimokkho ca paggaho
Sukhaṃ ñāṇam upaṭṭhānam upekkhā ca nikanti cā ti.

Obhāsādi-vipassan'upakkilese paripanthapariggahavasena maggāmaggalakkhaṇavavatthānaṃ maggāmaggañāṇadassanavisuddhi nāma.

Ru-Yi Translation

An aura, zest, tranquillity, resolution, and exertion; happiness, knowledge, established mindfulness, equanimity, and attachment.

The determination of the characteristics of path and not-path, by discerning these imperfections of insight — the aura and the rest — as obstacles: this is called purification by knowledge and vision of what is path and what is not path.

Key vocabulary — for Pāli learners
vipassanupakkilesa

imperfection of insight — ten genuinely impressive experiences (a radiant aura, intense zest, blissful tranquillity, and the rest) that arise as knowledge of comprehension matures, easily mistaken by an inexperienced meditator for the path itself, which is precisely why this purification exists.

nikantif.

attachment — the tenth and most dangerous item on the list: subtle delight in the other nine imperfections themselves, the very thing that must be recognized and released for insight to continue maturing.

Reading note — Ru-Yi

This list is a warning built directly into the structure of the path: nine of the ten items named here — an aura of light, zest, tranquillity, happiness, and the rest — are not defects in any ordinary sense. They are exactly what sustained, well-developed insight practice actually produces. The purification exists precisely because these genuine, impressive experiences are also exactly what a meditator is most likely to mistake for full liberation, and the tenth item, attachment, names the specific error: not the arising of these states, but delighting in them as if they were the goal rather than a milestone still short of it.

The Mature Path — Ten Insight-Knowledges in Sequence

Once the imperfections are recognized and set aside, insight matures through the remaining insight-knowledges in strict sequence, each a deepening of the same three characteristics — this is purification by knowledge and vision of the way.

Pali Source

Tato paraṃ pana tathāpariggahitesu sappaccayesu tebhūmakasankhāresu atītādibhedabhinnesu khandhādinayam ārabbha kalāpavasena sankhipitvā aniccaṃ khayaṭṭhena, dukkhaṃ bhayaṭṭhena, anattā asārakaṭṭhenā ti addhānavasena santativasena khaṇavasena vā sammasanañāṇena lakkhaṇattayaṃ sammasantassa tesv'eva paccayavasena khaṇavasena ca udayabbayañāṇena udayabbayaṃ samanupassantassa ca.

Ru-Yi Translation

Thereafter, having thus discerned the formations of the three planes together with their conditions — divided as past and the rest — he gathers them into groups by way of the aggregates and the like, and comprehends, with the knowledge of comprehension, the three characteristics: impermanence in the sense of destruction, suffering in the sense of fearfulness, non-self in the sense of coreless-ness — by way of duration, continuity, or moment. And he observes, with the knowledge of rise and fall, their rising and falling, by way of condition and by way of moment.

sankhār'upekkhāñāṇa

knowledge of equanimity towards formations — the ninth knowledge, in which "I" and "mine" are seen nowhere in the formations, so the mind grows neutral toward them, neither fleeing in terror nor grasping in delight.

anulomaadj.

conformity — the tenth knowledge, "conforming" both to the eight insight-knowledges that precede it and to the supramundane path about to follow; it is not a separate stage so much as the final threshold moment of the ninth.

The Path Arises

The seventh and final purification: change-of-lineage, the path itself, and its fruit — the supramundane cognitive process, following exactly the absorption process Chapter 4 already described, now with Nibbāna as object.

Pali Source

Tass'evaṃ paṭipajjantassa pana vipassanāparipākam āgamma idāni appanā uppajjissatī ti bhavangaṃ vocchinditvā uppannamanodvārāvajjanānantaraṃ dve tīṇī vipassanācittāni yaṃ kiñci aniccādilakkhaṇam ārabbha parikamm'-opacār'-ānulomanāmena pavattanti.

Tato paraṃ gotrabhūcittaṃ nibbānam ālambitvā puthujjanagottam abhibhavantaṃ ariyagottam abhisambhontañ ca pavattati. Tass'ānantaram eva maggo dukkhasaccaṃ parijānanto samudayasaccaṃ pajahanto nirodhasaccaṃ sacchikaronto maggasaccaṃ bhāvanāvasena appanāvīthim otarati. Tato paraṃ dve tīṇi phalacittāni pavattitvā nirujjhanti. Tato paraṃ bhavangapāto va hoti.

Ru-Yi Translation

For one practicing thus, owing to the ripening of insight — "now absorption will arise" — the life-continuum is arrested, and immediately after mind-door-adverting arises, two or three moments of insight consciousness occur, having as object any one of the characteristics such as impermanence, under the names preparation, access, and conformity.

Thereafter, the change-of-lineage citta, taking Nibbāna as object, occurs, overcoming the lineage of the worldling and evolving the lineage of the noble one. Immediately after it, the path — fully understanding the truth of suffering, abandoning the truth of its origin, realizing the truth of its cessation, developing the truth of the path — descends, by way of development, into the course of absorption. After that, two or three fruition cittas occur and cease. After that, subsidence into the life-continuum.

Pali Source

Puna bhavangaṃ vocchinditvā paccavekkhaṇañāṇāni pavattanti.

Maggaṃ phalañ ca nibbānaṃ paccavekkhati paṇḍito
Hīne kilese sese ca paccavekkhati vā na vā.
Chabbisuddhikkamen'evaṃ bhāvetabbo catubbidho
Ñāṇadassanavisuddhi nāma maggo pavuccati.

Ru-Yi Translation

Again, the life-continuum being arrested, the reviewing knowledges occur.

The wise one reviews the path, the fruit, and Nibbāna; the defilements destroyed, and those remaining, he may or may not review. Thus the fourfold path, to be developed in this way by the sequence of the sixfold purity, is called purification by knowledge and vision.

Key vocabulary — for Pāli learners
gotrabhūnt.

change-of-lineage — already met in Chapter 4's absorption process; here it marks the passage from the "lineage" of worldlings to that of the noble ones, the first citta ever to take Nibbāna as object, though it cannot yet dispel the defilements the way the path itself does.

paccavekkhaṇañāṇa

reviewing knowledge — occurring after each path attainment; the text notes it is not invariable, since an Arahant, having no defilements left, has nothing further to review on that count.

Three Doors, Three Names for the Same Path

The path and its fruit take one of three names, depending on which of the three characteristics the meditator's insight settled upon just before the breakthrough.

Pali Source

Tattha anattānupassanā attābhinivesaṃ muñcantī suññatānupassanā nāma vimokkhamukhaṃ hoti. Aniccānupassanā vipallāsanimittaṃ muñcantī animittānupassanā nāma. Dukkhānupassanā taṇhāpaṇidhiṃ muñcantī appaṇihitānupassanā nāma.

Tasmā yadi vuṭṭhānagāminīvipassanā anattato vipassati, suññato vimokkho nāma hoti maggo; yadi aniccato vipassati, animitto vimokkho nāma; yadi dukkhato vipassati, appaṇihito vimokkho nāmā ti ca.

Ru-Yi Translation

Herein, the contemplation of non-self, releasing the adherence to self, becomes the door to emancipation called the contemplation of the void. The contemplation of impermanence, releasing the sign of perversion, is called the contemplation of the signless. The contemplation of suffering, releasing the aspiration of craving, is called the contemplation of the desireless.

Therefore, if the insight leading to emergence contemplates by way of non-self, the path is called the void emancipation; if by way of impermanence, the signless emancipation; if by way of suffering, the desireless emancipation.

Key vocabulary — for Pāli learners
vimokkhamukha

door to emancipation — whichever of the three contemplations a meditator's insight happens to settle on, becoming their immediate approach to the path; the three names apply loosely to every path and fruit regardless, since all take Nibbāna as object and Nibbāna itself is void, signless, and desireless at once.

The Four Noble Persons

The chapter closes its account of the path by naming the four kinds of person it produces, each defined by exactly which fetters and defilements their path has permanently eradicated.

Pali Source

Ettha pana sotāpattimaggaṃ bhāvetvā diṭṭhivicikicchāpahānena pahīnāpāyagamano sattakkhattuparamo sotāpanno nāma hoti.

Sakadāgāmimaggaṃ bhāvetvā rāgadosamohānaṃ tanukarattā sakadāgāmī nāma hoti, sakid eva imaṃ lokaṃ āgantā.

Anāgāmimaggaṃ bhāvetvā kāmarāgavyāpādānaṃ anavasesappahānena anāgāmī nāma hoti, anāgantā itthattaṃ.

Arahattamaggaṃ bhāvetvā anavasesakilesappahānena arahā nāma hoti khīṇāsavo loke aggadakkhiṇeyyo.

Ru-Yi Translation

Herein: having developed the path of stream-entry, by abandoning wrong view and doubt, one whose way to the woeful states is cut off, [reborn] seven times at most, is called a stream-enterer.

Having developed the path of once-returning, by the attenuation of greed, hatred, and delusion, one who comes back to this world only once more is called a once-returner.

Having developed the path of non-returning, by the complete abandoning of sensual lust and ill will, one who does not come back to this state is called a non-returner.

Having developed the path of Arahantship, by the complete abandoning of defilements, one whose taints are destroyed, the foremost recipient of offerings in the world, is called an Arahant.

Key vocabulary — for Pāli learners
sattakkhattuparama

"seven times at most" — the stream-enterer's guaranteed ceiling on further rebirths, one of three traditional sub-types alongside the "one who returns after two or three good families" and the "one-seed" type with only one rebirth left.

khīṇāsava

"one whose taints are destroyed" — the standard epithet for the Arahant, met already in Chapter 4's rules on javana, where it explained why no wholesome or unwholesome javanas can arise for such a person any longer.

Two Further Attainments

The chapter's final technical section: fruition attainment, open to every noble person at their own level, and the attainment of cessation, open only to non-returners and Arahants of the highest meditative mastery.

Pali Source

Phalasamāpattiyo pan'ettha sabbesam pi yathāsakaphalavasena sādhāraṇā'va. Nirodhasamāpattisamāpajjanaṃ pana anāgāmīnañ c'eva arahantānañ ca labbhati.

Ru-Yi Translation

Herein, fruition attainments are common to all [noble persons], each according to their own fruit. But the attainment of cessation is obtained only by non-returners and Arahants.

Pali Source

Vuṭṭhānakāle pana anāgāmino anāgāmiphalacittaṃ arahato arahattaphalacittaṃ ekavāram eva pavattitvā bhavangapāto hoti. Tato paraṃ paccavekkhaṇañāṇaṃ pavattati.

Ayam ettha samāpattibhedo. Niṭṭhito ca vipassanākammaṭṭhānanayo.

Ru-Yi Translation

At the time of emergence, in a non-returner the fruition of non-returning, and in an Arahant the fruition of Arahantship, occurs just once, then subsidence into the life-continuum. After that, reviewing knowledge occurs.

Herein, this is the analysis of attainments. And the method of insight-meditation is finished.

Key vocabulary — for Pāli learners
nirodhasamāpatti

the attainment of cessation — already introduced in Chapter 4; obtainable only by a non-returner or Arahant with full mastery of every fine-material and immaterial jhāna, and only in the sensuous or fine-material planes.

phalasamāpatti

fruition attainment — a meditative re-entry into one's own already-attained fruition consciousness, undertaken to experience the peace of Nibbāna directly, available in principle to any of the four grades of noble person at their own level.

Kammaṭṭhānasaṅgaha Complete — And the Whole Saṅgaha With It

The chapter, and the entire nine-chapter work, closes with a single verse of aspiration.

Pali Source

Bhāvetabbaṃ pan'icc'evaṃ bhāvanādvayam uttamaṃ
Paṭipattirasassādaṃ patthayantena sāsane.

Iti Abhidhammatthasaṅgahe Kammaṭṭhānasaṅgahavibhāgo nāma navamo paricchedo.

Ru-Yi Translation

Thus this twofold development — so excellent — should be cultivated by one who longs to taste the flavour of practice within this Dispensation.

Thus ends, in the Compendium of the Things Contained in the Abhidhamma, the ninth chapter, called the Compendium of Meditation Subjects.

This closes the translation of the Abhidhammattha Saṅgaha's ninth chapter, Kammaṭṭhānasaṅgaha ("The Compendium of Meditation Subjects") — the forty subjects of calm matched to six temperaments and traced to the jhāna each can produce, and the seven purifications carrying insight from the bare discernment of mind-and-matter through the ten insight-knowledges and the ten imperfections that can be mistaken for them, to the moment of the supramundane path, its three names, and the four kinds of noble person it produces — closing with the chapter's own colophon.

Abhidhammatthasaṅgaho Niṭṭhito — The Compendium Is Finished

With this ninth chapter, Ru-Yi's translation of the entire Abhidhammattha Saṅgaha is complete: all nine chapters, made directly from the Pāli, from Chapter 1's first verse of homage — "Having bowed in homage to the Peerless One..." — to this chapter's closing wish that the reader taste the flavour of practice. Ācariya Anuruddha's own opening promise, to speak "a compendium of the things contained in the Abhidhamma," is now fully kept on this site: consciousness and its 89 (121) types; the 52 mental factors; the six further cross-cuts of feeling, root, function, door, object, and base; the cognitive process down to its seventeen mind-moments; what happens outside any process — rebirth, kamma, and death; the 28 kinds of matter, closing with Nibbāna; the classical categories reduced to fourteen cetasikas and fourteen more; the twenty-four conditions and the concepts that lie outside them all; and now, finally, the meditation subjects that the entire preceding theoretical structure exists to serve.
