Chapter 7 · Samuccayasaṅgaha · Complete

Seventy-Two Things, Sorted Every Way

This page carries Ru-Yi's complete translation of the Abhidhammattha Saṅgaha's seventh chapter, Samuccayasaṅgahavibhāga ("The Compendium of Categories") — the classical lists inherited from the Suttas and the Abhidhamma Piṭaka, all gathered here and reduced to the small set of cittas and cetasikas that actually compose them: the taints, floods, fetters, and other defilements; the mixed categories of roots, jhāna factors, faculties, and powers; the 37 requisites of enlightenment; and the compendium of the whole — the aggregates, sense bases, elements, and Four Noble Truths. Translated in full, with the Pāli alongside our English, closing with the chapter's own colophon.

Translation Method

The Pāli source is the Pali Text Society's critical edition of the Abhidhammattha Saṅgaha, edited by Ven. Hammalawa Saddhātissa, cross-checked against the Chaṭṭha Saṅgāyana (Sixth Council) romanized text maintained by the Vipassana Research Institute (VRI, tipitaka.org) for internal consistency.

The English translation is Ru-Yi's own, made directly from the Pāli. No existing English translation is consulted, paraphrased, or used as a guide for wording or structure — including Bhikkhu Bodhi's copyrighted A Comprehensive Manual of Abhidhamma, the standard modern edition of this text. That volume's charts for this chapter (Tables 7.1–7.4) are editorial apparatus, not part of Anuruddha's root text — several are drawn, per its own editorial note, from Ven. Nārada Mahāthera's earlier Manual. This page builds its own plain summary tables directly from the root text's own enumerations.

Reading notes are grounded only in the chapter's own internal structure and its cross-references to earlier chapters, and in classifications that are standard, uncontested Abhidhamma and Sutta doctrine across the tradition. Each translated passage also carries a short vocabulary list for readers learning Pāli alongside the text.

Seventy-Two Entities, Four Ways of Grouping Them

Having analyzed the four ultimate realities down to their 72 distinct entities across six chapters, the author now shows how the Suttas and Abhidhamma Piṭaka group these same entities into the classical lists a student is expected to know.

Pali Source

Dvāsattatividhā vuttā vatthudhammā salakkhaṇā
Tesaṃ dāni yathāyogaṃ pavakkhāmi samuccayaṃ.

Ru-Yi Translation

The seventy-two kinds of entities, each with its own characteristic, have been spoken of. Now I shall tell of their categories, each in its fitting way.

Pali Source

Akusalasangaho, missakasangaho, bodhipakkhiyasangaho, sabbasangaho cā ti samuccayasangaho catubbidho veditabbo.

Ru-Yi Translation

The compendium of categories should be understood as fourfold: the compendium of the unwholesome, the compendium of mixed categories, the compendium of the requisites of enlightenment, and the compendium of the whole.

Key vocabulary — for Pāli learners
vatthudhamma

"entity" — the 72 distinct realities Chapters 1–6 have described: 1 citta (all 89 cittas share one nature), 52 cetasikas, 18 concretely-produced material phenomena, and 1 Nibbāna (1+52+18+1=72).

samuccayam.

category, collection — saṃ- + uccaya ("heaping up"). The chapter's own title-word: not new phenomena, but new ways of heaping the same 72 into traditional lists.

Reading note — Ru-Yi

This chapter adds nothing new to the inventory of reality — every item in every list below is already one of the 72 entities Chapters 1 through 6 established. What it adds is cross-referencing: showing that the Suttas' "ten fetters" and the Abhidhamma's "seven latent dispositions" and the meditation manual's "five hindrances" are not competing systems, but different partial views of the very same small set of cetasikas, mostly the fourteen unwholesome ones from Chapter 2.

The Unwholesome — Taints, Floods, Bonds, Knots, Clingings

Five classical four-membered lists of defilement open the chapter — taints, floods, and bonds share an identical structure (sensual desire, existence, views, ignorance); the bodily knots and clingings vary the pattern slightly.

Pali Source

Kathaṃ? Akusalasangahe tāva cattāro āsavā: kāmāsavo, bhavāsavo, diṭṭhāsavo, avijjāsavo.

Cattāro oghā: kāmogho, bhavogho, diṭṭhogho, avijjogho.

Cattāro yogā: kāmayogo, bhavayogo, diṭṭhiyogo, avijjāyogo.

Cattāro ganthā: abhijjhā kāyagantho, vyāpādo kāyagantho, sīlabbataparāmāso kāyagantho idaṃsaccābhiniveso kāyagantho.

Cattāro upādānā: kāmupādānaṃ, diṭṭhupādānaṃ, sīlabbatupādānaṃ, attavādupādānaṃ.

Ru-Yi Translation

How? First, in the compendium of the unwholesome, there are four taints: the taint of sensual desire, of existence, of views, of ignorance.

Four floods: of sensual desire, of existence, of views, of ignorance.

Four bonds: of sensual desire, of existence, of views, of ignorance.

Four bodily knots: the bodily knot of covetousness, of ill will, of adherence to rites and ceremonies, of the dogmatic clinging that "this alone is true."

Four clingings: clinging to sense pleasures, to views, to rites and ceremonies, to a doctrine of self.

Key vocabulary — for Pāli learners
āsavam.

taint — literally "that which flows out" (ā- + savati), used of pus oozing from a wound or of long-fermented liquor; the commentaries gloss it as flowing all the way up to the highest plane, or up to the very threshold of the path.

gantham.

knot — ties the mind to "the body," here meaning any aggregation, mental or physical, present or future; covetousness and ill will are greed and hatred outright, the other two are modes of wrong view.

upādānant.

clinging — an intensified form of craving; three of the four are modes of wrong view, the fourth (sense-pleasure clinging) a mode of greed.

The Unwholesome — Hindrances, Dispositions, Fetters, Defilements

The chapter continues with the six hindrances, seven latent dispositions, two rival ten-fold lists of fetters (Sutta and Abhidhamma versions), and the ten root defilements — closing with a verse reducing the whole set to fourteen cetasikas.

Pali Source

Cha nīvaraṇāni: kāmacchandanīvaraṇaṃ, vyāpādanīvaraṇaṃ, thīnamiddhanīvaraṇaṃ, uddhaccakukkuccanīvaraṇaṃ, vicikicchānīvaraṇaṃ, avijjānīvaraṇaṃ.

Satt'ānusayā: kāmarāgānusayo, bhavarāgānusayo, paṭighānusayo, mānānusayo, diṭṭhānusayo, vicikicchānusayo, avijjānusayo.

Ru-Yi Translation

Six hindrances: the hindrance of sensual desire, of ill will, of sloth-and-torpor, of restlessness-and-worry, of doubt, of ignorance.

Seven latent dispositions: to sensual lust, to attachment to existence, to resistance, to conceit, to views, to doubt, to ignorance.

Pali Source

Dasa saṃyojanāni: kāmarāgasaṃyojanaṃ, rūparāgasaṃyojanaṃ, arūparāgasaṃyojanaṃ, paṭighasaṃyojanaṃ, mānasaṃyojanaṃ, diṭṭhisaṃyojanaṃ, sīlabbataparāmāsasaṃyojanaṃ, vicikicchāsaṃyojanaṃ, uddhaccasaṃyojanaṃ, avijjāsaṃyojanaṃ, suttante.

Aparāni dasa saṃyojanāni: kāmarāgasaṃyojanaṃ, bhavarāgasaṃyojanaṃ, paṭighasaṃyojanaṃ, mānasaṃyojanaṃ, diṭṭhisaṃyojanaṃ, sīlabbataparāmāsasaṃyojanaṃ, vicikicchāsaṃyojanaṃ, issāsaṃyojanaṃ, macchariyasaṃyojanaṃ, avijjāsaṃyojanaṃ, abhidhamme.

Ru-Yi Translation

Ten fetters, according to the Suttanta method: to sensual lust, to attachment to fine-material existence, to attachment to immaterial existence, to resistance, to conceit, to views, to adherence to rites and ceremonies, to doubt, to restlessness, to ignorance.

A further ten fetters, according to the Abhidhamma method: to sensual lust, to attachment to existence, to resistance, to conceit, to views, to adherence to rites and ceremonies, to doubt, to envy, to avarice, to ignorance.

Pali Source

Dasa kilesā: lobho, doso, moho, māno, diṭṭhi, vicikicchā, thīnaṃ, uddhaccaṃ, ahirikaṃ, anottappaṃ.

Ru-Yi Translation

Ten defilements: greed, hatred, delusion, conceit, wrong view, doubt, sloth, restlessness, shamelessness, fearlessness of wrongdoing.

Pali Source

Āsavoghā ca yogā ca tayo ganthā ca vatthuto
Upādānā duve vuttā aṭṭha nīvaraṇā siyuṃ.
Chaḷevānusayā honti nava saṃyojanā matā
Kilesā dasa vutto'yaṃ navadhā pāpasangaho.

Ru-Yi Translation

By way of entity, the taints, floods, bonds, and knots are threefold. The clingings are said to be twofold; the hindrances, eightfold. The latent dispositions are only six; the fetters are held to be nine. The defilements are ten. Thus this compendium of evil is stated as ninefold.

Key vocabulary — for Pāli learners
nīvaraṇant.

hindrance — two pairs are compounded into single hindrances (sloth+torpor, restlessness+worry) because they share function, condition, and antidote; the first five block jhāna, the sixth blocks wisdom.

anusayam.

latent disposition — from anuseti, "lies along with." A defilement dormant in the mental continuum until conditions bring it to the surface; eradicated stage by stage only by the supramundane paths.

saṃyojanant.

fetter — the only list in this section given in two rival versions, since the Sutta Piṭaka's own ten-fetter scheme (ending in restlessness) differs from the Abhidhamma Piṭaka's (which substitutes envy and avarice).

Reading note — Ru-Yi

The closing verse's arithmetic is the whole point of the section: nine traditional lists, ranging from four to ten members each, reduce to only fourteen distinct cetasikas — precisely the fourteen unwholesome mental factors Chapter 2 already enumerated. Every one of these classical Sutta and Abhidhamma terms — taint, flood, bond, knot, clinging, hindrance, disposition, fetter, defilement — is a different lens trained on the same small set of unwholesome states, distinguished only by which functional aspect of a given defilement each lens happens to be naming.

Mixed Categories — Roots, Jhāna Factors, Path Factors, Faculties

The second compendium is "mixed" because, unlike the first, it gathers factors that can be wholesome, unwholesome, or indeterminate all under one heading — beginning with the six roots already met in Chapter 3, then the seven jhāna factors and twelve path factors, right and wrong together.

Pali Source

Missakasangahe cha hetū: lobho, doso, moho, alobho, adoso, amoho.

Satta jhānangāni: vitakko, vicāro, pīti, ekaggatā, somanassaṃ, domanassaṃ, upekkhā.

Ru-Yi Translation

In the compendium of mixed categories, there are six roots: greed, hatred, delusion, non-greed, non-hatred, non-delusion.

Seven jhāna factors: initial application, sustained application, zest, one-pointedness, joy, displeasure, equanimity.

Pali Source

Dvādasa maggangāni: sammādiṭṭhi, sammāsankappo, sammāvācā, sammākammanto, sammā-ājivo, sammāvāyāmo, sammāsati, sammāsamādhi, micchādiṭṭhi, micchāsankappo, micchāvāyāmo, micchāsamādhi.

Ru-Yi Translation

Twelve path factors: right view, right intention, right speech, right action, right livelihood, right effort, right mindfulness, right concentration; wrong view, wrong intention, wrong effort, wrong concentration.

Pali Source

Bāvīsat'indriyāni: cakkhundriyaṃ, sotindriyaṃ, ghānindriyaṃ, jivhindriyaṃ, kāyindriyaṃ, itthindriyaṃ, purisindriyaṃ, jīvitindriyaṃ, manindriyaṃ, sukhindriyaṃ, dukkhindriyaṃ, somanassindriyaṃ, domanassindriyaṃ, upekkhindriyaṃ, saddhindriyaṃ, viriyindriyaṃ, satindriyaṃ, samādhindriyaṃ, paññindriyaṃ, anaññātaññassāmītindriyaṃ, aññindriyaṃ, aññātāvindriyaṃ.

Ru-Yi Translation

Twenty-two faculties: the eye, ear, nose, tongue, body, femininity, masculinity, life, mind, pleasure, pain, joy, displeasure, and equanimity faculties; the faculties of faith, energy, mindfulness, concentration, and wisdom; the faculty "I shall know the unknown," the faculty of final knowledge, and the faculty of one who has final knowledge.

Key vocabulary — for Pāli learners
jhānaṅga

jhāna factor — used here in the broader sense of "close contemplation" (upanijjhāyana) of an object, so these seven occur even outside actual meditative absorption; displeasure is the sole exclusively-unwholesome member.

anaññātaññassāmītindriya / aññindriya / aññātāvindriya

the three "supramundane knowledge" faculties — respectively the knowledge of the path of stream-entry, the six intermediate supramundane knowledges, and the knowledge of the fruit of Arahantship; three names tracking one advancing wisdom.

Reading note — Ru-Yi

The twelve path factors are the clearest demonstration yet of this chapter's whole method: eight "right" and four "wrong," presented as if structurally parallel, but reducing to entirely different kinds of fact. Wrong view is a real, distinct cetasika — the same wrong view from Chapter 2's fourteen unwholesome factors. But wrong speech, wrong action, and wrong livelihood have no cetasika of their own at all; they are just ordinary unwholesome bodily and verbal conduct, motivated by whatever defilement happens to be present, and there is no "wrong mindfulness" factor whatsoever, since mindfulness is exclusively a beautiful cetasika that cannot arise in unwholesome consciousness in the first place. The symmetry of the list (eight versus four) is a presentation device, not a claim that both sides are built the same way underneath.

Mixed Categories — Powers, Predominants, Nutriments

Three shorter lists close the mixed categories: the nine powers (unshakable versions of qualities already met as faculties), the four predominants (each capable of dominating a citta, but never more than one at a time), and the four nutriments.

Pali Source

Nava balāni: saddhābalaṃ, viriyabalaṃ, satibalaṃ, samādhibalaṃ, paññābalaṃ, hiribalaṃ, ottappabalaṃ, ahirikabalaṃ, anottappabalaṃ.

Cattāro adhipatī: chandādhipati, viriyādhipati, cittādhipati, vīmaṃsādhipati.

Cattāro āhārā: kabaḷīkāro āhāro, phasso dutiyo, manosañcetanā tatiyā, viññāṇaṃ catutthaṃ.

Ru-Yi Translation

Nine powers: the powers of faith, energy, mindfulness, concentration, wisdom, shame, and fear of wrongdoing; the powers of shamelessness and fearlessness of wrongdoing.

Four predominants: the predominance of desire, of energy, of consciousness, of investigation.

Four nutriments: edible food; contact, second; mental volition, third; consciousness, fourth.

Pali Source

Pañcaviññāṇesu jhānangāni, aviriyesu balāni, ahetukesu maggangāni na labbhanti. Tathā vicikicchācitte ekaggatā maggindriyabalabhāvaṃ na gacchati. Dvihetuka-tihetukajavanesv'eva yathāsambhavaṃ adhipati eko'va labbhati.

Ru-Yi Translation

The jhāna factors are not found in the fivefold sense-consciousness; the powers, not in those [cittas] without energy; the path factors, not in the rootless. Likewise, in the citta accompanied by doubt, one-pointedness does not attain the status of a path factor, faculty, or power. Only a single predominant is obtained, according to circumstance, and only in javanas with two or three roots.

Pali Source

Cha hetū pañca jhānangā maggangā nava vatthuto
Soḷas'indriyadhammā ca baladhammā nav'eritā.
Cattāro'dhipatī vuttā tathāhārā ti sattadhā
Kusalādisamākiṇṇo vutto missakasangaho.

Ru-Yi Translation

By way of entity, six roots, five jhāna factors, nine path factors, and sixteen faculties have been told; nine powers have likewise been declared. Four predominants have been stated, and likewise the four nutriments. Thus the compendium of mixed categories — a mixture of wholesome and other states — has been stated in seven ways.

Key vocabulary — for Pāli learners
balant.

power — the same five spiritual qualities as the faculties (indriya) above, re-described as "unshakable by their opposites" rather than merely "controlling their own domain."

adhipatim.

predominant — compared to a king ruling over his ministers (the faculties, each governing only its own sphere): only one predominant can dominate a given citta at a time, and only in javanas with two or three roots.

āhāram.

nutriment — a strong supporting condition, not only edible food: contact sustains feeling, volition sustains rebirth (since volition is kamma), and consciousness sustains the whole mind-body compound.

The Requisites of Enlightenment — The First Five Groups

The third compendium gathers the 37 bodhipakkhiyā dhammā, "states on the side of enlightenment" — the Buddha's own compression of the entire path into seven groups, opening with the four foundations of mindfulness.

Pali Source

Bodhipakkhiyasangahe cattāro satipaṭṭhānā: kāyānupassanā-satipaṭṭhānaṃ, vedanānupassanā-satipaṭṭhānaṃ, cittānupassanā-satipaṭṭhānaṃ, dhammānupassanā-satipaṭṭhānaṃ.

Cattāro sammappadhānā: uppannānaṃ pāpakānaṃ dhammānaṃ pahānāya vāyāmo, anuppannānaṃ pāpakānaṃ dhammānaṃ anuppādāya vāyāmo, anuppannānaṃ kusalānaṃ dhammānaṃ uppādāya vāyāmo, uppannānaṃ kusalānaṃ dhammānaṃ bhiyyobhāvāya vāyāmo.

Ru-Yi Translation

In the compendium of the requisites of enlightenment, there are four foundations of mindfulness: the foundation of mindfulness in contemplating the body, feelings, consciousness, and mental objects.

Four supreme efforts: the effort to abandon arisen evil states, the effort to prevent unarisen evil states from arising, the effort to develop unarisen wholesome states, the effort to increase arisen wholesome states.

Pali Source

Cattāro iddhipādā: chandiddhipādo, viriyiddhipādo, cittiddhipādo, vīmaṃsiddhipādo.

Pañc'indriyāni: saddhindriyaṃ, viriyindriyaṃ, satindriyaṃ, samādhindriyaṃ, paññindriyaṃ.

Pañca balāni: saddhābalaṃ, viriyabalaṃ, satibalaṃ, samādhibalaṃ, paññābalaṃ.

Ru-Yi Translation

Four means to accomplishment: the means consisting in desire, in energy, in consciousness, in investigation.

Five faculties: of faith, energy, mindfulness, concentration, wisdom.

Five powers: of faith, energy, mindfulness, concentration, wisdom.

bodhipakkhiya

"on the side of enlightenment" — bodhi ("awakening") + pakkhiya ("belonging to the side/wing of"). A rare Sutta phrase that becomes the standard umbrella term for these 37 factors in later literature.

iddhipādam.

means to accomplishment — the same four states as the predominants (adhipati) just met, but named iddhipāda specifically when applied to achieving the goal of the Buddha's teaching rather than any goal whatsoever.

The Requisites of Enlightenment — Factors and Path, Reduced to Fourteen

The last two groups — the seven enlightenment factors and the eight path factors — complete the 37, after which the chapter reduces the entire scheme to just fourteen underlying entities and tallies how often each recurs.

Pali Source

Satta bojjhangā: satisambojjhango, dhammavicayasambojjhango, viriyasambojjhango, pītisambojjhango, passaddhisambojjhango, samādhisambojjhango, upekkhāsambojjhango.

Aṭṭha maggangāni: sammādiṭṭhi, sammāsankappo, sammāvācā, sammākammanto, sammā-ājīvo, sammāvāyāmo, sammāsati, sammāsamādhi.

Ru-Yi Translation

Seven enlightenment factors: mindfulness, investigation-of-states, energy, zest, tranquillity, concentration, and equanimity, as factors of enlightenment.

Eight path factors: right view, right intention, right speech, right action, right livelihood, right effort, right mindfulness, right concentration.

Pali Source

Ettha pana cattāro satipaṭṭhānā ti sammāsati ekā va pavuccati. Tathā cattāro sammappadhānā ti ca sammāvāyāmo.

Chando cittam upekkhā ca saddhā-passaddhi-pītiyo
Sammādiṭṭhi ca sankappo vāyāmo viratittayaṃ
Sammāsati samādhī ti cuddas'ete sabhāvato
Sattatiṃsappabhedena sattadhā tattha sangaho.

Ru-Yi Translation

Here, by the four foundations of mindfulness, right mindfulness alone is meant; likewise by the four supreme efforts, right effort alone.

Desire, consciousness, equanimity, faith, tranquillity, zest, right view, intention, effort, the three abstinences, right mindfulness, and concentration: these fourteen, by way of intrinsic nature, compose the sevenfold compendium of the thirty-seven factors.

Pali Source

Sankappa-passaddhi ca pīt'upekkhā
Chando ca cittaṃ viratittayañ ca
Nav'ekaṭṭhānā viriyaṃ nav'aṭṭha
Satī samādhī catu pañca paññā
Saddhā duṭṭhān'uttamasattatiṃsa
Dhammānam eso pavaro vibhāgo.

Sabbe lokuttare honti na vā saṃkappapītiyo
Lokiye pi yathāyogaṃ chabbisuddhippavattiyaṃ.

Ru-Yi Translation

Intention, tranquillity, zest, and equanimity; desire, consciousness, and the three abstinences — these nine occur only once each. Energy occurs nine times; mindfulness, eight; concentration, four; wisdom, five; faith, twice. This is the excellent analysis of these thirty-seven states.

All occur in the supramundane, except that intention and zest sometimes do not. In the mundane too, they occur according to circumstance, in the course of the sixfold purification.

Key vocabulary — for Pāli learners
bojjhaṅgam.

enlightenment factor — bodhi + aṅga. Grouped in the tradition into two balancing triads (investigation/energy/zest against sluggishness; tranquillity/concentration/equanimity against excitation), with mindfulness overseeing both.

dhammavicaya

"investigation of states" — the enlightenment-factor name for wisdom (paññā), insight into mental and material phenomena exactly as they are.

chabbisuddhi

the sixfold purification — the mundane stages preceding the seventh, supramundane purification (taken up fully in Chapter 9); an expansion of the more familiar three-part virtue/concentration/wisdom scheme.

Reading note — Ru-Yi

Thirty-seven factors compress to fourteen entities, and the compression itself tells a story about which quality the tradition considers most load-bearing on the path: energy appears in four different groups under its own name (a supreme effort ×4, a means to accomplishment, a faculty, a power, an enlightenment factor, a path factor) for nine total appearances — more than any other state, including wisdom. Mindfulness at eight is a close second. The arithmetic is quietly making an argument: sustained effort and unbroken mindfulness, not wisdom alone, are what actually show up most often across every traditional description of what enlightenment requires.

The Compendium of the Whole — Aggregates and Sense Bases

The fourth and final compendium gathers every scheme that claims to comprehend the totality of conditioned and unconditioned reality at once — beginning with the five aggregates, the five aggregates of clinging, and the twelve sense bases.

Pali Source

Sabbasangahe pañcakkhandhā: rūpakkhandho, vedanākkhandho, saññākkhandho, sankhārakkhandho, viññāṇakkhandho.

Pañc'upādānakkhandhā: rūpupādānakkhandho, vedanupādānakkhandho, saññupādānakkhandho, sankhārupādānakkhandho, viññāṇupādānakkhandho.

Ru-Yi Translation

In the compendium of the whole, there are five aggregates: the aggregates of materiality, feeling, perception, mental formations, and consciousness.

Five aggregates of clinging: the clinging-aggregates of materiality, feeling, perception, mental formations, and consciousness.

Pali Source

Dvādas'āyatanāni: cakkhāyatanaṃ, sotāyatanaṃ, ghānāyatanaṃ, jivhāyatanaṃ, kāyāyatanaṃ, manāyatanaṃ, rūpāyatanaṃ, saddāyatanaṃ, gandhāyatanaṃ, rasāyatanaṃ, phoṭṭhabbāyatanaṃ, dhammāyatanaṃ.

Ru-Yi Translation

Twelve sense bases: the eye base, ear base, nose base, tongue base, body base, mind base, visible-form base, sound base, smell base, taste base, tangible base, and mental-object base.

Key vocabulary — for Pāli learners
khandham.

aggregate — a group, mass, or heap; the classical Sutta analysis of a living being, cited by the chapter's Guide-layer directly from the Buddha's own words at S.22:48.

upādānakkhandha

aggregate of clinging — not a different set of phenomena from the five aggregates, but the mundane portion of them: the supramundane mental aggregates are excluded, since they can never become an object of greed or wrong view.

manāyatana

mind base — wider than the "mind door" met in Chapter 3: identical to the aggregate of consciousness in its entirety, all 89 cittas without exception.

The Compendium of the Whole — Elements and the Four Noble Truths

The last two schemes: the eighteen elements, which split the mind base into its seven constituent consciousness-elements, and the Four Noble Truths — closing the chapter with a summary verse and its colophon.

Pali Source

Aṭṭhārasa dhātuyo: cakkhudhātu, sotadhātu, ghānadhātu, jivhādhātu, kāyadhātu, rūpadhātu, saddadhātu, gandhadhātu, rasadhātu, phoṭṭhabbadhātu, cakkhuviññāṇadhātu, sotaviññāṇadhātu, ghānaviññāṇadhātu, jivhāviññāṇadhātu, kāyaviññāṇadhātu, manodhātu, dhammadhātu, manoviññāṇadhātu.

Ru-Yi Translation

Eighteen elements: the eye, ear, nose, tongue, and body elements; the visible-form, sound, smell, taste, and tangible elements; the eye-, ear-, nose-, tongue-, and body-consciousness elements; the mind element, the mental-object element, and the mind-consciousness element.

Pali Source

Cattāri ariyasaccāni: dukkhaṃ ariyasaccaṃ, dukkhasamudayaṃ ariyasaccaṃ, dukkhanirodhaṃ ariyasaccaṃ, dukkhanirodhagāminīpaṭipadā ariyasaccaṃ.

Ettha pana cetasika-sukhumarūpa-nibbānavasena ekūnasattati dhammā dhammāyatanaṃ dhammadhātū ti sankhaṃ gacchanti. Manāyatanam eva sattaviññāṇadhātuvasena bhijjati.

Ru-Yi Translation

The Four Noble Truths: the noble truth of suffering, of the origin of suffering, of the cessation of suffering, and of the path leading to the cessation of suffering.

Herein, sixty-nine states — the cetasikas, the subtle matter, and Nibbāna — are reckoned as the mental-object base and the mental-object element. The mind base itself divides, by way of the seven consciousness-elements.

Pali Source

Rūpañ ca vedanā saññā sesā cetasikā tathā
Viññāṇam iti pañc'ete pañcakkhandhā ti bhāsitā.
Pañc'upādānakkhandhā ti tathā tebhūmakā matā
Bhedābhāvena nibbānaṃ khandhasangahanissaṭaṃ.

Dvārālambanabhedena bhavant'āyatanāni ca
Dvārālambataduppannapariyāyena dhātuyo.

Dukkhaṃ tebhūmakaṃ vaṭṭaṃ taṇhāsamudayo bhave
Nirodho nāma nibbānaṃ maggo lokuttaro mato.
Maggayuttā phalā c'eva catusaccavinissaṭā
Iti pañcappabhedena pavutto sabbasangaho.

Iti Abhidhammatthasaṅgahe Samuccayasaṅgahavibhāgo nāma sattamo paricchedo.

Ru-Yi Translation

Matter, feeling, perception, the remaining mental factors, and consciousness — these five, thus, are declared the five aggregates. The same states, confined to the three mundane planes, are held to be the five aggregates of clinging. Having no such differentiation, Nibbāna falls outside the compendium of aggregates.

By the distinction of door and object, the sense bases come to be; by the distinction of door, object, and the consciousness arisen in dependence on them, the elements.

The round, confined to the three planes, is suffering; craving is its origin; cessation is called Nibbāna; the path is held to be supramundane. The cittas conjoined with the path, and the fruits, both fall outside the Four Truths. Thus the compendium of the whole has been told in five ways.

Thus ends, in the Compendium of the Things Contained in the Abhidhamma, the seventh chapter, called the Compendium of Categories.

Key vocabulary — for Pāli learners
dhātuf.

element — dhāreti, "to bear [its own nature]." Splits the mind base and mental-object base of the twelve āyatanas into finer distinctions: seven separate consciousness-elements instead of one undifferentiated "mind."

ariyasaccant.

noble truth — ariya ("noble") + sacca ("truth"); called noble because penetrated by the noble ones, taught by the Noble One, and leading to nobility.

samudayam.

origin — identified here with a single cetasika, craving (taṇhā), itself a mode of greed, appearing in three forms: craving for sense pleasure, for continued existence, and for annihilation.

Reading note — Ru-Yi

The Four Noble Truths, alone among this chapter's five "whole" schemes, explicitly excludes something: the supramundane path cittas and fruition cittas themselves. The path's own eight cetasikas (right view, right speech, and the rest) count as the fourth truth, but the citta that carries them and its other conascent cetasikas do not — nor do the four fruits. This is not a gap in the scheme; the text states outright that this exclusion is deliberate. The four truths are formulated to comprehend what a meditator's insight must investigate and finally transcend, not to catalogue literally everything that exists — which is exactly why this section, alone among the whole chapter, does not close by claiming completeness.

Samuccayasaṅgaha Complete

This closes the translation of the Abhidhammattha Saṅgaha's seventh chapter, Samuccayasaṅgaha ("The Compendium of Categories") — the classical lists of unwholesome states reduced to fourteen cetasikas, the mixed categories of roots, jhāna factors, path factors, faculties, powers, predominants, and nutriments, the thirty-seven requisites of enlightenment reduced to fourteen entities, and the compendium of the whole — aggregates, sense bases, elements, and the Four Noble Truths — closing with the chapter's own colophon. The eighth chapter, Paccayasaṅgaha — the compendium of conditionality, covering both dependent arising and the twenty-four conditional relations of the Paṭṭhāna — continues from here, the next undertaking for this translation.
