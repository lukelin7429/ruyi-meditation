The Thirteen Ascetic Practices

This page carries Ru-Yi's translation of the Visuddhimagga's chapter 2, Dhutaṅganiddesa ("The Description of the Ascetic Practices") — thirteen optional, more austere observances a monk may voluntarily undertake on top of the ordinary training precepts, from restricting himself to rag-robes and a single almsbowl to never lying down at all. This page opens with why the chapter exists at all, and the etymology of each of the thirteen practices in turn.

Why the Ascetic Practices

The chapter opens by explaining its own place in the larger structure of the Path of Purification: virtue was cleansed by qualities like wanting little and contentment in the previous chapter, and the ascetic practices exist to actually cultivate those same qualities. It then lists all thirteen practices at once, and a verse laying out everything the chapter will cover about them — a table of contents in miniature, exactly like the one that opened Sīlaniddesa's own fifth question.

Pali Source

Idāni yehi appicchatāsantuṭṭhitādīhi guṇehi vuttappakārassa sīlassa vodānaṃ hoti, te guṇe sampādetuṃ yasmā samādinnasīlena yoginā dhutaṅgasamādānaṃ kātabbaṃ. Evañhissa appicchatāsantuṭṭhitāsallekhapavivekāpacayavīriyārambhasubharatādiguṇasalilavikkhālitamalaṃ sīlañceva suparisuddhaṃ bhavissati, vatāni ca sampajjissanti. Iti anavajjasīlabbataguṇaparisuddhasabbasamācāro porāṇe ariyavaṃsattaye patiṭṭhāya catutthassa bhāvanārāmatāsaṅkhātassa ariyavaṃsassa adhigamāraho bhavissati. Tasmā dhutaṅgakathaṃ ārabhissāma.

Bhagavatā hi pariccattalokāmisānaṃ kāye ca jīvite ca anapekkhānaṃ anulomapaṭipadaṃyeva ārādhetukāmānaṃ kulaputtānaṃ terasadhutaṅgāni anuññātāni. Seyyathidaṃ — paṃsukūlikaṅgaṃ, tecīvarikaṅgaṃ, piṇḍapātikaṅgaṃ, sapadānacārikaṅgaṃ, ekāsanikaṅgaṃ, pattapiṇḍikaṅgaṃ, khalupacchābhattikaṅgaṃ, āraññikaṅgaṃ, rukkhamūlikaṅgaṃ, abbhokāsikaṅgaṃ, sosānikaṅgaṃ, yathāsanthatikaṅgaṃ, nesajjikaṅganti. Tattha —

Atthato lakkhaṇādīhi, samādānavidhānato;
pabhedato bhedato ca, tassa tassānisaṃsato.

Kusalattikato ceva, dhutādīnaṃ vibhāgato;
samāsabyāsato cāpi, viññātabbo vinicchayo.

Ru-Yi Translation

Now, since it is by such qualities as wanting little and contentment that the cleansing of virtue, already described, comes about, therefore, in order to accomplish those very qualities, the meditator who has undertaken virtue should also undertake the ascetic practices. For thus, his virtue — its stain washed away by the water of such qualities as wanting little, contentment, austerity, seclusion, non-accumulation, the arousing of energy, and being easy to support — will become thoroughly purified, and his observances will succeed. Thus, one whose entire conduct is purified by blameless virtue and by the good qualities of these observances, having become established in the ancient threefold noble lineage, will become worthy of attaining the fourth noble lineage as well — the one reckoned as delight in meditative development. Therefore we shall begin the discourse on the ascetic practices.

For the Blessed One has permitted thirteen ascetic practices to clansmen who have given up worldly gain, who have no concern for body or life, and who wish only to accomplish the practice that conforms to the goal. Namely: the rag-robe practice, the triple-robe practice, the alms-food practice, the uninterrupted-alms-goer's practice, the one-sessioner's practice, the bowl-food-eater's practice, the after-meal-refuser's practice, the forest-dweller's practice, the tree-root-dweller's practice, the open-air-dweller's practice, the charnel-ground-dweller's practice, the any-bed-user's practice, and the sitter's practice. Herein —

By way of meaning, by characteristics and the rest, by way of the manner of undertaking, by way of classification and distinction, and by way of the benefit of each,

and by way of the wholesome triad, by way of the division into the ascetic-practice-observer and the practice itself, and also by way of summary and elaboration — the determination of these practices should be known.

Reading note — Ru-Yi

Notice how explicitly this chapter is framed as a continuation, not a fresh start: it opens by naming the exact qualities (wanting little, contentment) that Sīlaniddesa's closing pages said were needed to cleanse virtue, and presents everything to follow as the practical means for cultivating precisely those qualities. The "fourth noble lineage" mentioned here — delight in meditative development — completes a set: the other three are contentment with whatever robes, almsfood, and lodging one happens to receive. Virtue, cleansed by the ascetic practices, is being positioned as the foundation for the entire remainder of the path, not as an end in itself.

The eight-part outline verse is a direct structural echo of the seven-question matrix that organized the entire previous chapter — Buddhaghosa reaches for the same "state the whole plan before executing it" method at the start of this new topic that he used to open the discussion of virtue itself.

The Thirteen Practices, By Meaning

The chapter's first task is simply to explain where each practice's name comes from — a chain of thirteen brief etymologies, several turning on wordplay that only works in Pāli. Together they read almost as an index to the rest of the chapter: from rag-robes gathered off a rubbish heap to the discipline of never lying down.

Pali Source

Tattha atthato tāva rathikasusānasaṅkārakūṭādīnaṃ yattha katthaci paṃsūnaṃ upari ṭhitattā abbhuggataṭṭhena tesu tesu paṃsukūlamivāti paṃsukūlaṃ, atha vā paṃsu viya kucchitabhāvaṃ ulatīti paṃsukūlaṃ, kucchitabhāvaṃ gacchatīti vuttaṃ hoti. Evaṃ laddhanibbacanassa paṃsukūlassa dhāraṇaṃ paṃsukūlaṃ, taṃ sīlamassāti paṃsukūliko. Paṃsukūlikassa aṅgaṃ paṃsukūlikaṅgaṃ. Aṅganti kāraṇaṃ vuccati. Tasmā yena samādānena so paṃsukūliko hoti, tassetaṃ adhivacananti veditabbaṃ.

Eteneva nayena saṅghāṭiuttarāsaṅgaantaravāsakasaṅkhātaṃ ticīvaraṃ sīlamassāti tecīvariko. Tecīvarikassa aṅgaṃ tecīvarikaṅgaṃ.

Bhikkhāsaṅkhātānaṃ pana āmisapiṇḍānaṃ pātoti piṇḍapāto, parehi dinnānaṃ piṇḍānaṃ patte nipatananti vuttaṃ hoti. Taṃ piṇḍapātaṃ uñchati taṃ taṃ kulaṃ upasaṅkamanto gavesatīti piṇḍapātiko. Piṇḍāya vā patituṃ vatametassāti piṇḍapātī, patitunti carituṃ, piṇḍapātī eva piṇḍapātiko. Piṇḍapātikassa aṅgaṃ piṇḍapātikaṅgaṃ.

Dānaṃ vuccati avakhaṇḍanaṃ, apetaṃ dānatoti apadānaṃ, anavakhaṇḍananti attho. Saha apadānena sapadānaṃ, avakhaṇḍanarahitaṃ anugharanti vuttaṃ hoti. Sapadānaṃ carituṃ idamassa sīlanti sapadānacārī, sapadānacārī eva sapadānacāriko. Tassa aṅgaṃ sapadānacārikaṅgaṃ.

Ekāsane bhojanaṃ ekāsanaṃ, taṃ sīlamassāti ekāsaniko. Tassa aṅgaṃ ekāsanikaṅgaṃ.

Dutiyabhājanassa paṭikkhittattā kevalaṃ ekasmiṃyeva patte piṇḍo pattapiṇḍo. Idāni pattapiṇḍagahaṇe pattapiṇḍasaññaṃ katvā pattapiṇḍo sīlamassāti pattapiṇḍiko. Tassa aṅgaṃ pattapiṇḍikaṅgaṃ.

Khalūti paṭisedhanatthe nipāto. Pavāritena satā pacchā laddhaṃ bhattaṃ pacchābhattaṃ nāma, tassa pacchābhattassa bhojanaṃ pacchābhattabhojanaṃ, tasmiṃ pacchābhattabhojane pacchābhattasaññaṃ katvā pacchābhattaṃ sīlamassāti pacchābhattiko. Na pacchābhattiko khalupacchābhattiko. Samādānavasena paṭikkhittātirittabhojanassetaṃ nāmaṃ. Aṭṭhakathāyaṃ pana vuttaṃ khalūti eko sakuṇo. So mukhena phalaṃ gahetvā tasmiṃ patite puna aññaṃ na khādati. Tādiso ayanti khalupacchābhattiko. Tassa aṅgaṃ khalupacchābhattikaṅgaṃ.

Araññe nivāso sīlamassāti āraññiko. Tassa aṅgaṃ āraññikaṅgaṃ.

Rukkhamūle nivāso rukkhamūlaṃ, taṃ sīlamassāti rukkhamūliko. Rukkhamūlikassa aṅgaṃ rukkhamūlikaṅgaṃ. Abbhokāsikasosānikaṅgesupi eseva nayo.

Yadeva santhataṃ yathāsanthataṃ, idaṃ tuyhaṃ pāpuṇātīti evaṃ paṭhamaṃ uddiṭṭhasenāsanassetaṃ adhivacanaṃ. Tasmiṃ yathāsanthate viharituṃ sīlamassāti yathāsanthatiko. Tassa aṅgaṃ yathāsanthatikaṅgaṃ.

Sayanaṃ paṭikkhipitvā nisajjāya viharituṃ sīlamassāti nesajjiko. Tassa aṅgaṃ nesajjikaṅgaṃ.

Sabbāneva panetāni tena tena samādānena dhutakilesattā dhutassa bhikkhuno aṅgāni, kilesadhunanato vā dhutanti laddhavohāraṃ ñāṇaṃ aṅgaṃ etesanti dhutaṅgāni. Atha vā dhutāni ca tāni paṭipakkhaniddhunanato aṅgāni ca paṭipattiyātipi dhutaṅgāni. Evaṃ tāvettha atthato viññātabbo vinicchayo.

Ru-Yi Translation

Herein, first, by way of meaning: because it lies upon the dust in various places such as a street, a charnel ground, or a rubbish heap, it is called "dust-heap cloth" — as if springing up there like a heap of dust; or else it is so called because, like dust, it reaches a repulsive condition — that is, it comes to a despicable state. The wearing of such dust-heap cloth, its etymology thus obtained, is itself called "dust-heap-wearing"; one whose habit this is, is a "dust-heap-robe wearer." The factor of a dust-heap-robe wearer is "the dust-heap-robe practice." Here, "factor" means "cause"; therefore this term should be understood as a designation for the undertaking by which one becomes a dust-heap-robe wearer.

By this same method: one whose habit is restricting himself to the triple robe — reckoned as the outer double-robe, the upper robe, and the inner robe — is a "triple-robe-user." The factor of a triple-robe-user is "the triple-robe practice."

"Almsfood" is the "falling of lumps" of material food given as alms — that is, the falling into the bowl of the lumps given by others. One who gleans that almsfood, seeking it by approaching one family after another, is an "alms-food-eater." Or else: one whose observance is "to go for the falling of alms" is an "alms-food-goer" — "to fall" here meaning "to walk on almsround"; an "alms-food-goer" is the same as an "alms-food-eater." The factor of an alms-food-eater is "the alms-food practice."

"Dāna" here means "a break, a cutting-off"; "apadāna" is that which is free from such a break — the meaning being, "unbroken." "Sapadāna" is "together with apadāna" — that is, going house after house without a break. One whose habit is to walk for alms without a break, house after house, is an "uninterrupted-alms-goer." His factor is "the uninterrupted-alms-goer's practice."

Eating in a single sitting is "one-sessioning"; one whose habit this is, is a "one-sessioner." His factor is "the one-sessioner's practice."

Since a second vessel is refused, food taken only in a single bowl is called "bowl-food." One who, in taking bowl-food, forms the perception "bowl-food" as such and whose habit is only bowl-food, is a "bowl-food-eater." His factor is "the bowl-food-eater's practice."

"Khalu" is a particle used in the sense of denial. Food received afterward, once one has already indicated he is satisfied, is called "after-meal food"; eating such food is "after-meal eating." One who, recognizing such eating as "after-meal," makes it his habit to refuse it, is an "after-meal-refuser." "Not an after-meal-eater" gives "the khalu-after-meal-refuser" — a name for one who, by his undertaking, has refused any extra food taken after he has already indicated satisfaction. But it is said in the commentary that "khalu" is the name of a certain bird, which, having taken a fruit in its beak, does not eat another if that one falls — "this one is like that bird," hence the name "khalu-after-meal-refuser." His factor is "the after-meal-refuser's practice."

One whose habit is dwelling in the forest is a "forest-dweller." His factor is "the forest-dweller's practice."

Dwelling at the root of a tree is "tree-root-dwelling"; one whose habit this is, is a "tree-root-dweller." The factor of a tree-root-dweller is "the tree-root-dweller's practice." The same method applies to the open-air-dweller's and the charnel-ground-dweller's practices.

Whatever lodging is thus prepared is called "as-allotted" — a designation for whatever lodging is first assigned to a monk with the words, "This falls to you." One whose habit is to dwell in whatever lodging is so allotted, without seeking a better one, is an "any-bed-user." His factor is "the any-bed-user's practice."

One who, refusing to lie down, has as his habit dwelling only in the sitting posture, is a "sitter." His factor is "the sitter's practice."

All of these, by way of their respective undertakings, are the factors of a monk who has "shaken off" defilement, since they shake off defilement; or else, since it is the knowledge that has acquired the name "ascetic" (dhuta) — precisely from its shaking off of defilement — which serves as the factor of these practices, they are called "ascetic-factors" (dhutaṅga). Or else: they are themselves "ascetic," since they shake off the opposing defilements, and they are also "factors" by way of practice — hence, in this sense too, they are called "ascetic-factors." Thus, first of all, the determination here by way of meaning should be understood.

Reading note — Ru-Yi

The khalu-pacchābhattika etymology is a small showcase of the commentarial method in miniature: the text offers two competing derivations back to back without choosing between them — a grammatical one (khalu as a simple negating particle, giving "not an after-meal-eater") and a folk-etymological one from the ancient sub-commentary (khalu as the name of a bird that never returns for a second fruit once the first has dropped). Both explanations point to the same practice; the text is content to preserve both rather than adjudicate, exactly as it did earlier in Sīlaniddesa with competing etymologies for sīla itself.

The double etymology of dhutaṅga at the end is worth sitting with: the compound can mean either "the factor belonging to one who has shaken off defilement" or "a factor that is itself an ascetic practice of shaking off defilement" — a genuine ambiguity in how the whole chapter's key term should be parsed, which the text simply presents as two equally valid readings rather than forcing a single answer.

Key vocabulary — for Pāli learners: the thirteen dhutaṅgas
paṃsukūlika

the rag-robe wearer — paṃsu (dust) + kūla; restricts himself to robes made from discarded cloth rather than cloth given directly by donors.

tecīvarika

the triple-robe user — ti (three) + cīvara (robe); owns no more than the three standard robes.

piṇḍapātika

the alms-food eater — eats only food gathered on almsround, refusing invitations to meals elsewhere.

sapadānacārika

the uninterrupted-alms-goer — sa (with) + apadāna (unbroken) + cārika (walker); calls at every house along an almsround in sequence, not skipping to preferred donors.

ekāsanika

the one-sessioner — eka (one) + āsana (sitting); eats only once, in one sitting, per day.

pattapiṇḍika

the bowl-food eater — eats only from a single bowl, refusing a second dish or vessel.

khalupacchābhattika

the after-meal-refuser — refuses any further food once he has indicated he is satisfied, even if offered more.

āraññika

the forest-dweller — arañña (forest, wilderness); lives away from villages and towns.

rukkhamūlika

the tree-root-dweller — rukkha (tree) + mūla (root); dwells at the foot of a tree rather than in a building.

abbhokāsika

the open-air-dweller — abbhokāsa (the open air); dwells with no roof over him at all.

sosānika

the charnel-ground-dweller — susāna (cremation/charnel ground); dwells among the dead as a support for meditation on death.

yathāsanthatika

the any-bed-user — yathā (as, according to) + santhata (spread, allotted); accepts whatever lodging is assigned without requesting a better one.

nesajjika

the sitter — nisajjā (sitting); never lies down, sleeping (if at all) sitting upright.

What All Thirteen Practices Have in Common

Before treating each practice individually, the chapter closes its general discussion with two final points: the characteristic, function, manifestation, and proximate cause shared by all thirteen practices alike — the exact same fourfold analytical lens applied to virtue itself back in Sīlaniddesa — and a strict hierarchy for who a practice should be undertaken in front of, running from the Buddha himself down to an empty shrine courtyard.

Pali Source

Sabbāneva panetāni samādānacetanālakkhaṇāni. Vuttampi cetaṃ "yo samādiyati, so puggalo. Yena samādiyati, cittacetasikā ete dhammā. Yā samādānacetanā, taṃ dhutaṅgaṃ. Yaṃ paṭikkhipati, taṃ vatthū"ti. Sabbāneva ca loluppaviddhaṃsanarasāni, nilloluppabhāvapaccupaṭṭhānāni appicchatādiariyadhammapadaṭṭhānāni. Evamettha lakkhaṇādīhi veditabbo vinicchayo.

Samādānavidhānatotiādīsu pana pañcasu sabbāneva dhutaṅgāni dharamāne bhagavati bhagavatova santike samādātabbāni. Parinibbute mahāsāvakassa santike. Tasmiṃ asati khīṇāsavassa, anāgāmissa, sakadāgāmissa, sotāpannassa, tipiṭakassa, dvipiṭakassa, ekapiṭakassa, ekasaṅgītikassa, aṭṭhakathācariyassa. Tasmiṃ asati dhutaṅgadharassa, tasmimpi asati cetiyaṅgaṇaṃ sammajjitvā ukkuṭikaṃ nisīditvā sammāsambuddhassa santike vadantena viya samādātabbāni, apica sayampi samādātuṃ vaṭṭati eva. Ettha ca cetiyapabbate dve bhātikattherānaṃ jeṭṭhakabhātu dhutaṅgappicchatāya vatthu kathetabbaṃ. Ayaṃ tāva sādhāraṇakathā.

Ru-Yi Translation

All of these have as their characteristic the volition of undertaking. For this was also said: "Whoever undertakes it — that is the person. That by which one undertakes it — these are the mental states and mental factors. Whatever volition of undertaking there is — that is the ascetic-factor. Whatever one thereby refuses — that is its object." And all of these have as their function the destruction of greed for requisites; their manifestation is the state of being free from such greed; their proximate cause is such noble qualities as wanting little and the rest. Thus the determination here by way of characteristic and the rest should be understood.

But, as to "the manner of undertaking" and the remaining topics: while the Blessed One is alive, all the ascetic practices should be undertaken in his own presence. When he has attained final nibbāna, in the presence of a great disciple. When he is not available, in the presence of one whose taints are destroyed; when he is not available, a non-returner; a once-returner; a stream-enterer; one who has mastered the three piṭakas; one who has mastered two piṭakas; one who has mastered a single piṭaka; one who has preserved a single portion of the recitation; a teacher of the commentaries. When he is not available, in the presence of one who upholds the ascetic practices; and when even he is not available, having swept the courtyard of a shrine and sat down in a crouching position, one should undertake them as though speaking in the very presence of the Fully Enlightened One — though it is indeed also proper simply to undertake them by oneself. And here, the story of the elder brother of the two brother-elders at Cetiya Mountain, concerning his modest disinclination toward the ascetic practices, should be told. This, then, is the discussion common to all thirteen practices.

Reading note — Ru-Yi

The characteristic/function/manifestation/proximate-cause analysis applied here is the identical analytical framework Sīlaniddesa used to define virtue itself, back near the start of this translation — a direct sign that the ascetic practices are being treated with the same rigor as virtue proper, not as some lesser, merely optional add-on. Note too the striking claim buried in the technical definition: "whoever undertakes it — that is the person" is immediately followed by "that by which one undertakes it — these are mental states and mental factors," a very Abhidhamma move of distinguishing the conventional person who appears to be doing something from the actual momentary mental events that constitute the doing.

The hierarchy of nine possible people to undertake a practice in front of — the Buddha, a chief disciple, an arahant, a non-returner, a once-returner, a stream-enterer, then increasingly by scriptural learning alone (three, two, or one piṭaka, or a single recited portion), then a commentary teacher, then simply another ascetic-practice holder — quietly acknowledges that spiritual attainment and textual learning are two different, only loosely correlated qualifications, and ranks the chapter's own subject (learning) below actual attainment at every level. That the whole ceremony can, in the end, simply be dispensed with — sweeping a shrine courtyard and speaking as if to the Buddha, or even undertaking it in complete privacy — suggests the formal witness was never really the point; the volition itself was.

§1 The Rag-Robe Practice

The general discussion finished, the chapter turns to its promised practice-by-practice treatment, and follows the same five-part outline announced in the opening verse for each of the thirteen: the manner of undertaking, the regulation governing its use, its classification into grades, what breaks it, and its benefit. The rag-robe practice comes first, and with it the chapter's longest single piece of description — the roughly twenty-three named varieties of cast-off cloth a monk may gather rather than accept a robe given directly by a householder.

Pali Source

Idāni ekekassa samādānavidhānappabhedabhedānisaṃse vaṇṇayissāma. Paṃsukūlikaṅgaṃ tāva "gahapatidānacīvaraṃ paṭikkhipāmi, paṃsukūlikaṅgaṃ samādiyāmī"ti imesu dvīsu vacanesu aññatarena samādinnaṃ hoti. Idaṃ tāvettha samādānaṃ.

Evaṃ samādinnadhutaṅgena pana tena sosānikaṃ, pāpaṇikaṃ, rathiyacoḷaṃ, saṅkāracoḷaṃ, sotthiyaṃ, nhānacoḷaṃ, titthacoḷaṃ, gatapaccāgataṃ, aggiḍaḍḍhaṃ, gokhāyitaṃ, upacikākhāyitaṃ, undūrakhāyitaṃ, antacchinnaṃ, dasācchinnaṃ, dhajāhaṭaṃ, thūpacīvaraṃ, samaṇacīvaraṃ, ābhisekikaṃ, iddhimayaṃ, panthikaṃ, vātāhaṭaṃ, devadattiyaṃ, sāmuddiyaṃ — etesu aññataraṃ cīvaraṃ gahetvā phāletvā dubbalaṭṭhānaṃ pahāya thiraṭṭhānāni dhovitvā cīvaraṃ katvā porāṇaṃ gahapaticīvaraṃ apanetvā paribhuñjitabbaṃ.

Tattha sosānikanti susāne patitakaṃ. Pāpaṇikanti āpaṇadvāre patitakaṃ. Rathiyacoḷanti puññatthikehi vātapānantarena rathikāya chaḍḍitacoḷakaṃ. Saṅkāracoḷanti saṅkāraṭṭhāne chaḍḍitacoḷakaṃ. Sotthiyanti gabbhamalaṃ puñchitvā chaḍḍitavatthaṃ. Tissāmaccamātā kira satagghanakena vatthena gabbhamalaṃ puñchāpetvā "paṃsukūlikā gaṇhissantī"ti tālaveḷimagge chaḍḍāpesi. Bhikkhū jiṇṇakaṭṭhānatthameva gaṇhanti. Nhānacoḷanti yaṃ bhūtavejjehi sasīsaṃ nhāpitā "kāḷakaṇṇicoḷan"ti chaḍḍetvā gacchanti.

Titthacoḷanti nhānatitthe chaḍḍitapilotikā. Gatapaccāgatanti yaṃ manussā susānaṃ gantvā paccāgatā nhatvā chaḍḍenti. Aggiḍaḍḍhanti agginā ḍaḍḍhappadesaṃ. Tañhi manussā chaḍḍenti. Gokhāyitādīni pākaṭāneva. Tādisānipi hi manussā chaḍḍenti. Dhajāhaṭanti nāvaṃ ārohantā dhajaṃ bandhitvā ārūhanti. Taṃ tesaṃ dassanātikkame gahetuṃ vaṭṭati. Yampi yuddhabhūmiyaṃ dhajaṃ bandhitvā ṭhapitaṃ, taṃ dvinnampi senānaṃ gatakāle gahetuṃ vaṭṭati.

Thūpacīvaranti vammikaṃ parikkhipitvā balikammaṃ kataṃ. Samaṇacīvaranti bhikkhusantakaṃ. Ābhisekikanti rañño abhisekaṭṭhāne chaḍḍitacīvaraṃ. Iddhimayanti ehibhikkhucīvaraṃ. Panthikanti antarāmagge patitakaṃ. Yaṃ pana sāmikānaṃ satisammosena patitaṃ, taṃ thokaṃ rakkhitvā gahetabbaṃ. Vātāhaṭanti vātena paharitvā dūre pātitaṃ, taṃ pana sāmike apassantena gahetuṃ vaṭṭati. Devadattiyanti anuruddhattherassa viya devatāhi dinnakaṃ. Sāmuddiyanti samuddavīcīhi thale ussāritaṃ.

Yaṃ pana "saṅghassa demā"ti dinnaṃ, coḷakabhikkhāya vā caramānehi laddhaṃ, na taṃ paṃsukūlaṃ. Bhikkhudattiyepi yaṃ vassaggena gāhetvā vā dīyati, senāsanacīvaraṃ vā hoti, na taṃ paṃsukūlaṃ. No gāhāpetvā dinnameva paṃsukūlaṃ. Tatrapi yaṃ dāyakehi bhikkhussa pādamūle nikkhittaṃ, tena pana bhikkhunā paṃsukūlikassa hatthe ṭhapetvā dinnaṃ, taṃ ekatosuddhikaṃ nāma. Yaṃ bhikkhuno hatthe ṭhapetvā dinnaṃ, tena pana pādamūle ṭhapitaṃ, tampi ekatosuddhikaṃ. Yaṃ bhikkhunopi pādamūle ṭhapitaṃ, tenāpi tatheva dinnaṃ, taṃ ubhatosuddhikaṃ. Yaṃ hatthe ṭhapetvā laddhaṃ, hattheyeva ṭhapitaṃ, taṃ anukkaṭṭhacīvaraṃ nāma. Iti imaṃ paṃsukūlabhedaṃ ñatvā paṃsukūlikena cīvaraṃ paribhuñjitabbanti idamettha vidhānaṃ.

Ayaṃ pana pabhedo, tayo paṃsukūlikā — ukkaṭṭho, majjhimo, mudūti. Tattha sosānikaṃyeva gaṇhanto ukkaṭṭho hoti. "Pabbajitā gaṇhissantī"ti ṭhapitakaṃ gaṇhanto majjhimo. Pādamūle ṭhapetvā dinnakaṃ gaṇhanto mudūti.

Ru-Yi Translation

Now we shall describe, for each practice individually, the manner of its undertaking, the regulation governing it, its classification, what breaks it, and its benefit. The rag-robe practice, first: it is undertaken by either one of these two forms of words: "I refuse a robe given by a householder; I undertake the rag-robe practice." This, for the present, is its undertaking.

Now, one who has undertaken the ascetic practice in this way should take one of the following kinds of cloth — charnel-ground cloth, shop-door cloth, street cloth, rubbish-heap cloth, birth-cloth, bath cloth, ford cloth, gone-and-returned cloth, fire-burnt cloth, cattle-chewed cloth, termite-eaten cloth, rat-gnawed cloth, hem-cut cloth, border-cut cloth, flag-carried cloth, mound cloth, ascetic's cloth, coronation cloth, supernormally-produced cloth, road cloth, wind-carried cloth, deity-given cloth, or ocean cloth — tear it, give up its weak places, wash the sound places, make it into a robe, and use it, having put away his former householder-given robe.

Herein: "charnel-ground cloth" is what has fallen in a charnel ground. "Shop-door cloth" is what has fallen at the door of a shop. "Street cloth" is a rag thrown away in the street, through a window, by those seeking merit. "Rubbish-heap cloth" is a rag thrown away at a rubbish heap. "Birth-cloth" is a garment thrown away after wiping up the impurity of the womb. It is said that the mother of the minister Tissa, having had the impurity of the womb wiped up with a cloth worth a hundred pieces, had it thrown away on the fan-palm road, thinking, "the rag-robe wearers will take it." Monks take such a thing only for use as a worn rag. "Bath cloth" is what those bathed head to foot by exorcists throw away as they go, calling it "unlucky-star cloth."

"Ford cloth" is a worn wrapper thrown away at a bathing ford. "Gone-and-returned cloth" is what people, having gone to a charnel ground and come back, bathe and then throw away. "Fire-burnt cloth" is a patch of cloth burnt by fire; for people throw such a thing away. "Cattle-chewed" and the rest are self-evident; people throw such things away too. "Flag-carried cloth": those boarding a ship tie a flag and board with it; it is proper to take that once it has passed out of their sight. And whatever flag has been tied and set up on a battlefield, it is proper to take once both armies have departed.

"Mound cloth" is what has been used in performing an offering-rite by wrapping it round an anthill. "Ascetic's cloth" is what belongs to a monk. "Coronation cloth" is a robe thrown away at the site of a king's consecration. "Supernormally-produced cloth" is the robe of one ordained by the words "Come, monk." "Road cloth" is what has fallen along the middle of a journey; but what has fallen through the owners' lapse of mindfulness should be watched over a little before being taken. "Wind-carried cloth" is what has been struck by the wind and dropped far away; it is proper to take that when the owners are not in sight. "Deity-given cloth" is what has been given by deities, as in the case of the Elder Anuruddha. "Ocean cloth" is what has been cast up on dry land by the waves of the ocean.

But what is given saying "we give this to the Sangha," or what is obtained by those going about begging for cloth as alms — that is not rag-robe cloth. Even among what is given to monks, what is obtained by allotment according to seniority, or what belongs to a dwelling — that too is not rag-robe cloth. Only what is given without being solicited is rag-robe cloth. And even among that: what is left by donors at the foot of a monk, and that monk then places it in the rag-robe wearer's hand and gives it — that is called "pure on one side." What is placed in a monk's hand and given, and that monk then places it at the rag-robe wearer's feet — that too is "pure on one side." What is placed at a monk's feet, and given by him again in that same way, placed at the feet — that is "pure on both sides." What is received placed directly in the hand, and given on directly into the hand — that is called "cloth not set down." Having known this classification of rag-robe cloth in this way, the rag-robe wearer should make use of a robe accordingly. This, herein, is the regulation.

This, now, is the classification: there are three kinds of rag-robe wearer — strict, middling, and mild. Herein, one who takes only charnel-ground cloth is strict. One who takes what has been set aside with the thought "renunciants will take this" is middling. One who takes what is given, placed at his feet, is mild.

For any of these, the practice breaks the very moment he consents to accept, by his own preference, a robe given by a householder. This, herein, is the breach.

This, now, is the benefit: because of the saying "going-forth is founded upon rag-robe cloth" (Mahāvagga 128), there is a manner of practice suited to one's reliance; establishment in the first of the noble lineages; freedom from the trouble of guarding one's robe; a livelihood not dependent on others; freedom from fear of thieves; the absence of craving in what one uses; requisites befitting a renunciant; being grounds praised by the Blessed One as "both few and easy to obtain, and blameless" (A.N. 4.27; Itivuttaka 101); being pleasing; the accomplishment of the fruit of wanting little and the rest; the fostering of right practice; and setting an example for people of later times to follow.

For the destruction of Māra's army, the ascetic who wears the rag-robe shines like a warrior-noble armored for battle.

Having given up fine garments fit to wear, such as those of Kāsi — that rag-robe worn by the Teacher of the World — who would not wear it?

Therefore, indeed, let a monk, recollecting his own undertaking, take delight in the rag-robe, fitting for one devoted to the practice of meditation.

This, then, is the account of the rag-robe practice by way of undertaking, regulation, classification, breach, and benefit.

Reading note — Ru-Yi

The catalogue of twenty-three named sources is worth reading as a single deliberate spectrum rather than a random list: it runs from the frankly repellent (birth-cloth, cattle-chewed cloth) through the merely discarded (shop-door cloth, rubbish-heap cloth) to the almost ceremonial (mound cloth from an offering-rite, a robe from a king's own coronation, even a deity-given robe like the Elder Anuruddha's) — establishing that "rag-robe" is a category defined by the manner of acquisition, not by the physical condition or dignity of the cloth itself. A robe touched by royal consecration counts exactly the same as one gnawed by rats, so long as neither was solicited or given directly by a householder.

The fourfold grading of how cloth passes to the rag-robe wearer — "pure on one side," "pure on both sides," and cloth that never touches the ground at all — reveals how precisely this practice polices its own boundary against ordinary alms-receiving. The entire point of the practice is that a robe must never simply be handed to its wearer the way an ordinary gift is; even when a fellow monk acts as intermediary, the text tracks exactly how many times the cloth touched the ground versus a hand, because that detail alone is what keeps the exchange from collapsing back into an ordinary, solicited gift.

§2 The Triple-Robe Practice

The second practice restricts a monk to exactly three robes — no fourth garment at all, apart from one narrow exception. The chapter treats it far more briefly than the rag-robe practice, since most of its content is procedural: how to manage dyeing a robe without ever being, even briefly, without one to wear.

Pali Source

Tadanantaraṃ pana tecīvarikaṅgaṃ "catutthakacīvaraṃ paṭikkhipāmi, tecīvarikaṅgaṃ samādiyāmī"ti imesaṃ aññataravacanena samādinnaṃ hoti.

Tena pana tecīvarikena cīvaradussaṃ labhitvā yāva aphāsukabhāvena kātuṃ vā na sakkoti, vicārakaṃ vā na labhati, sūciādīsu vāssa kiñci na sampajjati, tāva nikkhipitabbaṃ. Nikkhittapaccayā doso natthi. Rajitakālato pana paṭṭhāya nikkhipituṃ na vaṭṭati, "dhutaṅgacoro" nāma hoti. Idamassa vidhānaṃ.

Pabhedato pana ayampi tividho hoti. Tattha ukkaṭṭhena rajanakāle paṭhamaṃ antaravāsakaṃ vā uttarāsaṅgaṃ vā rajitvā taṃ nivāsetvā itaraṃ rajitabbaṃ. Taṃ pārupitvā saṅghāṭi rajitabbā. Saṅghāṭiṃ pana nivāsetuṃ na vaṭṭati. Idamassa gāmantasenāsane vattaṃ. Āraññake pana dve ekato dhovitvā rajituṃ vaṭṭati. Ru-Yi Translation

Next in order, the triple-robe practice is undertaken by either one of these forms of words: "I refuse a fourth robe; I undertake the triple-robe practice."

Now, having obtained robe-cloth, the triple-robe user may keep it aside for only as long as he is unable to work it in comfort, or cannot obtain someone to help him arrange it, or lacks some requisite such as a needle. There is no fault on account of keeping it aside for such a reason. But from the time the dyeing has begun, it is not proper to set it aside; one who does so becomes what is called "a thief of the ascetic practice." This is its regulation.

By way of classification, this practice too is threefold. Herein: for the strict practitioner, at the time of dyeing, he should first dye either the inner robe or the upper robe, wear it, and then dye the other; having put that one on, he should dye the outer double-robe. But it is not proper to wear the double-robe as the inner garment. This is the observance for one dwelling at the edge of a village. For a forest-dweller, however, it is proper to wash and dye two robes together at once; and provided that, on seeing anyone approach, he is able to pull up an ochre cloth and cover himself, he may sit in a place nearby to do so. For the middling practitioner, there is what is called a dyeing-cloth kept in the dyeing-hall; it is proper to do the work of dyeing having put that on, either worn or wrapped. For the mild practitioner, it is proper to do the work of dyeing wearing or wrapping the robes of like-minded fellow monks; a communal seat-cloth kept there is also proper for him, though it is not proper to carry it away. It is also proper, from time to time, to make use of the robes of like-minded fellow monks among themselves. But for one holding the triple-robe ascetic practice, the only fourth item that may occur is a shoulder-cloth — and that only a span in width and no more than three cubits in length.

For all three of these, the practice breaks the very moment he consents to a fourth robe. This, herein, is the breach.

This, now, is the benefit: the triple-robe-holding monk is content with a robe sufficient only for the maintenance of his body. Hence such virtues as these come to be accomplished for him: a going about as though with wings, taking only what he carries; little undertaking of activity; the avoidance of storing up cloth; a light way of living; the abandonment of greed for extra robes; austerity of living through moderation even in what is allowable; and the accomplishment of the fruit of wanting little and the rest.

Having given up craving for extra cloth, the wise one, avoiding all storing-up, knowing the taste of the happiness of contentment, becomes a meditator who wears the triple robe.

Therefore, wishing to fare about happily together with his robe, as a bird with its wings, let the excellent meditator take delight in this restriction to his robes.

This is the account of the triple-robe practice by way of undertaking, regulation, classification, breach, and benefit.

Reading note — Ru-Yi

"A thief of the ascetic practice" (dhutaṅgacoro) is a striking piece of commentarial vocabulary: it names the situation of a monk who has publicly undertaken the triple-robe practice but continues, in secret, to keep a robe set aside past the permitted window — outwardly appearing to hold the practice while inwardly having already broken it. The term appears nowhere in the canonical undertaking formula itself; it belongs to the commentary's own habit of coining sharp, memorable labels for subtle lapses that the bare rule would not otherwise call attention to.

The single narrow exception carved out here — a shoulder-cloth no wider than a span and no longer than three cubits — is a good illustration of how exactingly these rules are specified: rather than leaving "small utility cloth" vague, the text pins its permitted dimensions down precisely, closing off the obvious loophole of calling an ordinary fourth robe a mere accessory.

antaravāsaka

saṅghāṭi

the outer double-robe, worn over the other two in public or in cold weather — the last of the three to be dyed in the strict practitioner's procedure.

rajana

sabhāgabhikkhu

a like-minded fellow monk — sabhāga, "of the same part/portion, congenial"; the source of borrowed robes for the mild-grade practitioner while his own are being dyed.

aṃsakāsāva
