Nutriment and the Elements

This page carries Ru-Yi's translation of the Visuddhimagga's chapter 11, Samādhiniddesa ("The Description of Concentration") — the perception of repulsiveness in nutriment, worked through ten unflinching angles from the journey to the village to the smearing of the mouth, and the determining of the four elements, worked through all thirty-two bodily constituents by the "neither knows the other" formula, thirteen further analytical angles, and a digression on why the elements are called "great" that ends with entire world-systems dissolved by fire, water, and wind. These are the only two of the treatise's forty meditation subjects that reach access concentration alone, never absorption — and with this chapter's own colophon, the entire first part of the Visuddhimagga, its full treatment of virtue and concentration across eleven chapters, is complete.

The Four Nutriments, and Ten Angles of Repulsiveness

The chapter opens with the second of the two remaining "single perception" meditation subjects: the perception of repulsiveness in nutriment. Before turning to ordinary physical food, the text first distinguishes all four kinds of nutriment recognized in the canon — physical food, contact, mental volition, and consciousness itself — each carrying its own specific danger, illustrated by four grim canonical similes: eating one's own child's flesh, a hideless cow beset on every side, a pit of glowing coals, and a hundred spears. Only the first of these four, ordinary chewed and swallowed food, is the actual object of this meditation, to be examined through ten distinct angles of repulsiveness in the sections that follow.

Ru-Yi Translation

Now, next after the immaterial states, the development of the perception of repulsiveness in nutriment — set out as "one perception" — has been reached in its turn. Herein, "that which carries" is called "nutriment." It is fourfold: physical food, contact, mental volition, and consciousness.

What, then, does each of these carry? Physical food carries nutritive essence, at the eighth remove, as a kind of matter. Contact carries the three kinds of feeling. Mental volition carries rebirth-linking into the three kinds of existence. Consciousness carries mentality-materiality at the moment of rebirth-linking.

Among these, physical food carries the danger of attachment. Contact carries the danger of approach. Mental volition carries the danger of rebirth. Consciousness carries the danger of rebirth-linking. These, being thus fraught with danger, should be illustrated: physical food, by the simile of a child's flesh (S.N. 2.63); contact, by the simile of a hideless cow (S.N. 2.63); mental volition, by the simile of a pit of coals (S.N. 2.63); and consciousness, by the simile of a hundred spears (S.N. 2.63). But among these four kinds of nutriment, it is specifically the physical food, distinguished as what is eaten, drunk, chewed, and tasted, that is intended here by the word "nutriment." The perception that arises by grasping the repulsive aspect of that nutriment is "the perception of repulsiveness in nutriment."

One wishing to develop that perception of repulsiveness in nutriment, having learned the meditation subject without missing even a single word of the instruction, should, alone in seclusion, reflect on the repulsiveness of physical food — distinguished as what is eaten, drunk, chewed, and tasted — by ten specific angles: namely, by way of going for it, by way of seeking it, by way of using it, by way of its resting-place, by way of its storage, by way of its being not yet digested, by way of its being digested, by way of its fruit, by way of its outflow, and by way of its smearing.

Reading note — Ru-Yi

The four canonical similes for the four nutriments form a deliberately escalating scale of horror, matched to each nutriment's own characteristic danger: eating a child's flesh (undertaken only from desperate necessity, and only ever in a bare minimum) for ordinary food's danger of attachment; a hideless cow beset by insects from every direction for contact's danger, since contact is precisely the point at which an unprotected mind is exposed on every side; a pit of glowing coals one is pushed toward for volition's danger of driving rebirth; and being stabbed by a hundred spears for consciousness's role in actually establishing a new existence at the moment of rebirth-linking. Only the first of the four receives the extended ten-part treatment that follows — the other three belong more properly to the analysis of dependent origination elsewhere in the treatise.

The instruction to master the meditation subject "without missing even a single word" before attempting it alone is a small but characteristic methodological note running throughout this treatise: technique here is never meant to be reconstructed from a vague impression of instructions half-remembered, but transmitted and retained with word-for-word precision before independent practice begins.

Key vocabulary — for Pāli learners
āhāra

nutriment, that which nourishes or sustains — from ā- + har- (to carry, to bring); the canonical fourfold classification (physical food, contact, mental volition, consciousness) covering everything that sustains a being's continued existence, not physical food alone.

ojā

nutritive essence — the specific subtle quality within physical food identified as what physical food actually "carries" into the body, at what the commentaries reckon as the eighth remove of derived matter.

paṭikkūla

repulsive, disagreeable — paṭi- (against) + kūla (a bank, a shore, by extension a natural inclination); the specific quality of ordinary food this entire meditation subject trains the mind to perceive clearly, cutting through the usual appetite that obscures it.

Repulsive in the Going, Repulsive in the Seeking

The first two angles of repulsiveness concern everything that happens before food is even eaten. "By way of going" traces the monk's entire walk from a beautiful, peaceful forest dwelling — reluctantly abandoned each morning — down an ordinary village road strewn with thorns and animal carcasses, straight toward the sights and smells any ordinary person would go out of their way to avoid. "By way of seeking" continues into the village itself, where the monk, bowl in hand like a pauper, must walk past filth-choked gutters swarming with flies, and receive whatever response the day happens to bring — food, silence, an averted face, or outright insult.

Ru-Yi Translation

Herein, "by way of going": one who has gone forth into a teaching of such great power should, after spending the whole night either reciting the Buddha's word or practicing the ascetic's duties, rise early, perform the duty at the shrine-terrace and the Bodhi-tree terrace, set out drinking water and water for washing, sweep the lodging, attend to his body, and, sitting down, attend to the meditation subject for the time it takes to recite twenty or thirty verses; then, rising, taking bowl and robe, he must leave behind those secluded, cramped, blissfully solitary, shade-and-water-provided, clean, cool, delightful forest groves, and, setting aside all regard for the noble delight in seclusion, go toward the village for the sake of food, facing it the way a jackal faces a charnel ground.

And one going in this way, from the very moment of stepping down from his bed or bench, must tread upon a floor-covering strewn with foot-dust, house-lizards' droppings, and the like. Then, on occasion, he must see an entrance-hall even more repulsive than the inner chamber, spoiled by the droppings of rats and geckos. Then a lower floor even more repulsive than the upper, smeared with the droppings of owls and pigeons. Then, at times, a lodging-ground even more repulsive than the lower floor, soiled by old grass and leaves scattered by the wind, by the urine, feces, saliva, and mucus of sick novices, and, in the rainy season, by mud and standing water. The monastery road is to be seen as more repulsive still than the lodging-ground.

Then, having in due course venerated the Bodhi tree and the shrine, and having stood at the pavilion for walking meditation to look back once more at that shrine like a heap of pearls, that Bodhi tree charming as a peacock's spread tail, and that lodging as glorious as a heavenly mansion, thinking, "I shall be leaving so delightful a place behind me for the sake of food" — having then set out and taken the road to the village, he must see a path strewn with stumps and thorns, or made uneven and broken by the force of rainwater.

Then, having put on his lower robe as though bandaging a wound, tied his waistband as though binding a strip of cloth over a wound, put on his outer robe as though covering over a skeleton, and taken out his bowl as though taking out a medicine-bowl, on reaching the vicinity of the village gate, he must see the carcasses of elephants, horses, cattle, buffaloes, humans, snakes, and dogs. And not only must he see them — he must also endure their smell as it assaults his nose. Then, standing at the village gate, he must scan the village streets to avoid the danger of fierce elephants, horses, and the like.

Thus, all this — from the floor-covering to the many carcasses at the end — is repulsive, and must, for the sake of food, be trodden upon, seen, and smelled. "Ah, how repulsive indeed is this thing called food!" — thus should the repulsiveness by way of going be reflected upon.

How is it "by way of seeking"? Having thus endured the repulsiveness of the going, and entered the village, wrapped in his outer robe like a pauper, bowl in hand, he must walk the village streets from house to house in order. There, in the rainy season, wherever he steps, his feet sink into the mud up to the very flesh of the calf; with one hand he must hold his bowl, with the other he must lift up his robe. In the hot season, he must walk with his body covered by dust and chaff whirled up by gusts of wind. Coming to each house door, he must see, and even tread upon, cesspools and filth-heaps mixed with fish-washings, meat-washings, rice-washings, spittle, mucus, dog- and pig-droppings, teeming with worms and swarming with blue flies — flies which then rise up and settle even on his outer robe, his bowl, and his head.

Even having entered a house, some give and some do not give. Of those who give, some give yesterday's cooked rice, or stale snacks, or rotten porridge-cakes and the like. Of those who do not give, some say, "please pass on, venerable sir," while others, as though not seeing him, remain silent; some turn their face aside; and some address him with harsh words such as, "go away, you shaven-head!" In this way, like a pauper, he must walk for alms through the village and then depart.

Thus, all this — from entering the village to leaving it — the repulsiveness of mud and the rest must, for the sake of food, be trodden upon, seen, and endured. "Ah, how repulsive indeed is this thing called food!" — thus should the repulsiveness by way of seeking be reflected upon.

Reading note — Ru-Yi

The extended before-and-after contrast — a forest dwelling likened to a heavenly mansion, deliberately looked back upon one last time before departure — is a carefully constructed piece of psychological realism: the point is not that alms-going is objectively worse than any other way of obtaining food, but that felt contrast is what actually makes repulsiveness vivid to the meditating mind. Without first calling to mind exactly what is being left behind, the later details of mud, filth, and harsh words would land far less forcefully.

The catalogue of possible responses at each door — food given, food withheld, silence, an averted face, outright insult — is offered without any editorial comment on which response is more common or more likely; the meditation's point is not to complain about how alms-going is treated, but to establish, with total honesty, that a monk's food supply depends entirely on unpredictable outside goodwill, arriving by way of an experience that offers no guarantee of dignity at any single step.

Repulsive in the Eating, in Its Resting-Place, and in Its Storage

The next three angles follow the food past the mouth and into the body itself. "By way of using it" describes the mechanical, thoroughly unglamorous process of chewing — sweat-soaked hands kneading it soft, the lower teeth serving as mortar and the upper as pestle, the whole mass reduced, once thoroughly mashed and mixed with saliva, to something the text compares directly to a dog's vomit in a trough. "By way of its resting-place" locates whatever is swallowed within one of four possible bodily reservoirs — bile, phlegm, pus, or blood — and "by way of its storage" follows it into the stomach itself, described as no better appointed than an unwashed latrine pit, however many decades old the person eating it may be.

Ru-Yi Translation

How is it "by way of using it"? Having sought food in this way, sitting comfortably in a convenient spot outside the village, until he actually lowers his hand into it, it is still possible to invite such a person — a monk of standing, or a person of decent conscience — to share it. But once his hand has actually gone in, wishing to eat, if someone should then say "please take some," he ought to feel ashamed. And as he kneads it with his lowered hand, sweat oozing along the lines of his five fingers moistens and softens even food that was dry and hard.

Then, when a mouthful, its former appearance already spoiled by this mere kneading, is placed in the mouth, the lower teeth perform the function of a mortar, the upper teeth the function of a pestle, and the tongue the function of a hand. There, pounded by the teeth-pestle like a dog's portion in a dog's trough, and turned over by the tongue, it is smeared, at the tip of the tongue, with thin, clear saliva, and, from the middle onward, with thick saliva, and, in places the tooth-stick never reached, with the film that coats the teeth. Thus pulverized and smeared, its distinct color, smell, and character vanishing on the very instant, it comes to a state of utter disgust comparable to a dog's vomit lying in a dog's trough — and yet, precisely because it has now passed out of the range of sight, it must still be swallowed. Thus should the repulsiveness by way of using it be reflected upon.

How is it "by way of its resting-place"? This food, having thus come to be used, and entering within, goes to rest, without exception — even for Buddhas, for Paccekabuddhas, and for a wheel-turning king — in one or another of these four reservoirs: bile, phlegm, pus, or blood. But for people of little merit, all four reservoirs are present at once. Therefore, for one in whom the bile-reservoir predominates, it becomes utterly disgusting, as though smeared with thick, honey-colored oil; for one in whom the phlegm-reservoir predominates, as though smeared with the sap of the nāgabala creeper; for one in whom the pus-reservoir predominates, as though smeared with rotten buttermilk; and for one in whom the blood-reservoir predominates, it becomes utterly disgusting as though smeared with dye. Thus should the repulsiveness by way of its resting-place be reflected upon.

How is it "by way of its storage"? This food, smeared by whichever of these four reservoirs, having entered within the belly, comes to rest neither in a golden vessel, nor in a vessel of jewels, silver, or the like. If it is swallowed by one who is ten years old, it comes to rest in a place resembling an unwashed latrine pit ten years old. If it is swallowed by one who is twenty, thirty, forty, fifty, sixty, seventy, eighty, or ninety years old, or if it is swallowed by one who is a hundred years old, it comes to rest in a place resembling an unwashed latrine pit a hundred years old. Thus should the repulsiveness by way of its storage be reflected upon.

Reading note — Ru-Yi

The observation that even Buddhas and wheel-turning monarchs share this same anatomy of digestion — while ordinary people of lesser merit have all four humoral reservoirs active simultaneously — makes a point that runs throughout this meditation subject: repulsiveness is being presented not as a moral failing peculiar to the unenlightened body, but as a simple, universal biological fact that holds regardless of spiritual attainment, precisely so that it cannot be dismissed as somehow correctable through virtue.

The unwashed-latrine-pit comparison, scaled explicitly to the eater's exact age — a ten-year-old's stomach compared to a ten-year-old pit, a hundred-year-old's to a hundred-year-old pit — is a small but pointed piece of arithmetic: it insists that this is not a passing indignity but the accurate description of an internal environment that has been in continuous, unglamorous operation for a person's entire life, regardless of how carefully that same person washes and adorns the outside of their body every single day.

Repulsive Before and After Digestion, and Repulsive in Its Fruit

The seventh and eighth angles track what happens to food as the body's own inner fire acts upon it. Before digestion, it sits fermenting in the same dark, foul-smelling interior described in the previous section, developing a froth and scum compared directly to what appears on the surface of a cesspool struck by an unseasonal rain. Once properly digested, it becomes nothing more dignified than feces filling the rectum and urine filling the bladder — and its ultimate "fruit," when digestion has gone well, is simply the ordinary substance of hair, nails, teeth, and the rest of the body's parts; when it has gone badly, that same fruit is any of a hundred diseases.

Reading note — Ru-Yi

The "fruit" of digestion is presented with a deliberately double edge that the rest of this meditation subject's relentlessly negative catalogue might otherwise obscure: digestion done well produces nothing less than the hair, nails, teeth, and every other part of the living body — the meditation is not claiming that food is worthless or that its process serves no purpose, only that the process itself, examined honestly at every stage, offers nothing to romanticize even when it succeeds at its actual job.

The unseasonal-rain-on-a-charnel-pit simile for undigested food is chosen for a specific reason beyond mere vividness: an unseasonal rain is itself already an anomaly, an unwelcome disruption to normal expectation, matching the way undigested food sits as an unwanted, festering presence rather than a settled, expected process — the analogy works on the level of felt wrongness, not merely visual resemblance.

Repulsive in Its Outflow, Repulsive in Its Smearing — and This Meditation's Real Benefits

The final two angles complete the survey: food enters the body through a single door but, by the time it has been fully processed, leaves through nine — eyes, ears, nose, mouth, and the rest — carried by four old verses tracking how a person's initial delight in a meal inevitably reverses into disgust within a single day. And even at the moment of eating, it smears the hands, lips, and palate with residues that no amount of washing that same day can fully clear away. Having worked through all ten angles, the chapter turns at last to what this meditation actually accomplishes: not merely disgust for its own sake, but a settling of craving for taste, a full understanding of sensual desire directed at form, the completion of mindfulness of the body, and progress along the path that asubha-perception itself follows.

"Food, drink, what is chewed, and costly fare — one eats it with a whole retinue, but carrying it out, one hides away.
"Food, drink, what is chewed, and costly fare — one eats it with delight, but carrying it out, one is disgusted.

Smeared by this, these openings, even when washed every single day, are never clean, never pleasant. Some of them, once washed, must be washed again with water; some, even after being washed two or three times with cow-dung, clay, or scented powder, only then lose their offensiveness. He repeatedly cultivates, develops, and makes much of that sign. As he does so, the hindrances are suppressed. Because physical food's own inherent nature is profound, the mind becomes concentrated by access concentration alone, without reaching absorption. And here, perception becomes evident by way of grasping the repulsive aspect. Therefore this meditation subject goes by the name "the perception of repulsiveness in nutriment," precisely.

And in a monk devoted to this perception of repulsiveness in nutriment, the mind shrinks back from, curls away from, and turns back from craving for taste. Like one seeking to cross a wilderness, free of intoxication, he partakes of food only for the sake of crossing over suffering — comparable to eating a child's flesh. Then, with little difficulty, by way of the full understanding of physical food, lust for the five cords of sensual pleasure comes to full understanding in him. By way of the full understanding of the five cords of sensual pleasure, he fully understands the aggregate of form. And, by virtue of the repulsiveness inherent in food not yet digested and the rest, his development of mindfulness of the body also comes to fulfillment, and he has entered upon the path in conformity with the perception of foulness. And one who, relying on this practice, is unable to achieve the culmination in deathlessness in this very life, is bound for a good destination.

This is the detailed treatment of the development of the perception of repulsiveness in nutriment.

Reading note — Ru-Yi

The four verses tracking a single meal across two days — welcomed with delight on the first, expelled with disgust on the second — capture something the whole meditation subject has been building toward from its opening lines: repulsiveness is not a fixed, permanent quality food carries at all times, but the plain, undeniable outcome any meal reaches on its own timetable, whether or not anyone bothers to notice. The perception being trained here is simply the willingness to notice a fact that ordinary appetite otherwise successfully hides for exactly as long as the food remains inside the mouth.

The closing paragraph's chain of consequences — from settled craving for taste, to full understanding of sensual desire generally, to full understanding of form itself, to the completion of mindfulness of the body — shows this meditation subject's real ambition: it is not offered as an isolated technique for curbing appetite, but as one entry point into the very same progressive understanding that every other meditation subject in this treatise is ultimately aimed at producing.

The Butcher's Cow: Determining the Four Elements, in Brief

The chapter now turns to its second and final meditation subject, the determining of the four elements — earth, water, fire, and wind — which the tradition transmits in two forms, a concise version found in the Greater Discourse on the Foundations of Mindfulness, and a detailed version found in three further discourses. The concise version rests entirely on a single, famous simile: a skilled butcher who, before cutting up a slaughtered cow, cannot help perceiving it as "a cow," but who, the very moment it lies divided into portions at a crossroads, perceives only "meat" — the perception of "cow" gone the instant the animal's felt unity is broken down into its component parts. Exactly the same shift, the text says, occurs in a meditator who breaks down the felt compactness of "a being" or "a person" into the four bare elements that compose it.

Now, next after the perception of repulsiveness in nutriment, the development of the determining of the four elements — set out as "one determining" — has been reached in its turn. Herein, "determining" is a settling by way of the observation of intrinsic nature; the determining of the four elements is called "the determining of the four elements." Attention to the elements, the meditation subject of the elements, and the determining of the four elements are, in meaning, one and the same. This has come down to us in two forms: in brief, and in detail. In brief, it has come down in the Greater Discourse on the Foundations of Mindfulness. In detail, it has come down in the Greater Discourse on the Simile of the Elephant's Footprint, in the Advice to Rāhula, and in the Exposition of the Elements. For thus it is said —

"Suppose, monks, a skilled butcher, or a butcher's apprentice, having slaughtered a cow, were to sit at a crossroads, having divided it up into portions — just so, monks, a monk reviews this very body, however it is established, however it is disposed, by way of elements: 'in this body there is the earth element, the water element, the fire element, the wind element.'"

Thus, for one of sharp wisdom practicing the elements meditation subject, it has come down in brief in the Greater Discourse on the Foundations of Mindfulness (D.N. 2.378).

Its meaning is this: just as a skillful butcher, or his hired apprentice, fed and paid by him, having slaughtered a cow and cut it through, were to sit having divided it into portions at a crossroads — that is, at the point where the roads going in the four directions meet — just so a monk, by virtue of the body being established in whatever way among the four postures, reviews it "as it is established." And precisely because it is thus established, it is also "as it is disposed" — he reviews the body thus: "in this body there is the earth element … the wind element" — thus he reviews it by way of elements.

What is meant by this? Just as, for the butcher, while he is raising the cow, while he is bringing it to slaughter, while, having brought it, he ties it up and keeps it there, while he is killing it, and even while he sees it slaughtered and dead — the perception "a cow" does not disappear from him for as long as that, but only once he has cut it up and divided it into portions. But for one who, having divided it, sits with it in portions, the perception "a cow" disappears, and the perception "meat" arises. It does not occur to him, "I am selling a cow, these people are carrying off a cow"; rather, it occurs to him only, "I am selling meat, these people too are carrying off meat." Just so, for this monk too, previously, in the time of being a foolish ordinary person — whether as a layman or as one who has gone forth — the perception "a being," or "a person," or "an individual" does not disappear from him for so long as that, but only once he reviews this very body, however it is established, however it is disposed, by way of elements, having broken it down out of its compactness. But for one reviewing it by way of elements, the perception "a being" disappears, and the mind becomes established simply by way of elements. For this reason the Blessed One said, "Suppose, monks, a skillful butcher … were to sit — just so, monks, a monk … the wind element."

Reading note — Ru-Yi

The butcher simile locates the mechanism of this entire meditation subject with real precision: what changes between "cow" and "meat" is not the physical animal itself, which is exactly the same matter before and after the cutting, but purely the perceptual unity imposed on that matter by an observer. The meditation does not ask the practitioner to alter the body in any way, only to perform the same act of perceptual division on it that the butcher performs on the carcass — cutting the felt wholeness of "a being" into four bare, impersonal constituents.

It is worth noting how carefully the simile is chosen to avoid a common misreading: the butcher is never said to feel disgust, cruelty, or triumph over the animal — his perceptual shift from "cow" to "meat" is presented as a plain, almost clerical change of category, exactly the neutral, unemotional character this meditation is meant to cultivate toward one's own body, which is neither denigrated nor mourned but simply seen accurately for what it is.

The Detailed Version: Forty-Two Aspects of the Four Elements

For meditators not quite of the sharpest wisdom, the tradition supplies a far more detailed version of the same meditation, drawn from the Greater Discourse on the Simile of the Elephant's Footprint (and repeated in the Advice to Rāhula and the Exposition of the Elements). Rather than a single global review, each of the four elements is here defined canonically by an explicit list of specific bodily items and functions: twenty items of solidity for the earth element, twelve fluids for the water element, four functions of bodily heat for the fire element, and six kinds of internal wind for the wind element — forty-two aspects in all, each one to be individually recognized as nothing more than an instance of hardness, cohesion, heat, or distension.

Reading note — Ru-Yi

The recurring closing formula, "or whatever else, internally, belonging to oneself," attached to each of the four definitions, is a deliberate safety net built into the canonical wording itself: rather than claiming the preceding lists are exhaustive, each definition explicitly acknowledges its own incompleteness and folds any unlisted bodily phenomenon back into the appropriate element by its characteristic alone — hardness for earth, wetness for water, heat for fire, distension for wind — regardless of whether it happens to have its own name.

The fire element's fourth listed function — properly digesting what is eaten, drunk, chewed, and tasted — quietly connects this meditation subject back to the one immediately preceding it in this same chapter: the perception of repulsiveness in nutriment already walked through digestion in exhaustive, visceral detail, and this definition now simply names the underlying element responsible for that entire process.

Word by Word: Hardness, Cohesion, Heat, and Distension

The chapter now works through the canonical formula's key terms one by one — "internal," "belonging to oneself," "hard," "solid," "clung to" — before defining, in turn, the single defining characteristic behind each of the four elements: hardness for earth, cohesion for water, heat for fire, and distension for wind. Along the way it carefully sorts the wind element's various functions by their specific origin (some from the four standard causes together, breathing alone from mind), before arriving at the chapter's own tally: twenty aspects of earth, twelve of water, four of fire, and six of wind — forty-two in all.

Ru-Yi Translation

Herein, this is the explanation of the words not immediately clear. "Internally, belonging to oneself": both these terms, first of all, are designations for what is one's own. "One's own" means: born within oneself, included within one's own continuity — this is the meaning. And just as, in ordinary usage, talk about women is called "concerning women," so too, because it occurs within oneself, it is called "internal," and because it occurs specifically in dependence on oneself, it is also called "belonging to oneself." "Hard" means stiff. "Solid" means rough. Herein, the first is a statement of characteristic, the second a statement of mode — for the earth element has the characteristic of hardness, and it has a rough mode; therefore it is called "solid." "Clung to" means firmly grasped — grasped firmly in the sense of "I" and "mine," seized and adhered to; this is the meaning. "That is" is a mere particle; its meaning is, "what is that?" Then, in order to show that, it is said, "hair, body-hair," and so on. And here it should be understood that the earth element is specified by twenty aspects, including the brain among them. By the phrase "or whatever else," the earth element is also included among the remaining three categories.

By virtue of flowing, it moves to this or that place — thus it is "water." Having gone, by way of arising from kamma and the rest, into various kinds of water — thus it is "watery." What is that? The characteristic of the water element is cohesion.

By virtue of heating, it is "fire"; by the method already stated, having gone into various fires, it is "fiery." What is that? The characteristic of heat. "That by which": that by which, this fire element having become disturbed, this body is warmed, becoming feverish through such conditions as a one-day fever and the like. "That by which one ages": that by which this body ages, reaching a deterioration of the faculties, an exhaustion of strength, and such conditions as wrinkles and grey hair. "That by which one is consumed": that by which, this element having become disturbed, this body is consumed with burning. And that person, crying out, "I am burning, I am burning!", longs for an application of a hundred-times-clarified ghee, of sandalwood, and the like, and for the wind of a palm-leaf fan. "That by which what is eaten, drunk, chewed, and tasted is properly digested": that by which what is eaten — such as boiled rice — or drunk — such as a beverage — or chewed — such as flour-cakes — or tasted — such as ripe mango or molasses — is properly digested, that is, resolved into taste and the rest; this is the meaning. And here, the first three arise from the fire element itself; the last arises from kamma alone.

By virtue of blowing, it is "wind"; by the method already stated, having gone into various winds, it is "windy." What is that? The characteristic of distension. "Upward-going winds": winds that ascend upward, producing belching, hiccupping, and the like. "Downward-going winds": winds that descend downward, expelling feces, urine, and the like. "Winds in the belly": winds outside the intestines. "Winds in the bowels": winds inside the intestines. "Winds that course through the limbs": winds spread throughout the whole body along the network of channels, coursing through the limbs, producing bending, stretching, and the like. "In-breathing": the wind of breath entering by way of the nose. "Out-breathing": the wind of breath leaving by way of the nose. And here, the first five arise from all four origins; in-breathing and out-breathing arise from mind alone. Everywhere, by the phrase "or whatever else," the water element and the rest are also included among the remaining categories.

Thus, the earth element by twenty aspects, the water element by twelve, the fire element by four, and the wind element by six — the four elements have been detailed by forty-two aspects in all. This, then, is the explanation of the canonical text so far.

Reading note — Ru-Yi

The precise grammatical distinction drawn between "hard" as a statement of characteristic and "solid" as a statement of mode is a small but revealing piece of the treatise's method: even within a single canonical compound, the commentary insists on separating what a thing intrinsically is from how that quality happens to manifest, refusing to treat near-synonyms as simply redundant reinforcement.

The origin-tracing for the wind element's various functions — five kinds from all four standard causes (kamma, mind, temperature, nutriment) together, but breathing specifically from mind alone — is a genuinely important technical point for later practice: it identifies breathing as uniquely responsive to mental state among all the body's winds, which is part of why mindfulness of breathing, treated at length back in chapter eight, can function as a meditation subject in the first place.

Two Monks Reciting: The Concise Method, Matched to the Meditator's Wisdom

The chapter now addresses a practical question: which version of this meditation, concise or detailed, should any given meditator actually use? The answer turns entirely on the sharpness of the individual's wisdom, illustrated by two monks reciting a repetitive canonical text together — the sharp-witted one races ahead using only opening and closing markers, exasperating his slower companion, who in turn insists on spelling out every repeated passage in full, exasperating the first. Neither approach is wrong in itself; each simply suits a different mind. A meditator of sharp wisdom is given the concise method directly: attending to whatever in the body is stiff or rough as the earth element, whatever is cohesive or fluid as the water element, and so on for fire and wind — with an alternative version of the same concise method drawn from Venerable Sāriputta's own words, describing the body as simply "space enclosed by bone, sinew, flesh, and skin."

Ru-Yi Translation

In the method of development here: for a monk of sharp wisdom, grasping the elements in detail — "hair is the earth element, body-hair is the earth element," and so on — would appear tediously drawn out. For him, attending thus — "whatever has the characteristic of hardness, this is the earth element; whatever has the characteristic of cohesion, this is the water element; whatever has the characteristic of heat, this is the fire element; whatever has the characteristic of distension, this is the wind element" — the meditation subject becomes evident. But for one not of the sharpest wisdom, attending in this concise way leaves things dark and unclear; for him, attending in the earlier, detailed way, it becomes evident.

How so? Just as, when two monks are reciting together a text with much repeated material, the monk of sharp wisdom, having spelled out the repeated passage in full once or twice, thereafter goes on reciting using only the opening and closing words as markers. There, the one not of the sharpest wisdom says to him, "why does this recitation not even let you so much as move your lips properly? Reciting like this, when will the text ever become well mastered?" — and he himself recites by spelling out the repeated passage in full every single time it comes up. To him, the other says, "why does this recitation not even let you reach the end? Reciting like this, when will the text ever reach its conclusion?" Just so, for one of sharp wisdom, grasping the elements in detail by way of hair and the rest would appear tediously drawn out; for him, attending concisely by the method, "whatever has the characteristic of hardness, this is the earth element," and so on, the meditation subject becomes evident. For the other, attending in that same concise way leaves things dark and unclear; for him, attending in detail by way of hair and the rest, it becomes evident.

Therefore, one of sharp wisdom wishing to develop this meditation subject should, first, alone in seclusion, having brought to mind his entire physical body, reflect: "whatever in this body is a state of stiffness or of roughness — this is the earth element; whatever is a state of cohesion or of fluidity — this is the water element; whatever is a state of ripening or of heat — this is the fire element; whatever is a state of distension or of movement — this is the wind element." Having thus grasped the elements concisely, he should again and again advert to, attend to, and review them, as mere elements, without a being, without a living entity — "earth element, water element," and so on. For one striving in this way, before long, a concentration merely of access, not reaching absorption, arises, gripped by the wisdom that illuminates the distinction of the elements, because its object is a mere intrinsic-nature phenomenon.

Or alternatively: these four categories were stated by the General of the Dhamma, in order to show that the four great elements lack any living being, thus: "in dependence on bone, in dependence on sinew, in dependence on flesh, in dependence on skin, space thus enclosed simply comes to be reckoned as 'form'" (M.N. 1.306). Having, with the hand of knowledge, repeatedly separated out along each interval between these four categories, whatever among them is a state of stiffness or roughness — "this is the earth element" — having thus grasped the elements by the same method as before, one should again and again advert to, attend to, and review them, as mere elements, without a being, without a living entity — "earth element, water element," and so on. For one striving in this way, before long, a concentration merely of access, not reaching absorption, arises, gripped by the wisdom that illuminates the distinction of the elements, because its object is a mere intrinsic-nature phenomenon. This is the method of development in the concise version of the determining of the four elements.

Reading note — Ru-Yi

The two-monks-reciting simile is a rare moment in this treatise where a purely pedagogical, non-doctrinal scenario is used to make a doctrinal point — and it is worth noticing that neither monk is presented as being in the wrong: each is simply following the method genuinely suited to his own capacity, and each finds the other's method genuinely unworkable for himself. The treatise's evenhandedness here quietly forecloses any reading in which the concise method represents "advanced" practice and the detailed method "beginner" practice — they are matched to different minds, not different stages.

Sāriputta's alternative formula, "space enclosed by bone, sinew, flesh, and skin," achieves something the four-characteristics version does not quite manage on its own: it names the very shape and boundary of the body itself as nothing but empty space held in a particular configuration by four kinds of tissue, making the absence of any solid "self" underlying the visible form of a body vivid in a single, memorable phrase.

For the meditator not of the sharpest wisdom, who needs the full forty-two-aspect version, the chapter lays out four distinct approaches, to be tried in order if an earlier one does not succeed: concise treatment together with the constituents (simply grouping the twenty, twelve, four, and six items directly under their respective elements), detailed treatment of the constituents (drawing on the same sevenfold learning-skill and tenfold attention-skill already given for mindfulness of the body back in chapter eight, now redirected from cultivating foulness to identifying elements), and two further methods — concise and detailed — organized around characteristics rather than constituents. The first of these four methods is given here in full; the second, introduced at this section's close, will occupy the long sequence of body-part similes that follows.

Reading note — Ru-Yi

The instruction to fall back on mindfulness of the body's full sevenfold-and-tenfold apparatus, rather than inventing some new detailed technique from scratch, is a good example of how tightly interconnected this treatise's forty meditation subjects actually are beneath their surface variety: the same careful groundwork — learning the body part by part, reciting it forward and backward, attending to color, shape, direction, location, and delimitation — turns out to serve two entirely different purposes depending only on what the mind is instructed to notice at the very end of the process.

Reading note — Ru-Yi

The recurring "devoid of mutual awareness" refrain is the actual philosophical payload beneath all thirty-two similes, repeated with deliberate, almost liturgical exactness: if hair and scalp, or teeth and jawbone, share no awareness of each other despite their intimate physical contact, then whatever sense of an integrated, self-aware "I" seems to run continuously through the whole body is not something discoverable in any of its actual parts — it is added by the observer, not found in the constituents themselves.

The four similes chosen here span a deliberately wide range of ordinary experience — an anthill's grass, an abandoned village lot, children's toys, a carpenter's joinery — precisely so that the reader cannot dismiss the underlying point as some special property of unusual objects; the same total absence of mutual self-awareness that is obvious and undeniable in a stick-and-fruit toy is being claimed, with equal force, for the living hair on one's own head.

Reading note — Ru-Yi

The skeleton entry is given unusual length and structural detail compared with every other constituent in this sequence — a full chain running from heel to skull and back down again, each link both "supporting" and "supported by" its neighbor — because the skeleton is the one bodily structure whose apparent unity and purposeful engineering might most tempt a meditator to see intentional design or a directing self holding it together. The exhaustive, bone-by-bone repetition of the same "does not know" formula is a deliberate overkill, applied precisely where the intuition of coordinated structure is strongest.

The stack-of-bricks-or-cow-dung-cakes simile makes an argument by analogy that a modern reader might resist at first: a skeleton, unlike a stack of loose bricks, does not visibly wobble or threaten to topple, and its bones are actively held in relation by sinew and muscle rather than simply piled. But the text's point survives this objection — however the bones are held together, no single bone is itself aware of holding up, or of being held up by, the bone beside it; the felt coordination belongs entirely to the observing mind, not to the bones.

Reading note — Ru-Yi

The move from external, visible parts (hair, skin, nails) to internal organs marks a genuine escalation in this sequence's demands on the meditator: it is one thing to accept that a fingernail has no awareness of the finger it grows from, since neither is normally associated with felt selfhood in the first place, but the heart in particular carries an enormous cultural weight as the seat of emotion and identity across many traditions — and the text applies exactly the same mindless-mutual-ignorance formula to it as to a fingernail, without any special exemption.

The mango-pair-on-a-stalk simile for the kidney captures a specific anatomical detail with real care: kidneys are indeed paired and connect via a shared vascular root, and the simile's precision here (a single stalk splitting into two, exactly matching the paired organ) suggests careful anatomical observation underlying what might otherwise look like a purely poetic comparison.

Reading note — Ru-Yi

The bird's-nest-in-an-old-storehouse image for the lungs is chosen with a specific mechanical accuracy worth noting: a nest genuinely does hang suspended within a structure without being fused to it, resting there through a kind of loose containment rather than attachment — a fitting comparison for lungs, which are held in place by the pleural cavity's pressure differentials and connective tissue rather than being anchored directly to the chest wall.

By this point in the sequence, the sheer volume of accumulated similes — cloth, cow-dung, a bird's nest, a blood-filled trough with a headless carcass — has its own cumulative rhetorical effect distinct from any single simile's individual force: the repetition itself becomes part of the meditation's method, training the mind to receive image after unglamorous image about the body's interior without flinching, resisting, or needing each new comparison to be gentler than the last.

Bile, Phlegm, Pus, and Blood — The Water Group Begins

The chapter now turns from the twenty stiff, solid constituents of the earth element to the twelve fluid constituents of the water element, closing each entry with a new formula — no longer "stiff, the earth element," but "having become a fluid, of a cohesive nature, the water element." Bile comes in two forms, unbound bile pervading the whole body like oil soaked through a cake, and bound bile sitting in its own sac like rainwater collected inside a loofah-gourd shell. Phlegm sits in the stomach-lining topped by its own froth, exactly like the scum that forms on a cesspool's surface. Pus has no fixed location at all, appearing wherever a wound happens to occur, like sap oozing from wherever an axe happens to strike a tree. And blood, too, comes in two forms — circulating blood pervading the whole body like bile, and pooled blood settled beneath the liver, moistening the surrounding organs like rainwater soaking clods of earth in a cracked bowl.

Reading note — Ru-Yi

The shift in closing formula — from "stiff, the earth element" to "having become a fluid, of a cohesive nature, the water element" — marks a genuine change in what these twelve constituents are being asked to demonstrate: the earth-group similes established a lack of mutual awareness between solid parts in fixed contact, while the water-group similes must additionally establish that fluidity itself is not "wet" in any way that implies awareness, agency, or containment beyond mere physical cohesion.

Pus's total lack of any fixed anatomical location — the only one of the thirty-two constituents defined this way — is a deliberate departure from the sequence's usual pattern, and the tree-sap simile is chosen precisely because it shares this same contingent, wound-triggered quality: sap has no predetermined channel or reservoir waiting to be filled, appearing only wherever an injury happens to create the occasion for it, exactly as pus appears only wherever the body happens to be struck or wounded.

Reading note — Ru-Yi

With this fourth group, all thirty-two of the treatise's standard bodily constituents have now been walked through by name — the same list, in the same order, that chapter eight's mindfulness of the body used for its own foulness contemplation. Completing Fire and Wind, Then Two Further Methods by Characteristic

Having worked through all thirty-two solid and fluid constituents, the same detailed method is applied to the four fire-element functions (warming, aging, consuming, digesting) and the six wind-element winds (upward-going, downward-going, and the rest), each named, in turn, a separate portion, mindless and empty, of a ripening or distending nature. The chapter then offers two further methods for meditators still struggling: concise treatment by characteristic, which recognizes that even within a single group of constituents — say, the twenty solid ones — all four elements are simultaneously present (hardness, cohesion, heat, and distension all found together in hair itself, for instance), and detailed treatment by characteristic, which applies this same fourfold analysis individually to every single one of the thirty-two constituents in turn, the most exhaustive of all four approaches.

Thirteen Further Angles: Beginning with Word-Meaning and Grouping

Having exhausted four full methods of direct meditative practice, the chapter now adds thirteen further analytical angles for examining the elements — a list that will occupy the remainder of this meditation subject's treatment. The first, the meaning of the terms themselves, derives each element's name from its own defining activity: earth from "being spread out," water from "flowing" or "being drunk," fire from "maturing" or "burning," wind from "blowing." The second, grouping, makes an important reductive point: something as apparently solid and singular as a single hair is, on analysis, nothing more than a bundle of eight co-arising phenomena — color, smell, taste, nutritive essence, and the four elements — such that "hair" exists only as a convenient label for their combination, and dissolves the instant those eight are examined separately.

By Way of Atomization: A Body Like a Conjured Illusion

The next angle imagines the body's earth element pulverized down to its ultimate atomic particles — reduced to a mere bushel of fine dust, which the smaller measure of the water element holds together, the fire element sustains, and the wind element distends, so that it is neither scattered nor dispersed, but instead takes on the countless specific shapes and appearances — male, female, small, large, long, short, firm, rigid — that constitute an apparent living body. This section builds toward one of the chapter's most striking single images: the four elements together, each performing its own distinct function, produce a mechanism that deceives foolish people into seeing a coherent man or woman, "resembling a conjured, illusory form" — the same shape-shifting deception a stage magician performs, except sustained continuously, moment after moment, by the ordinary functioning of the body itself.

By Characteristic, Function, and Manifestation — And by Origin

The chapter now applies its standard fourfold analytical template — characteristic, function, manifestation, proximate cause — to the elements themselves: earth is hard, functions as a foundation, and manifests as receiving; water trickles, functions by augmenting, and manifests as cohesion; fire is hot, functions by ripening, and manifests as continual softening warmth; wind distends, functions by causing movement, and manifests as conveyance. A further angle then sorts all forty-two constituents by their specific origin: four (stomach-contents, feces, pus, urine) from temperature alone, four (tears, sweat, saliva, snot) from temperature and mind together, digestive fire from kamma alone, breathing from mind alone, and everything else from all four causes at once.

Reading note — Ru-Yi

The origin classification quietly reinforces a point this treatise keeps returning to across many of its meditation subjects: bodily phenomena are not a single homogeneous category but arise from genuinely different causal streams, and understanding a specific constituent correctly means knowing which of the four causes actually produced it, not merely cataloguing it as "part of the body." Diverse Yet One: Why the Elements Are Called "Great," Part One

The next angle, diversity-and-unity, opens a substantial digression on why the four elements are traditionally called "the great elements" — five distinct reasons, the first two of which are given here. By "great manifestation," they appear at truly vast scale, in the earth's own measured thickness and in ocean-dwelling beings whose bodies span a hundred leagues. By "similarity to great spirit-beings," they perform three distinct kinds of deception at once: like a magician transforming water into an apparent jewel, they display colors — blue, yellow, red, white — that the colorless elements themselves do not actually possess; like a possessing spirit found neither strictly inside nor outside its host, they occupy no locatable position relative to one another while still depending on each other entirely; and like a demoness concealing her true, terrifying nature behind a beautiful form, they hide their own coarse characteristics behind the pleasing skin, shape, and gestures of an apparent man or woman.

"Two hundred thousand, and four times ten thousand besides — reckoned to this thickness is this earth" —

by the method beginning thus, this great manifestation has already been stated in the exposition of the recollection of the Buddha.

In the clung-to continuum too, they have manifested in great form by way of the bodies of fish, tortoises, devas, and asuras. For this was said: "monks, in the great ocean there exist beings whose bodies are even a hundred leagues long," and so on.

Thus, by this unthinkable manner of location, similar to great spirit-beings, they too are "great elements."

And just as great spirit-beings reckoned as demonesses, concealing their own terrifying nature behind pleasing forms, shapes, and gestures, deceive beings — just so, these elements too, in the bodies of women and men and the like, concealing their own true characteristic — divided into hardness and the rest — behind a pleasing skin-color, a pleasing configuration of limbs, and pleasing gestures of hand, foot, finger, eyebrow, and face, deceive foolish people, not allowing them to see their true nature. Reading note — Ru-Yi

The three spirit-based similes escalate in a deliberate order, each one targeting a different dimension of how the elements mislead ordinary perception: the magician-simile addresses color (blue, yellow, red, white displayed by elements that are themselves colorless), the possessing-spirit simile addresses location (elements with no findable inside-or-outside relationship to each other, yet mutually dependent), and the demoness simile addresses the felt wholeness of a person (a beautiful, coherent-seeming body concealing nothing but hardness, cohesion, heat, and movement underneath). Together they cover perception, position, and personhood — the three levels at which the elements' deceptive appearance actually operates.

It is worth noting that the text does not treat this deception as a moral failing on the elements' part, any more than a magician's trick is a moral failing in the water or the clod being transformed — the "deception" is simply a name for the gap between how matter appears to an untrained observer and what it actually is upon careful analysis, a gap this entire meditation subject exists to close.

Why the Elements Are Called "Great," Part Two: World-Ending Fire, Water, and Wind

The remaining three reasons for calling the elements "great" close this long digression. By "great maintenance-requirement," they demand continual food, clothing, and upkeep merely to persist from one day to the next. By "great transformation," they undergo change at the most extreme possible scales in both the inanimate world and the living body alike — illustrated by a set of cosmological verses describing the destruction of entire world-systems by fire, water, and wind at the end of an aeon, followed immediately by four matching verses on the same three elements' disturbance producing bodily affliction on the vastly smaller scale of a single sick person. Finally, they are simply "great" because grasping them properly demands great effort, and "real" because, unlike the illusions they produce, they genuinely exist. The section then closes by naming the full set of terms — form, great elements, elements, phenomena, impermanent, suffering, not-self — under which the four elements, despite all their internal diversity, ultimately share a single unity.

"By reason of great maintenance-requirement": because they must be maintained by great, continually needed conditions. For these elements, requiring great quantities of food, clothing, and the like to be supplied day after day, have come to be and go on — thus they are "great elements." Or: they are phenomena requiring great maintenance — thus "great elements."

"By reason of great transformation": these elements, both in the non-clung-to and the clung-to continuum, undergo great transformations. Among these, for the non-clung-to continuum, the greatness of transformation becomes evident at the destruction of an aeon. Risen up from the earth, it races as far as the Brahmā-world — the flame of the blazing world, when it is being burned by fire.
A hundred thousand times ten million world-systems dissolve, when the world is destroyed by disturbed water.
A hundred thousand times ten million world-systems are scattered, when the world is destroyed by the eruption of the wind element.
The body becomes stiffened, as if bitten by lockjaw — by the disturbance of the earth element, it becomes just like one afflicted with lockjaw.
The body becomes putrid, as if bitten by a venomous, putrid-mouthed creature — by the disturbance of the water element, it becomes just like one so afflicted.

Reading note — Ru-Yi

The deliberate pairing of cosmic-scale destruction (whole world-systems dissolved by disturbed water, scattered by erupting wind) with individual-scale bodily affliction (a single sick person's body stiffened, putrefying, inflamed, or cut through by the very same three elements' disturbance) is one of this treatise's most striking rhetorical moves: it insists that a case of ordinary illness and the literal end of a world-cycle are instances of exactly the same underlying process, differing only in scale — the same four elements, disturbed, produce catastrophe whether the "world" in question spans a hundred thousand times ten million world-systems or a single suffering body.

The final closing terms — form, great elements, elements, phenomena, impermanent, suffering, not-self — trace a deliberate arc from the most concrete, physical description of the elements toward the three fundamental characteristics (aniccā, dukkhā, anattā) that this entire treatise's second major division, insight (paññā), will eventually take as its central object of contemplation — a quiet signal that this chapter on concentration is already laying groundwork for what comes much later in the work.

Four Quick Angles: Separable Yet Inseparable, Heavy Paired with Heavy

Four further angles follow in brief succession. Separability-and-inseparability notes that the four elements, though always co-arising inseparably within any single material group, remain separable by characteristic alone — hardness is never cohesion, however inseparably the two occur together in the same speck of matter. Similarity-and-dissimilarity pairs earth and water together as the two "heavy" elements, and fire and wind together as the two "light" ones, each pair dissimilar to the other. The distinction between internal and external marks the elements within a living being as serving as supports for consciousness, the senses, and bodily postures, unlike their external counterparts. And classification simply groups elements together according to matching origin — kamma-originated with kamma-originated, mind-originated with mind-originated, and so on.

Each a Condition for the Other Three — Yet None Aware of Any of It

Two further angles close in on the elements' mutual relationships. By way of condition, each element supports the other three through its own distinctive function: earth as their foundation, water as their cohesion, fire as their ripening, and wind as their distension — a fully reciprocal arrangement in which every element depends on the other three even as it supports them. And by way of non-mutual-awareness, the chapter applies, one final time, the same refrain used throughout the body-parts sequence: not one of the four elements knows that it is an element at all, nor that it serves as a condition for the others, nor do the others know that it serves them in this way.

Reading note — Ru-Yi

The conditionality analysis here describes a genuinely circular support structure rather than a linear chain: earth is "held together by" water, "sustained by" fire, and "distended by" wind even as it serves as their own foundation — no single element functions as an unconditioned first cause that the others merely depend on, each is simultaneously supporting and supported.

The non-mutual-awareness angle, applied here to the elements' conditioning relationships specifically rather than to specific body parts, generalizes the lesson of the entire thirty-two-part sequence into its most abstract form: even the deepest structural interdependence among phenomena — mutual, reciprocal, four-way support — carries with it no accompanying awareness whatsoever on the part of anything doing the supporting or being supported.

The Final Angle: A Full Analysis of Conditions, One by One and Two by Two

The last and most technical of the thirteen angles works out, with real combinatorial precision, exactly how the four elements condition one another. Four distinct kinds of condition are distinguished — kamma, mind, nutriment, and temperature — each generating its own matching group of elements outright while merely supporting the others from outside. A short verse then states the pattern with mathematical exactness: taking any one element as the basis, the other three occur, in four possible arrangements; taking any three as the basis, the remaining one occurs, again in four arrangements; and taking any two as the basis, the remaining two occur, in six possible arrangements — twenty-eight relationships in all. The section closes with a final, concrete application: how the elements' interaction actually produces the ordinary mechanics of pressing down, settling, lowering, lifting, and carrying the body through space.

By way of the analysis of conditions: the elements have four conditions — kamma, mind, nutriment, and temperature. Herein, for kamma-originated elements, kamma alone is the condition, not mind and the rest. For mind-originated elements too, mind and the rest alone are conditions, not the others. Mind is a generative condition for mind-originated elements, while for the rest it is a post-nascence condition, a presence condition, and a non-disappearance condition. Nutriment is a generative condition for nutriment-originated elements, while for the rest it is a nutriment condition, a presence condition, and a non-disappearance condition. Temperature is a generative condition for temperature-originated elements, while for the rest it is a presence condition and a non-disappearance condition. A kamma-originated great element is a condition both for kamma-originated great elements and for mind-originated and other great elements. Likewise for a mind-originated and a nutriment-originated great element. A temperature-originated great element is a condition both for temperature-originated great elements and for kamma-originated and other great elements.

For the great elements of the other three continuities, it is a condition only by way of support, presence, and non-disappearance, not by way of distension, nor by way of generation. The fire element, accompanied by the wind element, is a condition for lifting up. The wind element, accompanied by the fire element, is a condition for carrying forward and carrying across. For one who repeatedly adverts to and attends to them, access concentration arises by the very method already stated. Reading note — Ru-Yi

The combinatorial verse's arithmetic is worth checking explicitly, since the treatise clearly intends it to be exact rather than merely evocative: four ways of taking one element as basis for the other three, four ways of taking three as basis for the remaining one, and six ways of taking two as basis for the other two, gives twenty-eight distinct conditioning relationships among just four elements — a small demonstration of how much structural complexity can be generated even from the treatise's most basic four-part classification.

The closing application to ordinary bodily movement — pressing down, settling, lowering, lifting, carrying — quietly grounds this entire, highly abstract conditionality analysis back in something every reader has direct, immediate access to: the simple physical fact of standing up and walking forward, now revealed as a coordinated interplay of exactly these four elements, each contributing its own distinctive function to what feels, from the inside, like a single unified action.

Plunging into Voidness — And "How Should It Be Developed?" at Last Complete

The determining of the four elements closes with its own promised benefit: a monk devoted to this practice plunges into voidness and uproots the perception of a being altogether, becoming one who no longer flinches at wild beasts, spirits, or ogres, unmoved by discontent or delight, elation or dejection, over anything desirable or undesirable — a mind of great wisdom, bound either for the deathless or for a good rebirth. A closing verse calls this practice "the sport of a thousand excellent yogis." With this, the chapter also closes something much larger than itself: the entire extended treatment of "how should concentration be developed?" — a question first posed at the very start of this treatise's whole section on concentration — is now, after eight chapters of specific technique, finally and completely answered, distinguishing throughout only two kinds of concentration: access, reached by ten of the forty meditation subjects, and absorption, reached by all the rest.

Reading note — Ru-Yi

The determining of the four elements is deliberately placed last among the samatha meditation subjects treated across chapters four through eleven for a structural reason the text makes explicit here: it alone is said to "plunge into voidness" and uproot the perception of a being outright, a description that reads less like the modest access-concentration attainment it technically produces and more like a direct preview of the insight (vipassanā) practice this whole treatise's next major division will take up — a fitting bridge from the "how to concentrate" section to the "how to see clearly" section that follows.

The final clarification — that only two kinds of concentration, access and absorption, cover everything discussed across this entire lengthy section — is a useful reminder of how economical the treatise's underlying framework actually is. Eight chapters, forty meditation subjects, dozens of similes and technical distinctions have all, in the end, been in service of producing just these two outcomes, differing only in whether the one-pointedness achieved reaches full absorption or stops short of it.

The Five Benefits of Concentration, and the Whole Chapter List

Stepping back from every specific meditation subject treated across eight chapters, the treatise now answers, once and for all, the question of what concentration's development is actually good for: five distinct benefits, each tied to a different kind of practitioner and each backed by its own canonical citation. For an arahant, absorption yields simply a happy dwelling in the present life. For a trainee or ordinary worldling emerging from concentration to practice insight, it yields insight's own necessary foundation. For one aspiring to supernormal powers, it yields direct knowledge. For one whose jhāna does not deteriorate, it yields a superior mode of rebirth. And for the noble ones who enter the attainment of cessation itself, becoming without consciousness for seven full days, it yields the benefit of cessation — the deepest of the five. The chapter then closes with its own colophon, and, in a rare moment of explicit self-reference, lists the titles of all eleven chapters just completed before announcing that the entire first part of the Visuddhimagga — its whole treatment of virtue and concentration — is now finished.

As for what was asked, "what is the benefit of the development of concentration?" — herein, the benefit of the development of concentration is fivefold, beginning with dwelling happily in the present life. For thus: those arahants whose taints are destroyed, who, having attained absorption with one-pointed mind, develop concentration thinking, "I shall dwell happily throughout the day" — for them, the development of absorption concentration has the benefit of dwelling happily in the present life. For this reason the Blessed One said, "Cunda, these are not called effacements in the noble one's discipline; these are called dwellings happily in the present life in the noble one's discipline" (M.N. 1.82).

For trainees and ordinary worldlings, emerging from the attainment, developing concentration thinking, "with a concentrated mind, I shall practice insight" — because it serves as the proximate cause for insight, both the development of absorption concentration, and, by the method of gaining an opening within confinement, the development of access concentration, have the benefit of insight. For this reason the Blessed One said, "Monks, develop concentration. A concentrated monk, monks, understands things as they really are" (S.N. 3.5).

But for those who, having produced the eight attainments and entered the jhāna serving as the basis for direct knowledge, emerging from that attainment, aspire to and produce the direct knowledges — such as, by the stated method, "having been one, he becomes many" — for them, because it serves, on each and every occasion, as the proximate cause for direct knowledge, the development of absorption concentration has the benefit of direct knowledge. For this reason the Blessed One said: "He directs his mind to whatever state is to be realized by direct knowledge, and he attains the capacity of being a witness to it, on each and every occasion, provided the conditions obtain" (M.N. 3.158; A.N. 3.102).

For those worldlings whose jhāna has not deteriorated — whether hoping for rebirth in the Brahmā-world, thinking, "I shall be reborn in the Brahmā-world," or not so hoping, but simply not falling away from concentration — for them, because it brings about a superior mode of existence, the development of absorption concentration has the benefit of a superior mode of existence. For this reason the Blessed One said: "Having developed the first jhāna to a limited degree, where are they reborn? They are reborn in the company of the deities of Brahmā's retinue," and so on (Vibh. 1024).

But the development of access concentration too brings about a superior mode of existence within the sense-sphere's good destinations.

But for those noble ones who, having produced the eight attainments, enter upon the attainment of cessation, and, being without consciousness for seven days, reach, in this very life, cessation — Nibbāna — developing concentration thinking, "I shall dwell in happiness" — for them, the development of absorption concentration has the benefit of cessation. For this reason it is said: "the wisdom that is mastery, by way of sixteen kinds of exercise of knowledge and nine kinds of exercise of concentration, is the knowledge of the attainment of cessation" (Paṭis. i.34).

Reading note — Ru-Yi

The five benefits are arranged in a deliberate order of increasing depth rather than a simple list: a happy dwelling in the present life is available to one who is already fully liberated and needs nothing further from concentration; insight and direct knowledge serve those still on the path; a superior rebirth serves those who cannot avoid falling short of full liberation in this very life; and cessation — the total, if temporary, suspension of consciousness itself for seven days — is reserved for noble ones who have already mastered every other listed benefit and now seek something beyond all of them.

The chapter's own numbered list of eleven completed chapters, followed by the simple announcement that the Visuddhimagga's first part is now finished, is a genuine milestone within this whole translation project as well: chapters four through eleven have together walked through the entire technical apparatus of samatha meditation — nine full chapters covering the earth kasiṇa's foundational method, all thirty-nine remaining meditation subjects by cross-reference or in full detail, and the two subjects (food's repulsiveness, the four elements) that only ever reach access concentration. What follows next belongs to an entirely different division of the path: paññā, wisdom, beginning with the analysis of the aggregates.
