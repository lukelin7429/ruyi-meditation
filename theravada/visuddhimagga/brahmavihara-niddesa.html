Chapter 9 · Brahmavihāraniddesa · Complete

The Four Divine Abidings

This page carries Ru-Yi's translation of the Visuddhimagga's chapter 9, Brahmavihāraniddesa ("The Description of the Divine Abidings") — the four measureless states of loving-kindness, compassion, sympathetic joy, and equanimity. The chapter is dominated by its treatment of loving-kindness: a full nine-step sequence of specific remedies for resentment, eight jātaka stories illustrating the Buddha's own past patience, the precise technical definition of "the breaking of boundaries," and a complete numerical enumeration of loving-kindness's 528 possible attainments — before closing with compassion, sympathetic joy, and equanimity, and a miscellaneous discussion of etymology, near and far enemies, and the perfections these four abidings are said to fulfill.

The Danger of Hatred, and Whom Not to Begin With

The chapter opens by turning from the six (and now expanded, eight) recollections of the previous chapters to the four divine abidings — loving-kindness, compassion, sympathetic joy, and equanimity — beginning with loving-kindness itself. Before any technique is given, the beginner is told first to see clearly the danger in hatred and the benefit in patience, since nothing is ever actually abandoned whose danger has not been seen, and nothing is ever actually gained whose benefit has not been known. The chapter then gives a second, equally important piece of preliminary orientation: four specific kinds of person toward whom loving-kindness must never be developed first, and two further categories — a singled-out person of the opposite sex, and anyone already dead — toward whom it must never be developed at all, each rule illustrated by a small cautionary story.

Ru-Yi Translation

Next after the meditation subjects consisting in recollection, of the four divine abidings set out — loving-kindness, compassion, sympathetic joy, and equanimity — a beginning meditator wishing first to develop loving-kindness should, having taken up the meditation subject with all hindrances to it cut off, eaten his meal and dispelled the drowsiness that follows eating, sit down comfortably on a well-prepared seat in a secluded spot, and first of all consider the danger in hatred and the benefit in patience.

Why? Because by this development hatred is to be abandoned and patience is to be attained. And it is not possible to abandon anything whose danger has not been seen, nor to attain anything whose benefit has not been known. Therefore the danger in hatred should be seen by way of such passages as: "Friends, one who hates, overcome by hatred, his mind consumed by it, even kills a living being" (A.N. 3.72).

"Patience is the highest austerity, endurance; the Buddhas say Nibbāna is the highest" (D.N. 2.90; Dhp. 184).
"The power of patience is the power of an army — that one I call a brahmin." (Dhp. 399; Sn. 628)
The benefit in patience should be understood by way of such passages as "there is nothing better than patience" (S.N. 1.250).

Then, in order to disengage the mind from hatred whose danger has thus been seen, and in order to yoke it to patience whose benefit has thus been known, the development of loving-kindness should be begun. And one beginning it should first of all know the division of persons: "toward these persons loving-kindness should not be developed first; toward these it should never be developed at all."

For this loving-kindness should not be developed first toward these four: an unloved person, a very dear friend, a neutral person, and a hostile person. It should not be developed, singling out one individual, toward someone of the opposite sex. It should never be developed toward a person who has died. For what reason should it not be developed first toward the unloved and the rest? Placing an unloved person in the position of a loved one is wearisome. Placing a very dear friend in the position of a neutral person is wearisome, and even when only a slight suffering has arisen for him, one comes to be as if on the verge of weeping. Placing a neutral person in the position of one respected or one loved is wearisome. In one recollecting a hostile person, anger arises. For these reasons it should not be developed first toward the unloved and the rest.

But in one developing it, singling out a specific individual, toward someone of the opposite sex, lust arises. It is told that a certain minister's son asked a resident elder monk, "Venerable sir, toward whom should loving-kindness be developed?" The elder said, "Toward a loved person." Now his own wife was dear to him. Developing loving-kindness toward her, he fought a battle against the wall the entire night. Therefore it should not be developed, singling out one individual, toward someone of the opposite sex.

But one developing it toward a person who has died attains neither absorption nor access. It is told that a certain young monk took up loving-kindness with reference to his teacher. His loving-kindness would not proceed. He went to a great elder and said, "Venerable sir, the jhāna-attainment of loving-kindness is familiar to me, and yet I am unable to enter it — what, I wonder, could be the reason?" The elder said, "Friend, search for the sign." Searching, he learned that his teacher had died. Then, taking up loving-kindness with reference to another, he attained absorption. Therefore it should never be developed toward one who has died.

Reading note — Ru-Yi

The requirement to see hatred's danger and patience's benefit before attempting any technique is a small but pointed piece of method: the text treats abandoning a mental state as strictly impossible without first perceiving why it is worth abandoning, refusing to let technique substitute for genuine conviction. The same principle will recur throughout this chapter's long sequence of remedies for resentment — each remedy works by supplying a fresh perception, not merely a fresh instruction.

The two cautionary stories are worth reading as a matched pair. The minister's son fails because he takes "a loved person" too literally and too narrowly — his own wife is, in one sense, exactly what was asked for, yet the instruction was never meant to license singling out an object of sexual attachment for sustained, exclusive attention. The young monk fails for the opposite reason: his devotion to his teacher is unimpeachable, but the person no longer exists to be reached by loving-kindness's characteristic function of wishing someone well, so the practice simply produces no absorption at all — not a corruption of the meditation, but an absence of any object for it to take hold of.

Key vocabulary — for Pāli learners
brahmavihāra

divine abiding, sublime state — brahma (divine, of Brahmā) + vihāra (dwelling, abiding); the collective name for the four meditations this chapter treats: mettā, karuṇā, muditā, and upekkhā.

khanti

patience, forbearance, endurance — named here as the direct positive counterpart to dosa (hatred), the quality to be attained precisely as hatred is abandoned.

liṅgavisabhāga

difference of sex — liṅga (mark, sign, here: sex/gender) + visabhāga (dissimilar, of a different kind); the technical term for the specific hazard of developing loving-kindness toward one singled-out person of the opposite sex.

Beginning with Oneself

Before extending loving-kindness to anyone else at all, the meditator must first direct it toward himself, repeating some such formula as "may I be happy and free from suffering." This raises an apparent difficulty: the canonical formulas actually quoted for the practice — from the Vibhaṅga, the Paṭisambhidāmagga, and the Mettā Sutta itself — all describe extending loving-kindness to other beings, with no mention of oneself at all. The chapter resolves this by distinguishing two different functions loving-kindness can serve: taking oneself as a witness to establish the wish for well-being as something universally shared, as opposed to actually attaining absorption with a being as the object.

Ru-Yi Translation

But first of all it should be developed repeatedly toward oneself alone, thus: "may I be happy and free from suffering," or "may I be free from enmity, free from affliction, free from trouble, and may I keep myself happy."

This being so, one might object that this contradicts what is said in the Vibhaṅga —

"And how, monks, does one dwell pervading one direction with a mind endowed with loving-kindness? Just as one might feel loving-kindness on seeing a single dear and beloved person, so does one pervade all beings with loving-kindness" (Vibh. 643) —

and what is said in the Paṭisambhidāmagga —

"By which five modes is the unspecified-pervasion deliverance of mind through loving-kindness to be developed? May all beings be free from enmity, free from affliction, free from trouble, and may they keep themselves happy. May all breathing things … may all creatures … may all persons … may all those included in a person's own existence be free from enmity, free from affliction, free from trouble, and may they keep themselves happy" (Paṭis. ii.22) —

and what is said in the Mettā Sutta —

"May they be happy and secure, may all beings be happy at heart" (Khp. 9.3; Sn. 145) —

since in none of these is the development toward oneself mentioned at all. But this is no contradiction. Why not? Because those passages are stated with reference to absorption, while this one is stated with reference to serving as a witness.

For even if one develops loving-kindness toward oneself by the method beginning "may I be happy," for a hundred years or for a thousand years, no absorption will ever arise for him from it. But in one developing "may I be happy," the desire for the welfare and happiness of other beings arises by taking oneself as a witness, in this way: "just as I wish for happiness and am averse to suffering, wish to live and do not wish to die, so do other beings also." The Blessed One himself, speaking thus, showed just this method —

"Having traveled all directions with the mind, one finds nowhere anyone dearer than oneself; just so is one's own self dear to others as well — therefore let one who loves himself not harm another." (S.N. 1.119; Ud. 41)

Reading note — Ru-Yi

The distinction drawn here — between developing loving-kindness "with reference to absorption" (appanāvasena) and "with reference to serving as a witness" (sakkhibhāvavasena) — is doing real conceptual work: it separates loving-kindness's absorption-producing function, which requires another being as its object, from an entirely different, purely inferential function, in which contemplating one's own wish for happiness supplies the premise for a universal generalization about all beings. One could develop the self-directed formula for a millennium, the text insists, and never once touch jhāna by it — its whole value lies in the logical move it enables, not in any meditative attainment of its own.

The Udāna verse the chapter reaches for to justify this move is notable for what it does not say: it never instructs anyone to cultivate self-love, only observes, as a plain fact discoverable by anyone who searches "all directions" for someone dearer to themselves than themselves, that no such person is ever found — and draws from that single shared fact of self-regard the ethical conclusion that one who takes his own self-love seriously should extend the same regard to others rather than harm them.

Key vocabulary — for Pāli learners
sakkhibhāva

the state of being a witness — sakkhi (witness) + bhāva (state); the technical role assigned to self-directed loving-kindness, which serves only to establish a universal premise, never to produce absorption on its own.

appanā

absorption — the state of full jhānic immersion in a meditation object, contrasted here with the merely preparatory, witness-establishing function of the self-directed formula.

anodhisopharaṇā

unspecified pervasion — an- (not) + odhiso (by way of specific limits/categories) + pharaṇā (pervading); loving-kindness extended without singling out any category of being, the first of the three pervasion-modes this chapter will later enumerate in full.

Extending Outward: Teacher, Friend, Neutral, Enemy

Having taken himself as witness, the meditator extends loving-kindness next to a respected, dearly regarded teacher or preceptor-figure, recollecting the specific reasons that make such a person loved and worthy of esteem. But he is not to stop there: wishing to accomplish the complete "breaking of boundaries" between categories of person, he must go on, in strict sequence, to a very dear friend, then a neutral person, and only then to a hostile person, softening the mind at each stage before moving to the next — except that a meditator who genuinely has no enemy, or whose disposition is so great that no one's ill-treatment ever registers as hostility, need not manufacture the exercise artificially.

Ru-Yi Translation

Therefore, having first pervaded himself with loving-kindness for the purpose of serving as a witness, next, for the purpose of causing happiness to proceed, one should recollect that person — whether a loved, dear, respected, esteemed teacher, or one like a teacher, or a preceptor, or one like a preceptor — recollecting the reasons that make him dear and beloved, such as his generosity and kind speech, and the reasons that make him worthy of respect and esteem, such as his virtue and learning, and develop loving-kindness by the method beginning, "may this good person be happy and free from suffering."

And though absorption may certainly be reached with regard to a person of this kind, this monk, not content with that much alone but wishing to accomplish the breaking of boundaries, should next develop loving-kindness toward a very dear friend, then from the very dear friend toward a neutral person, and from the neutral person toward a hostile person. And in developing it, having made the mind soft and workable in each single category, it should be directed on to the very next one in turn.

But one who has no hostile person at all, or one who, by the nature of a great being, does not even conceive the perception "enemy" toward another even when that other does him harm, should not artificially undertake the exercise of thinking, "my mind of loving-kindness has become workable toward the neutral person — now let me direct it toward the hostile person." It is with reference to one who does have such a person that it was said, "loving-kindness should be developed from the neutral person toward the hostile person."

Reading note — Ru-Yi

The four-stage sequence — self, respected teacher, very dear friend, neutral person, hostile person — is not arbitrary ordering for its own sake; each transition is calibrated to be only slightly harder than the one before it, so that the "soft and workable" quality of mind achieved at each stage becomes the actual working material carried forward into the next, harder case. This is the same graduated-difficulty logic that will reappear, in reverse and even more explicitly, in the compassion and sympathetic-joy sections later in the chapter.

The exception for the meditator who has no enemy at all is a small but telling piece of realism: the text refuses to let the four-category schema become more important than the actual state of the practitioner's mind, explicitly warning against manufacturing an artificial "hostile person" category merely to complete a formal exercise when no such category genuinely exists for him.

Key vocabulary — for Pāli learners
sīmāsambheda

the breaking of boundaries — sīmā (boundary, limit) + sambheda (breaking down, merging); the technical goal of this entire graduated sequence, reached when the meditator's mind no longer distinguishes between loved, neutral, and hostile persons.

koṭṭhāsa

a portion, category, division — here referring to each of the successive categories of person (dear friend, neutral, enemy) through which the practice is carried in strict sequence.

mahāpurisajātika

of the nature of a great being — mahāpurisa (great person) + jātika (having the nature of); describes an exceptional disposition so free of ill will that even genuine harm from another never registers as enmity at all.

The First Remedy for Resentment: The Saw-Simile Admonition

When directing the mind at last toward the hostile person, anger is likely to arise through remembering the wrongs he has done. The chapter now begins its long sequence of specific remedies for such resentment — a sequence that will run, one remedy tried after another only if the previous one fails, all the way to paragraph 251. The first remedy is simply to return again and again to loving-kindness for the earlier, easier categories of person, using each fresh attainment of it as a tool to dispel the anger; if even that fails, the meditator is to strive repeatedly by way of the Buddha's famous "saw-simile" admonition and a cluster of supporting verses on the special ugliness anger produces in the person who harbors it.

Ru-Yi Translation

But if, in directing his mind toward the hostile person, resentment arises in him through remembering some wrong that person has done, then he should, again and again, attain loving-kindness toward whichever of the earlier persons is available, and emerging from it again and again, dispel the resentment by suffusing that person with loving-kindness. If, even striving in this way, it does not go out, then —

By following the saw-simile admonition and the rest, one should strive again and again for the abandoning of resentment.

And this is done precisely by admonishing oneself in this manner: "Hey, you angry man, was it not said by the Blessed One —

'Monks, even if bandits were to sever you limb from limb with a two-handled saw, whoever there defiles his mind at that would not, on that account, be a follower of my teaching' (M.N. 1.232), and
'Worse than the angered is the one who angers back; not angering back at the angered, one wins a battle hard to win. One practices for the good of both — one's own and the other's — knowing the other provoked, one who remains mindful is at peace' (S.N. 1.188), and
'Monks, there are these seven things, dear to an enemy and productive of an enemy's purpose, that come upon an angry woman or man. What seven? Here, monks, an enemy wishes this of an enemy: "Oh, may this one be ugly!" Why is that? An enemy does not delight in an enemy's good looks. Monks, this person, angry, overcome by anger, mastered by anger — though he be well-bathed, well-anointed, his hair and beard trimmed, clothed in white — is nevertheless ugly, being overcome by anger. This, monks, is the first thing, dear to an enemy and productive of an enemy's purpose, that comes upon an angry woman or man. Again, monks, an enemy wishes this of an enemy: "Oh, may this one sleep badly!" … "may he not prosper!" … "may he not be wealthy!" … "may he not be famous!" … "may he not have friends!" … "may he not, at the breaking up of the body after death, arise in a good destination, in a heavenly world!" Why is that? An enemy does not delight in an enemy's going to a good destination. Monks, this person, angry, overcome by anger, mastered by anger, conducts himself badly in body, badly in speech, badly in mind. Having conducted himself badly in body, speech, and mind, at the breaking up of the body after death, overcome by anger, he arises in a state of misery, a bad destination, a fallen state, in hell' (A.N. 7.64), and
'Monks, suppose there were a firebrand from a funeral pyre, burning at both ends and smeared with dung in the middle — it would serve neither as timber in the village nor as timber in the forest. Just such a person, monks, is what I call this one.'

Now then, being angry in this way, you will not even be a follower of the Blessed One's teaching; angering back, you will be worse than the angry person himself and will not win the battle hard to win; you will be doing to yourself the very things an enemy would wish done to you; and you will become just like that firebrand from a funeral pyre."

Reading note — Ru-Yi

The saw-simile admonition, quoted here from the Kakacūpama Sutta, is the tradition's single most extreme standard for the abandonment of hatred: even the literal, limb-by-limb dismemberment by bandits is offered not as a hypothetical worth flinching from but as the outer boundary against which any lesser provocation — a hostile word, a past wrong recalled — can be measured and, by comparison, dissolved into insignificance.

The "seven things dear to an enemy" passage works by a distinctive rhetorical strategy: rather than warning that anger harms the angry person in some abstract sense, it specifies exactly what an actual enemy would wish upon you, and then demonstrates that anger itself — quite apart from anything the enemy does — independently manufactures every one of those seven wished-for miseries, so that the angry person becomes, in effect, his own enemy's most effective agent.

sapatta

an enemy, a rival — sa- (with) + patti (falling, i.e. one who falls upon, opposes); the term used throughout the seven-things sutta for the hostile party whose wishes anger is shown, ironically, to fulfill on its own.

chavālāta

The Second Remedy: Recollecting What Is Already Purified

If the saw-simile admonition still fails to settle the resentment, the chapter's second remedy directs attention away from whatever in the hostile person continues to provoke and toward whatever quality of his — bodily, verbal, or mental — is already good, since recollecting it brings a calm confidence that displaces the grudge. Three illustrative types of person are given, one whose bodily conduct alone is composed, one whose speech alone is gracious, and one whose mind alone is settled, each recognizable by specific, concrete behavior — and, in the rare case where a person shows not even one of the three, compassion is prescribed instead of continued searching.

Ru-Yi Translation

If, one striving and making effort in this way, that resentment settles down, this is good. But if it does not settle down, then whatever quality of that person is already settled and purified, and which, when recollected, brings confidence, that quality should be recollected, and the grudge dispelled by it.

For in one person, only bodily conduct is settled. His settled state is known to everyone by the many observances and practices he performs, while his verbal and mental conduct remain unsettled. Without thinking about those, only the settledness of his bodily conduct should be recollected.

In another, only verbal conduct is settled. His settled state is known to everyone: by nature he is skilled at hospitality, gracious, pleasant to converse with, welcoming, cheerful of countenance, one who speaks first, who delivers the Dhamma with a sweet voice, who tells a Dhamma-talk with well-rounded words and phrasing — while his bodily and mental conduct remain unsettled. Without thinking about those, only the settledness of his verbal conduct should be recollected.

In another, only mental conduct is settled, and his settled state becomes evident to everyone on such occasions as venerating a shrine. For one whose mind is unsettled does not venerate a shrine, or the Bodhi tree, or the elders, with full respect, and sits at the hall for hearing the Dhamma with a distracted mind or nodding off. But one whose mind is settled venerates with full commitment, ears inclined, giving it his whole attention, listening to the Dhamma while producing confidence of mind by body or by speech. Thus in another, only mental conduct is settled, while bodily and verbal conduct remain unsettled; without thinking about those, only the settledness of his mental conduct should be recollected.

But in another, not even one of these three qualities is settled. Toward such a person compassion should be established: "although this one now walks about in the human world, yet within a matter of days he will become one who fills up the eight great hells and their sixteen surrounding hells." For a grudge settles down also on account of compassion.

In yet another, all three of these qualities are settled; toward him, whichever quality one wishes may be recollected. For in a person of that kind the development of loving-kindness is not difficult at all.

And to make this matter clear, the Āghātapaṭivinaya Sutta of the Book of Fives should be given in full: "Friends, there are these five means of dispelling a grudge, by which a grudge that has arisen in a monk should be entirely dispelled" (A.N. 5.162).

Reading note — Ru-Yi

The threefold analysis into bodily, verbal, and mental conduct is a psychologically realistic acknowledgment that almost no one is uniformly admirable across all three doors of action — a person who is a model of courteous speech may be inwardly distracted, one whose mind is exemplary in devotion may be graceless in manner — and rather than demanding an idealized whole person as the object of recollection, the method simply asks the meditator to find whichever single facet is genuinely, verifiably good and rest attention there.

The fallback to compassion, for the rare person in whom none of the three doors shows any settledness, is a quiet but significant pivot: rather than abandoning the effort or forcing a false perception of virtue, the text redirects the very same energy that was seeking a reason to feel goodwill toward a reason to feel pity instead — anger and compassion being treated here as mutually exclusive states, so that establishing one is itself sufficient to dispel the other.

kāruñña

compassion, pity — the fallback response prescribed when a hostile person shows no settledness in any of the three doors, redirecting the mind's energy from resentment to pity for that person's grim future.

The Third Remedy: A Verse-Sequence of Self-Admonition

If recollecting the hostile person's purified qualities still does not settle the grudge, the chapter supplies a third remedy: a chain of eleven closely reasoned verses of direct self-admonition, each one attacking the logic of anger from a different angle — the domain in which harm actually occurred, the value of what anger destroys, the futility of matching another's fault with one's own, the way anger fulfills exactly what an antagonist wanted, and, in its final and most philosophically pointed turns, the momentariness of all phenomena, which dissolves the very continuity an angry mind assumes it is holding someone accountable to.

But if, admonishing himself in this way, the resentment still does not settle down, then he should admonish himself thus —

"If your enemy has done you harm within his own domain, why do you wish to do harm, within a domain not his own, to your own mind?
"Having abandoned your very helpful circle of relatives, their faces streaked with tears, why do you not abandon anger, that greatly harmful enemy?
"Anger is the very uprooting of the virtues you now protect — yet you coddle this thing called anger; who is as much a fool as you?
"Angry, you say, 'an ignoble deed has been done by another' — but why is it that you yourself wish to do that very same kind of deed?
"If another, wishing to provoke you to anger, has done something displeasing to you, why, by giving rise to anger, do you fulfill exactly his wish?
"Whether, being angry, you will cause him suffering or not, you are certainly afflicting yourself, right now, with the suffering of anger.
"If your enemies have mounted the harmful path of anger, why, by getting angry yourself, do you follow their very example?
"Cut off that very fault in dependence on which the enemy has done you this displeasure — why torment yourself over what is beside the point?
"Since phenomena are momentary, the very aggregates by which that displeasing deed was done to you have already ceased — toward whom, then, are you now angry?
"Whoever causes suffering to whom — without that other one's presence, to whom could he cause it at all? Since you yourself are also a cause of that suffering, why are you angry with him?"

Reading note — Ru-Yi

The sequence is carefully escalating in sophistication. The opening verses work at the level of ordinary practical reasoning any angry person could follow — the harm was done to your body, not your mind, so why import it there; anger destroys more than the enemy ever could. But the closing pair shifts register entirely, invoking the Abhidhamma doctrine of momentariness (khaṇikatā): the very aggregates that performed the offending act have already ceased to exist by the time resentment is nursed, so that a grudge held toward "the person who wronged me" is, strictly analyzed, resentment toward someone who, in the technical sense that matters to this system, no longer exists.

The final verse's claim that "you yourself are also a cause of that suffering" is easy to misread as blaming the victim; what it actually targets is the assumption, built into anger itself, that suffering has a single, isolable author who can be justly punished by resentment — a view the text treats as simply false to how conditioned phenomena actually arise, regardless of how morally responsible the other party's original action may have been.

Key vocabulary — for Pāli learners
visaya

domain, sphere, range — used in the opening verse to distinguish the "domain" in which an enemy's blow actually lands (the body) from the "domain" into which anger imports the harm without warrant (one's own mind).

khaṇikatā

momentariness — khaṇika (momentary) + -tā (abstract-noun suffix); the doctrine, invoked in the sequence's penultimate verse, that all conditioned phenomena, including the aggregates that performed a wrong, cease virtually as soon as they arise.

jaḷa

a fool, a dullard — the word the third verse uses for anyone who "coddles" (upaḷāleti) anger even while professing to guard the very virtues anger destroys at the root.

The Fourth Remedy: Reflecting on Ownership of Deeds

If even the verse-sequence fails to settle the grudge, the fourth remedy turns to the doctrine of kamma-ownership, applied first to oneself and then, in an exact mirror, to the offending party. Neither reflection denies that a wrong was done; both simply observe that the one who acts in anger — whichever of the two that is — is the one who will inherit the results of that very kamma, an act incapable of leading to any of the great attainments and capable only of leading downward. The section closes with a Dhammapada verse comparing an angry retaliator to a man who flings fine dust into the wind, only to have it blow back into his own face.

Herein, his own should first be reflected on thus: "Hey, being angry at him, what will you accomplish? Will this very deed, whose root is hatred, not lead to your own harm? For you are the owner of your deeds, the heir of your deeds, deeds are your origin, your kinsman, your refuge; whatever deed you do, of that you will be the heir. And this deed of yours is not capable of accomplishing full self-awakening, nor private awakening, nor the level of a disciple, nor any of the various attainments — becoming a Brahmā, a Sakka, a wheel-turning monarch, a regional king, and the rest — but is a deed that will only cause you to fall away from the Dispensation into the state of one who feeds on scraps and the like, and into the various distinct sufferings of hell and the rest. Having thus reflected on the ownership of his own deeds, he should also reflect thus on the other's: "And what will this one accomplish by being angry at you? Will this not lead only to his own harm? For this venerable one too is the owner of his deeds, the heir of his deeds … whatever deed he will do, of that he will be the heir. And this deed of his is not capable of accomplishing full self-awakening, nor private awakening, nor the level of a disciple, nor any of the various attainments — becoming a Brahmā, a Sakka, a wheel-turning monarch, a regional king, and the rest — but is a deed that will only cause him to fall away from the Dispensation into the state of one who feeds on scraps and the like, and into the various distinct sufferings of hell and the rest. This one, in doing this, is like a man who, standing against the wind and wishing to scatter dust upon another, only scatters it upon himself. For this was said by the Blessed One —

"'Whoever offends an inoffensive man, one who is pure and without blemish — that very evil returns upon the fool himself, like fine dust thrown against the wind.'" (Dhp. 125; Sn. 667)

Reading note — Ru-Yi

The perfectly symmetrical structure — the identical reflection run first on oneself, then word-for-word on the offender — is itself the argument: kamma-ownership is not a special doctrine invoked selectively to excuse one party, but a completely impartial law that applies with equal force to whichever person, in this exchange, chooses to act out of hatred. Anger, on this reading, is not primarily a moral failing to be judged but a straightforwardly bad investment, incapable of purchasing any of the great attainments the text lists and capable only of purchasing a fall.

The dust-thrown-against-the-wind image, unlike the earlier saw-simile and firebrand images, locates the harm precisely in the retaliatory act itself rather than in anger generally — it is the man who "stands against the wind," that is, who actively responds to an offense with hostility of his own, who receives the dust back in his own face, leaving open the possibility that the one who was originally wronged, if he does not retaliate, need not share that fate at all.

vighāsāda

one who feeds on scraps, leftovers — vighāsa (leftover food, refuse) + ada (eater); one of the degraded states this deed of hatred is said to lead to, after falling away from the Buddha's Dispensation.

paṭivāta

The Fifth Remedy: The Teacher's Own Past Conduct

If reflecting on kamma-ownership still fails, the next remedy calls to mind eight episodes from the Buddha's own past lives as a bodhisatta — some as a human king, one as a newborn infant, others as an elephant, a monkey, and three separate nāga-kings — in every one of which he endured torture, mutilation, or betrayal without a single moment of hatred toward his tormentor. The argument closing the section is direct: for a follower of a Teacher who set this standard across countless lifetimes to indulge a grudge over any lesser provocation is called simply unfitting.

"Āsīsetheva puriso, na nibbindeyya paṇḍito;
Passāmi vohamattānaṃ, yathā icchiṃ tathā ahū"ti. (jā. 1.1.51)

Khantivādījātake dummedhena kāsiraññā "kiṃvādī tvaṃ samaṇā"ti puṭṭho "khantivādī nāmāha"nti vutte sakaṇṭakāhi kasāhi tāḷetvā hatthapādesu chijjamānesu kopamattampi nākāsi.

Anacchariyañcetaṃ, yaṃ mahallako pabbajjūpagato evaṃ kareyya. Cūḷadhammapālajātake pana uttānaseyyakopi samāno –

"Candanarasānulittā, bāhā chijjanti dhammapālassa;
Dāyādassa pathabyā, pāṇā me deva rujjhantī"ti. (jā. 1.5.49)

Evaṃ vippalapamānāya mātuyā pitarā mahāpatāpena nāma raññā vaṃsakaḷīresu viya catūsu hatthapādesu chedāpitesu tāvatāpi santuṭṭhiṃ anāpajjitvā sīsamassa chindathāti āṇatte "ayaṃ dāni te cittapariggaṇhanakālo, idāni ambho dhammapāla, sīsacchedāṇāpake pitari, sīsacchedake purise, paridevamānāya mātari, attani cāti imesu catūsu samacitto hohī"ti daḷhaṃ samādānamadhiṭṭhāya paduṭṭhākāramattampi nākāsi.

Idañcāpi anacchariyameva, yaṃ manussabhūto evamakāsi. Tiracchānabhūtopi pana chaddanto nāma vāraṇo hutvā visappitena sallena nābhiyaṃ viddhopi tāva anatthakārimhi luddake cittaṃ nappadūsesi. Yathāha –

"Samappito puthusallena nāgo,
Aduṭṭhacitto luddakaṃ ajjhabhāsi;
Kimatthayaṃ kissa vā samma hetu,
Mamaṃ vadhī kassa vāyaṃ payogo"ti. (jā. 1.16.124)

Evaṃ vatvā ca kāsirañño mahesiyā tava dantānamatthāya pesitomhi bhadanteti vutte tassā manorathaṃ pūrento chabbaṇṇarasminiccharaṇasamujjalitacārusobhe attano dante chetvā adāsi.

Mahākapi hutvā attanāyeva pabbatapapātato uddharitena purisena –

"Bhakkho ayaṃ manussānaṃ, yathevaññe vane migā;
Yaṃnūnimaṃ vadhitvāna, chāto khādeyya vānaraṃ.
"Āhitova gamissāmi, maṃsamādāya sambalaṃ;
Kantāraṃ nittharissāmi, pātheyyaṃ me bhavissatī"ti. (jā. 1.16.205-206) —

Evaṃ cintetvā silaṃ ukkhipitvā matthake sampadālite assupuṇṇehi nettehi taṃ purisaṃ udikkhamāno –

"Mā ayyosi me bhadante, tvaṃ nāmetādisaṃ kari;
Tvaṃ khosi nāma dīghāvu, aññaṃ vāretumarahasī"ti. (jā. 1.16.209) —

Vatvā tasmiṃ purise cittaṃ appadūsetvā attano ca dukkhaṃ acintetvā tameva purisaṃ khemantabhūmiṃ sampāpesi.

Bhūridatto nāma nāgarājā hutvā uposathaṅgāni adhiṭṭhāya vammikamuddhani sayamāno kappuṭṭhānaggisadisena osadhena sakalasarīre siñciyamānopi peḷāya pakkhipitvā sakalajambudīpe kīḷāpiyamānopi tasmiṃ brāhmaṇe manopadosamattampi na akāsi. Yathāha –

"Peḷāya pakkhipantepi, maddantepi ca pāṇinā;
Alampāne na kuppāmi, sīlakhaṇḍabhayā mamā"ti. (cariyā. 2.16)

Campeyyopi nāgarājā hutvā ahituṇḍikena viheṭhiyamāno manopadosamattampi nuppādesi. Yathāha –

"Tadāpi maṃ dhammacāriṃ, upavutthauposathaṃ;
Ahituṇḍiko gahetvāna, rājadvāramhi kīḷati.
"Yaṃ so vaṇṇaṃ cintayati, nīlaṃ pītaṃ va lohitaṃ;
Tassa cittānuvattanto, homi cintitasannibho.
"Thalaṃ kareyyaṃ udakaṃ, udakampi thalaṃ kare;
Yadihaṃ tassa kuppeyyaṃ, khaṇena chārikaṃ kare.
"Yadi cittavasī hessaṃ, parihāyissāmi sīlato;
Sīlena parihīnassa, uttamattho na sijjhatī"ti. (cariyā. 2.21-24)

Saṅkhapālanāgarājā hutvā tikhiṇāhi sattīhi aṭṭhasu ṭhānesu ovijjhitvā pahāramukhehi sakaṇṭakā latāyo pavesetvā nāsāya daḷhaṃ rajjuṃ pakkhipitvā soḷasahi bhojaputtehi kājenādāya vayhamāno dharaṇītale ghaṃsiyamānasarīro mahantaṃ dukkhaṃ paccanubhonto kujjhitvā olokitamatteneva sabbe bhojaputte bhasmaṃ kātuṃ samatthopi samāno cakkhuṃ ummīletvā paduṭṭhākāramattampi na akāsi. Yathāha —

"Cātuddasiṃ pañcadasiñcaḷāra,
Uposathaṃ niccamupāvasāmi;
Athāgamuṃ soḷasa bhojaputtā,
Rajjuṃ gahetvāna daḷhañca pāsaṃ.
"Bhetvāna nāsaṃ atikassa rajjuṃ,
Nayiṃsu maṃ samparigayha luddā;
Etādisaṃ dukkhamahaṃ titikkhaṃ,
Uposathaṃ appaṭikopayanto"ti. (jā. 2.17.180-181)

Na kevalañca etāneva, aññānipi mātuposakajātakādīsu anekāni acchariyāni akāsi. Tassa te idāni sabbaññutaṃ pattaṃ sadevaloke kenaci appaṭisamakhantiguṇaṃ taṃ bhagavantaṃ satthāraṃ apadisato paṭighacittaṃ nāma uppādetuṃ ativiya ayuttaṃ appatirūpanti.

Ru-Yi Translation

But if, even reflecting thus on the ownership of deeds, it does not settle down, then the virtues of the Teacher's own past conduct should be recollected.

Here is the method of that reflection: "Hey, renunciant — was it not the case that your Teacher, even before his full awakening, while still unenlightened, a bodhisatta fulfilling the perfections for four incalculable aeons and a hundred thousand aeons besides, on this occasion and that, never once corrupted his mind even toward murderous enemies? For instance: in the Sīlava Jātaka, when a rival king — brought in by a wicked minister whom his own queen had corrupted — was seizing a kingdom three hundred leagues wide, he would not even let the ministers who had risen up to stop it so much as touch a weapon. Again, buried up to the neck together with a thousand ministers in a raw burial-pit, without producing so much as a trace of mental corruption, and having regained his life by an act of resolve with the help of jackals who had come to feed on corpses and scattered away the earth, he then, by a spirit's power, descended into his own royal bedchamber and, seeing his enemy lying there upon the royal couch, felt no anger at all — instead, having exchanged mutual oaths with him, he placed him in the position of a friend, and said —

"One should indeed go on hoping; the wise should never grow weary; I see it for myself: just as I wished, so it has come to be." (Jā. 1.1.51)

In the Khantivādī Jātaka, when the foolish King of Kāsi asked, "What is your teaching, ascetic?" and was told, "I am called a teacher of patience," he did not produce even a trace of anger while being flogged with thorn-studded whips and having his hands and feet cut off.

And this is not so remarkable, that one who had gone forth in mature adulthood should act thus. But in the Cūḷadhammapāla Jātaka, even while still an infant lying on his back —

"Smeared with sandalwood paste, the arms of Dhammapāla, heir to the earth, are being cut off; my very life-breath, O king, is being torn away." (Jā. 1.5.49)

While his mother thus cried out in lament, his father — a king named Mahāpatāpa — had all four of his limbs cut off like sections of bamboo, and even then, not yet satisfied, commanded, "Cut off his head!" — and the infant Bodhisatta thought: "Now is the time for you to take hold of your own mind. Now, dear Dhammapāla, be of even mind toward these four: the father who orders the beheading, the man who performs the beheading, the mother who is lamenting, and yourself." Having firmly undertaken this resolve, he did not produce even a trace of a corrupted state.

And this too is not so remarkable, that a human being should act in this way. But even as an animal — being the elephant named Chaddanta — though pierced in the navel by a barbed arrow, he did not corrupt his mind even toward the hunter who had done him this harm. As he said —

"Struck through by a broad-bladed shaft, / the elephant, his mind undefiled, spoke to the hunter: / 'For what purpose, or for what reason, friend, / have you slain me — whose undertaking is this?'" (Jā. 1.16.124)

And having spoken thus, and being told, "Venerable one, I have been sent for the sake of your tusks, by the chief queen of the King of Kāsi," fulfilling her wish, he cut off, with his own effort, his own tusks — radiant with the beautiful glow of six-colored rays streaming from them — and gave them to the man.

Being the Great Monkey, he himself pulled a man up out of a mountain chasm; and that man thought —

"This one is food for men, just like any other creature of the forest; what if, having killed it, I, being hungry, were to eat this monkey. I will go bearing it as a burden, taking its flesh as provisions; I shall cross the wilderness, and it shall be my food for the journey." —

Having thought this, he lifted a stone and struck the monkey's head, splitting it open; and the monkey, with eyes full of tears, looking upon that man, said —

"May you not, good sir, be one who has done such a deed to me; may you indeed live long — you should rather be one who restrains others from doing likewise." (Jā. 1.16.209) —

Having said this, without corrupting his mind toward that man, and without regarding his own agony, he brought that very man safely to a place of security.

Being the nāga-king named Bhūridatta, having undertaken the uposatha factors and lying atop an anthill, even while his entire body was being doused with a caustic preparation like the fire that arises at the destruction of an aeon, and even while being placed in a basket and made to perform tricks throughout the whole of Jambudīpa, he did not produce even a trace of mental corruption toward that brahmin. As he said —

"Even while he put me in the basket, and even while he crushed me with his hand, / I did not grow angry — for fear of breaking my virtue." (Cariyā. 2.16)

Being also the nāga-king Campeyya, though tormented by a snake-charmer, he did not produce even a trace of mental corruption. As he said —

"At that time too, though I was one practicing the Dhamma, having undertaken the uposatha, / the snake-charmer seized me and made me perform at the king's gate.
"Whatever color he thought of — blue, yellow, or red — / following his thought, I became just as he had thought.
"I could have turned dry land into water, and water into dry land; / had I grown angry at him, in an instant I could have reduced him to ashes.
"But if I were to come under the sway of that anger, I would fall away from virtue; / and for one fallen away from virtue, the highest goal is never accomplished." (Cariyā. 2.21–24)

Being the nāga-king Saṅkhapāla, pierced with sharp spears in eight places, with thorny creepers pushed into the mouths of the wounds, a stout rope thrust through his nose, and being carried slung on a pole by sixteen village youths, his body dragged along the ground, undergoing great suffering — though capable, merely by looking upon them in anger, of reducing all those village youths to ashes — he did not, even opening his eyes, produce so much as a trace of a corrupted state. As he said —

"On the fourteenth and the fifteenth days, O noble one, / I constantly kept the uposatha; / then sixteen village youths came, / taking a rope and a firm snare.
"Having pierced my nose and drawn the rope through, / the cruel ones led me away, seizing me on every side; / such suffering as this I endured, / never letting my uposatha vow be disturbed." (Jā. 2.17.180–181)

And not only these — in the Mātuposaka Jātaka and others too, he performed many other wonders of this same kind. For you now, professing allegiance to that Blessed One, the Teacher who has attained omniscience, whose quality of patience is matched by no one in the world together with its gods, to give rise to so much as a thought of resentment is exceedingly improper and unfitting."

Reading note — Ru-Yi

The eight episodes are arranged in a deliberate ascending order of difficulty rather than simple chronology: from a mature king's forbearance, to an infant's (where the ordinary excuse of adult self-control is explicitly ruled out — "this is not so remarkable, that one who had gone forth in mature adulthood should act thus," the text says, precisely to set up the greater wonder of an infant doing the same), to three separate animal incarnations, ending with the nāga Saṅkhapāla, dragged through the dirt on a rope through his own pierced nose, who is said to have had the actual destructive power to incinerate his tormentors and chose, moment by moment, not to use it.

The Mahākapi story stands apart from the others in a way worth noticing: it is the only one of the eight in which the aggressor is not a stranger or captor but someone the Bodhisatta had just personally rescued, making the betrayal itself, rather than the physical suffering, the point of the test — and the monkey's dying words to the man are not even a statement of forgiveness so much as a plea on the man's own behalf, urging him to become someone who protects others rather than live on having done this.

Key vocabulary — for Pāli learners
uttānaseyyaka

an infant lying on its back — uttāna (face-up, supine) + seyyaka (one who lies); used of the Bodhisatta as Cūḷadhammapāla, precisely to emphasize that even at an age with no capacity for deliberate self-control, his patience held firm.

uposatha

the observance day, the precepts undertaken on it — the vow several of the nāga-kings in this section are shown maintaining even under torture, treating the keeping of virtue as more important than escaping physical agony by force.

appaṭisama

unequaled, unmatched — a- (not) + paṭisama (equal, comparable); the word used of the Buddha's patience in the section's closing line, "matched by no one in the world together with its gods."

The Sixth and Seventh Remedies: Beginningless Kinship, and the Benefits of Loving-Kindness

Two further remedies follow for the meditator whose resentment has proven especially stubborn, long habituated as he is to the servitude of the defilements. The sixth calls on the doctrine of the beginningless round of rebirths — since no being is easily found who has not, at some point across that unfathomable expanse of time, actually been one's own mother, father, brother, sister, son, or daughter — to reframe the hostile person as, quite literally, a former intimate family member. The seventh, reserved for cases where even that fails, simply lists the eleven concrete benefits that scripture promises to one who cultivates loving-kindness, framed as a direct warning: fail to cool this mind, and these are precisely the benefits one forfeits.

Pali Source

Sace panassa evaṃ satthu pubbacaritaguṇaṃ paccavekkhatopi dīgharattaṃ kilesānaṃ dāsabyaṃ upagatassa neva taṃ paṭighaṃ vūpasammati, athānena anamataggiyāni paccavekkhitabbāni. Tatra hi vuttaṃ —

"Na so, bhikkhave, satto sulabharūpo, yo na mātābhūtapubbo, yo na pitābhūtapubbo, yo na bhātā, yo na bhaginī, yo na putto, yo na dhītābhūtapubbā"ti (saṃ. ni. 2.137-142).

Tasmā tasmiṃ puggale evaṃ cittaṃ uppādetabbaṃ, "ayaṃ kira me atīte mātā hutvā dasamāse kucchiyā pariharitvā muttakarīsakheḷasiṅghāṇikādīni haricandanaṃ viya ajigucchamānā apanetvā ure naccāpentī aṅgena pariharamānā posesi, pitā hutvā ajapathasaṅkupathādīni gantvā vāṇijjaṃ payojayamāno mayhamatthāya jīvitampi pariccajitvā ubhatobyūḷhe saṅgāme pavisitvā nāvāya mahāsamuddaṃ pakkhanditvā aññāni ca dukkarāni karitvā 'puttake posessāmī'ti tehi tehi upāyehi dhanaṃ saṃharitvā maṃ posesi. Bhātā, bhaginī, putto, dhītā ca hutvāpi idañcidañcupakāraṃ akāsīti tatra me nappatirūpaṃ manaṃ padūsetu"nti.

Sace pana evampi cittaṃ nibbāpetuṃ na sakkotiyeva, athānena evaṃ mettānisaṃsā paccavekkhitabbā – "ambho pabbajita, nanu vuttaṃ bhagavatā —

'Mettāya kho, bhikkhave, cetovimuttiyā āsevitāya bhāvitāya bahulīkatāya yānīkatāya vatthukatāya anuṭṭhitāya paricitāya susamāraddhāya ekādasānisaṃsā pāṭikaṅkhā. Katame ekādasa? Sukhaṃ supati, sukhaṃ paṭibujjhati, na pāpakaṃ supinaṃ passati, manussānaṃ piyo hoti, amanussānaṃ piyo hoti, devatā rakkhanti, nāssa aggi vā visaṃ vā satthaṃ vā kamati, tuvaṭaṃ cittaṃ samādhiyati, mukhavaṇṇo pasīdati, asammūḷho kālaṅkaroti, uttarimappaṭivijjhanto brahmalokūpago hotī'ti (a. ni. 11.15).

"Sace tvaṃ idaṃ cittaṃ na nibbāpessasi, imehi ānisaṃsehi paribāhiro bhavissasī"ti.

Ru-Yi Translation

But if, even reflecting thus on the virtues of the Teacher's own past conduct, the resentment still does not settle down in one who has long been given over to servitude under the defilements, then the beginninglessness of the round should be reflected on. For it was said —

"Monks, it is not easy to find a being who has not, at some time in the past, been your mother, been your father, been your brother, been your sister, been your son, been your daughter." (S.N. 2.137–142)

Therefore this thought should be aroused with regard to that person: "It is said that this one, in the past, having been my mother, carried me in her womb for ten months, and, without any disgust — removing my urine, feces, saliva, and mucus as if they were yellow sandalwood — raised me, dancing me upon her breast and carrying me about on her body; or, having been my father, going by goat-tracks and rough mountain paths, conducting trade, sacrificing even his own life for my sake, entering battle arrayed on both sides, plunging by ship into the great ocean, and doing other difficult deeds besides, thinking, 'I shall raise my children,' gathered wealth by this means and that, and so raised me. Or, having been my brother, my sister, my son, or my daughter, did me this favor and that. Therefore it is not fitting for me to let my mind be corrupted toward him."

But if, even so, one is simply unable to cool the mind, then the benefits of loving-kindness should be reflected on thus: "Hey, renunciant, was it not said by the Blessed One —

'Monks, when the deliverance of mind through loving-kindness is cultivated, developed, made much of, made into a vehicle, made a basis, firmly established, consolidated, and properly undertaken, eleven benefits may be expected. What eleven? One sleeps happily; one wakes happily; one does not see bad dreams; one is dear to human beings; one is dear to non-human beings; deities protect one; fire, poison, or weapons do not touch one; one's mind becomes concentrated quickly; the complexion of one's face is serene; one dies unconfused; and, if one penetrates no further, one is headed for the Brahmā-world.' (A.N. 11.15)

"If you do not cool this mind, you will be excluded from these very benefits."

Reading note — Ru-Yi

The beginningless-round reflection works by a specific, almost mathematical logic rather than mere sentiment: given a past stretching back with no discoverable first point, the sheer statistical improbability of any given being never once having occupied one of these six closest possible relationships to oneself becomes, in effect, a near-certainty that they have. The vivid, sensory details supplied for the mother and father in particular — removing bodily filth "as if it were yellow sandalwood," crossing oceans and battlefields to provide for a child — are not incidental color; they are the emotional content the reflection needs in order to function, since an abstract statistical claim alone would carry none of the felt weight required to dissolve resentment.

The eleven benefits, unlike every prior remedy in this sequence, appeal to self-interest rather than to any reasoning about the other person at all — a deliberate final register, offered only once every attempt to see the hostile person differently has been exhausted, on the premise that even bare enlightened self-interest is a legitimate lever if it actually succeeds in cooling the mind where more refined reflections have failed.

Key vocabulary — for Pāli learners
anamatagga

without a discoverable beginning — an- (not) + amata (known) + agga (starting point); the standard epithet for the round of rebirths (saṃsāra) invoked here to establish that every being has, in the immeasurable past, occupied every possible close relationship to oneself.

yānīkata / vatthukata

made into a vehicle / made into a basis — two of the seven intensifying qualifiers (alongside āsevita, bhāvita, bahulīkata, anuṭṭhita, paricita, susamāraddha) describing loving-kindness developed not merely occasionally but as one's habitual, load-bearing mode of mind.

paribāhira

excluded, left outside — pari- (around, completely) + bāhira (external, outer); the word used for what happens to one who fails to develop loving-kindness: exclusion from all eleven of the benefits just enumerated.

The Eighth and Ninth Remedies: Analysis into Elements, and Giving

The eighth remedy dissolves the very object of anger by analysis: interrogated closely enough — hair, or nails, or the earth-element within them, or one of the five aggregates, or one of the twelve sense-bases, or one of the eighteen elements — the target of one's hatred simply disappears, leaving nothing for anger to stand on, "like a mustard seed on the point of an awl, or a painting in the air." For the meditator still unable to perform so exacting an analysis, the ninth and final remedy is far simpler: give the hostile person a gift, or accept one from him — illustrated by a monastery story in which anger carried over from a previous birth is dissolved in a single instant by an alms-going monk's small act of generosity.

Pali Source

Dhātuvinibbhogaṃ pana kātuṃ asakkontena dānasaṃvibhāgo kātabbo. Attano santakaṃ parassa dātabbaṃ, parassa santakaṃ attanā gahetabbaṃ. Sace pana paro bhinnājīvo hoti aparibhogārahaparikkhāro, attano santakameva dātabbaṃ. Tassevaṃ karoto ekanteneva tasmiṃ puggale āghāto vūpasammati. Itarassa ca atītajātito paṭṭhāya anubandhopi kodho taṅkhaṇaññeva vūpasammati, cittalapabbatavihāre tikkhattuṃ vuṭṭhāpitasenāsanena piṇḍapātikattherena "ayaṃ, bhante, aṭṭhakahāpaṇagghanako patto mama mātarā upāsikāya dinno dhammiyalābho, mahāupāsikāya puññalābhaṃ karothā"ti vatvā dinnaṃ pattaṃ laddhamahātherassa viya. Evaṃ mahānubhāvametaṃ dānaṃ nāma. Vuttampi cetaṃ —

"Adantadamanaṃ dānaṃ, dānaṃ sabbatthasādhakaṃ;
Dānena piyavācāya, unnamanti namanti cā"ti.

Ru-Yi Translation

But for one unable to perform the analysis into elements, sharing through giving should be done. What is one's own should be given to the other, and what is the other's should be taken by oneself. But if the other's livelihood is broken and his requisites are not fit for use, only what is one's own should be given. For one who acts in this way, the grudge in that person is certainly settled. And even anger that had followed the other from a past birth was settled in that very moment — as in the case of the great elder who received the bowl, after the alms-round-going elder, who had had his lodging turned him out three times at the Cittalapabbata monastery, said, "Venerable sir, this bowl, worth eight kahāpaṇas, is a legitimate gain given to me by my mother, a lay devotee; please make merit for my great lay-devotee mother by accepting it." Such is the great power of this thing called giving. For it was also said —

"Giving tames the untamed; giving accomplishes everything; by giving, and by kind speech, some are raised up, and some bow down."

Reading note — Ru-Yi

The Cittalapabbata story is placed here rather than earlier in the sequence for a specific reason: it demonstrates giving succeeding precisely where the text says other remedies could not reach, dissolving an anger so entrenched it had carried across a rebirth — the alms-monk does not argue, admonish, or reflect the resident monk into calm, but simply offers him something valuable framed as an opportunity for his own mother to gain merit, and the anger ends in that instant, with no further explanation needed.

The instruction that "what is the other's should be taken by oneself" is easy to overlook but structurally important: the remedy is not one-directional almsgiving toward a hostile party but a genuine exchange, restoring a normal relationship of mutual give-and-take between two people whose connection had been reduced to grudge alone — unless the other's livelihood is itself disreputable, in which case receiving from him is waived and only the meditator's own giving proceeds.

Key vocabulary — for Pāli learners
dhātuvinibbhoga

analysis into elements — dhātu (element) + vinibbhoga (separating out, resolving); the technique of interrogating anger's object down through aggregates, sense-bases, and elements until no stable target for hatred remains.

dānasaṃvibhāga

sharing through giving — dāna (giving) + saṃvibhāga (distribution, sharing); the ninth and final listed remedy, prescribed as the accessible fallback for anyone unable to carry out the more demanding elemental analysis.

piṇḍapātika

one who lives by alms-round — piṇḍa (a lump, a portion of food) + pāta (falling, i.e. into the bowl) + -ika (one who practices); an ascetic observance (dhutaṅga) name for the monk in the Cittalapabbata story whose small gift dissolved a grudge dating from a former life.

The Breaking of Boundaries, Tested by Bandits

With resentment toward the hostile person finally settled by one or another of the nine preceding remedies, loving-kindness now flows toward him exactly as it does toward a loved one, a very dear friend, or a neutral person. The meditator must then complete the "breaking of boundaries" proper: cultivating a genuinely equal mind toward all four categories of person at once. The chapter defines this precisely through a deliberately extreme test case — bandits demanding that one member of a group of four be handed over for sacrifice — and states bluntly that boundary-breaking is accomplished only when the meditator can find no one among the four he would prefer to see handed over, not even himself in the others' place. Once this equality of mind is genuinely reached, both the mental sign and access concentration are attained simultaneously, and absorption follows by the same method already given for the earth kasiṇa.

Once the resentment toward that hostile person has thus settled, the mind proceeds by way of loving-kindness toward him just as it does toward a loved one, a very dear friend, and a neutral person. Then, by developing loving-kindness again and again and accomplishing an equal-mindedness among these four persons — oneself, a loved person, a neutral person, and a hostile person — the breaking of boundaries should be brought about. Because in wishing that any one of them at all be taken, he is thereby wishing harm to that one, while wishing well only to the others.

But when, among these four persons, he sees not even a single one who should be given to the bandits, and his mind proceeds equally toward himself and toward those three others, then the breaking of boundaries has been accomplished. On this the ancient teachers said —

"When, among these four kinds — oneself, a loved one, a neutral one, and a hostile one — one sees no difference, being of a mind that wishes well to all living beings —
"then one is called skilled in loving-kindness, one who has genuinely attained it — when the four boundaries have been broken down for that monk.
"He pervades, with equal loving-kindness, the entire world together with its gods; far greater than the one before him is the one for whom no boundary can any longer be found."

And by this monk, precisely at the same moment as the breaking of boundaries, both the sign and access concentration are obtained. Reading note — Ru-Yi

The bandit scenario is a deliberately extreme thought-experiment precisely because it forces the issue past any comfortable middle ground: an ordinary sense of fairness might already feel satisfied by refusing to nominate the loved one or the neutral party for death, but the text insists that even the seemingly noble instinct to offer oneself in the others' place is itself still a failure of boundary-breaking, since it still discriminates — wishing harm, however self-directed, to someone rather than genuinely wishing well to all four without exception.

The closing verse's claim that boundary-breaking coincides exactly with the arising of both the sign and access concentration is a significant technical point: it means the breaking of boundaries is not merely an ethical achievement running parallel to the meditative one, but is identical, in this system, with the very moment concentration itself matures — the ethical and the meditative attainment are, at this juncture, one and the same event.

Herein, "endowed with loving-kindness" means possessed of loving-kindness. "With a mind" means with the mind. "One direction" is said with reference to pervading the beings included within a single direction, taking as one's starting point whichever being is first apprehended in each particular direction. But the passage beginning "everywhere" is stated for the purpose of showing pervasion without specification. Herein, "everywhere" means in every place. Because a further explanation is here shown by way of such synonyms as "extensive" and the rest, it is said once again, "with a mind endowed with loving-kindness." Or else, because here, unlike in the pervasion by specification, the word "so" or the word "thus" is not repeated, therefore it is said again, "with a mind endowed with loving-kindness." Or this is said by way of a summing-up. And here, in "extensive," extensiveness should be understood by way of the pervasion. But "grown great," by way of level, and "measureless," by way of both its mastery and its measureless object of beings, are meant; "free from enmity" is meant by the abandoning of ill will, its opposing quality; "free from affliction" is meant by the abandoning of displeasure, that is, free from suffering. This is the meaning of the transformation stated by the method beginning "with a mind endowed with loving-kindness."

Reading note — Ru-Yi

The commentary's careful separation of "pervasion by specification" (odhiso, directed at successive compass directions in turn) from "pervasion without specification" (anodhiso, directed at "everywhere, in every way" without reference to direction at all) mirrors a distinction already familiar from the treatise's kasiṇa chapters: a technique that begins with a structured, sequential procedure eventually gives way to a mode of practice that no longer needs the scaffolding, the mind now able to hold the entire field of beings as a single, undivided object.

The horse-on-a-training-ground simile for sending the mind of loving-kindness out through all directions and back again is a small but vivid detail worth pausing on: it captures a controlled, deliberate, repeatable exercise rather than a single diffuse feeling, closer to disciplined training than to a passing emotional state — consistent with how rigorously this whole chapter treats loving-kindness as a technical meditative accomplishment rather than a mere sentiment.

Five Hundred and Twenty-Eight Ways: The Full Enumeration of Loving-Kindness's Pervasions

The chapter now works out, with genuine numerical precision, exactly how many distinct "attainments" of loving-kindness the Paṭisambhidāmagga's three pervasion-modes generate. Unspecified pervasion, applied through five synonyms for "being" and four wish-formulas each, yields twenty. Specified pervasion, applied through seven categories of person and the same four formulas, yields twenty-eight. Directional pervasion, applying both of the previous counts across each of the ten directions, yields four hundred and eighty. The total — five hundred and twenty-eight — is offered not as an exercise in scholastic arithmetic for its own sake but as confirmation that all of this multiplicity, however it is sliced, "is accomplished only in a mind that has actually reached absorption." Herein, "all" is a term of exhaustive inclusion, leaving nothing out. By virtue of breathing, they are "breathing things" (pāṇā) — the meaning is, their functioning is dependent on in-breathing and out-breathing. By virtue of having become, they are "creatures" (bhūtā) — the meaning is, by virtue of having come into being, having been generated. "Pu" is a word for hell. Those who fall (galanti) into it are "persons" (puggalā) — the meaning is, those who go there. "Own existence" (attabhāva) is a word for the body — or rather, for the mere group of five aggregates, since it is only in dependence on that group that the mere conceptual designation "own existence" arises. Those included within that own existence are "those included in a person's own existence" (attabhāvapariyāpannā). "Included" (pariyāpanna) means delimited, contained within.

Just as the word "beings," so too all the remaining terms, having been applied by common usage, should be understood as all being synonyms for "all beings." Therefore, without taking the meaning in that way, loving-kindness should be pervaded without specification by way of any one of these five modes.

Herein, "may all beings be free from enmity" — this is one attainment. "May they be free from affliction" — this is one attainment. "Free from affliction" (abyāpajjā) means without ill will. "May they be free from trouble" — this is one attainment. "Free from trouble" (anīghā) means free from suffering. "May they keep themselves happy" — this is one attainment. Therefore, in these phrases too, whichever one is clear to a given practitioner, loving-kindness should be pervaded by way of that one. Thus, by way of four attainments in each of the five modes, there are twenty attainments in unspecified pervasion.

In specified pervasion, by way of four in each of seven modes, there are twenty-eight. Herein, "women" and "men" are stated by way of sex; "noble ones" and "worldlings," by way of the distinction between the noble and the ordinary person; "deities," "human beings," and "those in states of loss," by way of mode of rebirth.

In directional pervasion, by the method beginning "may all beings in the eastern direction," reckoning twenty for each single direction, there are two hundred; and by the method beginning "may all women in the eastern direction," reckoning twenty-eight for each single direction, there are two hundred and eighty — thus, altogether, four hundred and eighty attainments. So in all, as stated in the Paṭisambhidāmagga, there are five hundred and twenty-eight attainments.

Thus, having developed the deliverance of mind through loving-kindness by way of any one of these attainments, this meditator obtains the eleven benefits stated by the method beginning "one sleeps happily."

Reading note — Ru-Yi

The five-hundred-and-twenty-eight count is arrived at by strict, checkable arithmetic rather than any looser figure of speech: five synonyms for "being" times four wish-formulas gives twenty; seven categories of person times the same four formulas gives twenty-eight; and both of those totals, multiplied across ten directions, give four hundred and eighty — twenty plus twenty-eight plus four hundred and eighty comes to exactly five hundred and twenty-eight, matching the figure the Paṭisambhidāmagga itself states. The exercise is less about producing a large number for its own sake than about demonstrating that the tradition's terminology, however elaborately it enumerates loving-kindness's possible objects, always resolves back into the same handful of actual jhāna attainments — variety of expression, not variety of attainment.

The etymological aside on "being" (satta) is a small but genuine piece of doctrinal caution: rather than letting so basic a word pass unexamined, the text insists on showing that ordinary language calling anyone at all a "being" is technically imprecise — strictly, only one still gripped by desire and lust is "attached" in the sense the word literally names — while also making clear that the imprecision is harmless and unavoidable, since even those free of such attachment must still be referred to somehow, exactly as a fan woven from plant fiber still gets called by a name that originally meant a palm-leaf.

Reading note — Ru-Yi

The Visākha story is unusual among this treatise's illustrative tales for tracking a monk across an entire life of restless practice — from a wealthy layman who sails to Sri Lanka on a single coin, to a monk who deliberately gives up even his ordination-day fortune, to a wanderer who spends exactly four months at each monastery for years on end — before it ever reaches the specific point the text is illustrating: the tree spirit's grief is not for his death but for his mere departure, a striking measure of how thoroughly his loving-kindness had become a felt, protective presence for beings who were never its intended human audience at all.

The cow story makes an important technical distinction explicit: the cow's protection from the spear is expressly said to come "neither through the power of access, nor through the power of absorption" — that is, not through any meditative attainment whatsoever, since a cow is obviously incapable of jhāna — but purely through the natural, unforced intensity of a mother's love for her calf, offered as evidence that the eleventh benefit's protective power is a real feature of loving-kindness of any intensity, not a supernatural side-effect reserved only for accomplished meditators.

The Development of Compassion

The chapter now turns to the second divine abiding, compassion, whose starting sequence inverts the logic just established for loving-kindness in one crucial respect: rather than beginning with a loved person, the meditator must begin with someone genuinely wretched and suffering, since a loved person, precisely because he is loved, does not naturally call forth the specific quality of "wishing to remove another's suffering" that defines compassion. Failing to find an actually wretched person close at hand, the chapter offers a second option: viewing a currently prosperous wrongdoer through the same lens used for a condemned criminal being led to execution — outwardly enjoying pleasures, yet, to the compassionate onlooker, already as good as doomed. From there the same graduated sequence as loving-kindness resumes: loved person, neutral person, hostile person, then boundary-breaking and absorption by the identical method.

The condemned-man simile is doing careful philosophical work: it is not claiming that the happy wrongdoer is secretly miserable right now, in the way ordinary sympathy might assume, but rather that his present enjoyment is simply irrelevant to an accurate assessment of his situation, exactly as a condemned man's meal of delicacies on the way to execution does nothing to change what is actually about to happen to him. Compassion, on this reading, tracks a person's real trajectory rather than his momentary felt experience — which is precisely why it can be extended even to someone who, by every visible external measure, has nothing wrong with him at all.

The chapter's explicit rejection of the Aṅguttara commentary's alternative sequence — starting with the hostile person rather than the wretched one — is a rare moment of open scholarly disagreement within the text, and it settles the dispute by appeal to nothing more elaborate than the canonical formula's own wording: since the Vibhaṅga's illustrative case is a person "fallen into misery, wretched," rather than a hostile person, the practice sequence must be built to match that specific canonical image rather than any theoretically tidier progression.

The Development of Sympathetic Joy

The third divine abiding likewise avoids starting with an ordinary loved person, on the grounds that mere affection alone is not yet the specific "proximate cause" sympathetic joy requires — that cause being, rather, someone's actual happiness or good fortune. The best starting point, the chapter suggests, is a lighthearted, high-spirited "boon companion," a person whose defining trait is joy itself, so naturally cheerful that he laughs before he even speaks; failing such a person, an ordinary loved one currently enjoying visible happiness will do, and even a loved one presently in misfortune can serve as the object, so long as the meditator reaches for a memory of that person's past happiness or an anticipation of happiness yet to come.

Ru-Yi Translation

In beginning the development of sympathetic joy as well, one should not begin first with a loved person and the rest. For a loved person, by the mere fact of being loved, is not yet the proximate cause for sympathetic joy — still less a neutral or hostile person. As with the previous two abidings, a person of the opposite sex singled out, and one who has died, are simply not the field for this practice at all.

But a very dear friend of the boon-companion type, the sort called in the commentaries a "jolly companion," could well serve as the proximate cause. For such a person is joy itself, joyous through and through — he laughs first and speaks afterward — and so he, or such a one, should be pervaded with sympathetic joy first. Alternatively, having seen or heard of a loved person who is happy, prosperous, and rejoicing, sympathetic joy should be aroused thus: "indeed, this being is rejoicing — oh, how good, how excellent!" For it is with reference to this same sense that it is said in the Vibhaṅga, "And how, monk, does one dwell pervading one direction with a mind endowed with sympathetic joy? Just as one might feel joy on seeing a single dear and beloved person — so does one pervade all beings with sympathetic joy" (Vibh. 663).

And if that boon companion, or that loved person, was happy in the past but is presently fallen into misery and wretchedness, then, recollecting only his past state of happiness, sympathetic joy should be aroused by taking hold of just that aspect of his former joy, thinking, "in the past this one was of such great wealth, such a great retinue, always so joyful." Or else, sympathetic joy should be aroused by taking hold of an aspect of his future joy, thinking, "or else, in the future, having regained that same good fortune, he will go about riding on the backs of elephants and horses, or in golden litters."

Having thus aroused sympathetic joy toward the loved person, next, in due order, sympathetic joy should be developed toward the neutral person, then the hostile person. Absorption should then be developed. If, as before, resentment arises toward the hostile person, it should be settled by the very method already described for loving-kindness; then, having brought about, in these three together with oneself, the breaking of boundaries among the four persons by way of equal-mindedness, and cultivating, developing, and making much of that sign, absorption should be developed by way of the first-through-third and first-through-fourth jhāna methods, exactly as described for loving-kindness. Beyond this, the transformation — "unspecified pervasion with five modes, specified pervasion with seven modes, directional pervasion with ten modes" — and the benefits beginning "one sleeps happily" should be understood exactly as stated for loving-kindness.

This is the detailed treatment of the development of sympathetic joy.

Reading note — Ru-Yi

The specification of sympathetic joy's proximate cause as someone's actual happiness, rather than mere affection, is the chapter's most philosophically precise moment in distinguishing the four divine abidings from one another: loving-kindness wishes well regardless of the other's current state, compassion responds specifically to suffering, and sympathetic joy responds specifically to another's good fortune — three superficially similar warm feelings toward others turn out, on inspection, to require three logically distinct triggering conditions.

The permission to use a memory of past happiness, or even an anticipation of future happiness, as a substitute object when someone loved has currently fallen on hard times is a notable methodological flexibility: it shows the divine abidings functioning less like a literal, moment-by-moment response to a person's actual circumstances and more like a disciplined redirection of attention toward whatever true fact about that person — past, present, or future — is capable of generating the specific wholesome state being cultivated.

The Development of Equanimity, and Why It Can Only Follow the Other Three

Equanimity is unique among the four divine abidings in that it cannot be attempted from a standing start: the meditator must already have attained the third jhāna in loving-kindness, compassion, or sympathetic joy, and only then, emerging from that proficient attainment, see the specific dangers still latent in the previous three — their being bound up with an attitude of "cherishing" beings, their proximity to the near enemies of favoritism and resentment, their grossness due to being joined with pleasure — before beginning equanimity itself, appropriately enough, with a neutral person as the very first object. The section closes with a precise technical point: this fourth jhāna of equanimity can never arise directly out of the earth-kasiṇa's own third jhāna, no matter how accomplished the meditator, because the objects are simply too dissimilar — only the third jhāna of the other three divine abidings shares enough in common with it to serve as a basis.

Reading note — Ru-Yi

The dangers identified in the first three divine abidings are a striking piece of self-critique built into the very system that just spent three long sections cultivating them: loving-kindness, compassion, and sympathetic joy, however skillful and necessary as a foundation, are each said to sit dangerously close to a specific corrupted counterpart — resentment lurking near compassion's concern for suffering, favoritism lurking near loving-kindness's and sympathetic joy's warmth — and all three, simply by virtue of being joined with pleasant feeling, are judged too "gross" to be the final word on how a fully purified mind relates to other beings.

The technical point about object-similarity closing the section is easy to pass over but genuinely significant for how this treatise conceives of jhāna: it is not merely depth of concentration that determines what the next jhāna in a sequence can be, but the specific character of the object concentrated upon — a third jhāna grown out of contemplating beings with loving-kindness carries forward an affinity for equanimity toward beings that a third jhāna grown out of staring at a colored disk simply does not share, no matter how deep either concentration may be.

Miscellaneous Discussion: Etymologies, and the Four Abidings Analyzed by Characteristic

Having given the detailed method for developing each of the four divine abidings individually, the chapter now opens a "miscellaneous discussion" gathering further points that apply across all four together. It begins, in the treatise's customary manner, with a brief etymological note on each of the four Pāli names, and then subjects each abiding to the same rigorous fourfold analysis — characteristic, function, manifestation, and proximate cause — used everywhere else in this work, adding for each one its specific mark of success and its specific mode of failure: the precise, near-miss corruption each abiding is liable to collapse into when not properly sustained.

Reading note — Ru-Yi

The paired "success" (sampatti) and "failure" (vipatti) given for each abiding is one of this chapter's most practically useful contributions: each divine abiding is shown to sit directly beside a corrupted double that superficially resembles it — loving-kindness beside mere worldly affection, compassion beside grief, sympathetic joy beside giddy frivolity, and equanimity beside blank, uninformed indifference — meaning that simply feeling something warm, sad, delighted, or unmoved toward another person is never sufficient evidence that one of these four genuine qualities is actually present.

Equanimity's proximate cause, seeing that beings are the owners of their own deeds, quietly reframes what "equanimity toward beings" actually means in this system: it is not indifference to their fate, but a clear-eyed recognition that their fate is governed by a law — kamma — outside the meditator's power to alter by wishing, which is precisely why unknowing, uninformed indifference is named as this quality's specific counterfeit rather than its natural conclusion.

Common and Particular Purposes, and the Near and Far Enemies

All four divine abidings share a common purpose — the pleasure of insight and a favorable rebirth — but each also has its own individual purpose: the specific defilement it is designed to remove. The chapter then supplies, for each abiding, a pair of "enemies," a distinction that will become one of the most frequently cited analytical tools in later Theravādin literature: a "near enemy," dangerously similar in flavor and therefore able to slip in unnoticed disguised as the genuine quality, and a "far enemy," different enough in character that it poses no real risk of being mistaken for the real thing, however directly opposed the two may be.

For all four of these divine abidings, the pleasure of insight and the attainment of a favorable rebirth are their common purpose; the removal of ill will and the rest is each one's own particular purpose. "Friends, this is the escape from ill will, namely, the deliverance of mind through loving-kindness. Friends, this is the escape from cruelty, namely, the deliverance of mind through compassion. Friends, this is the escape from discontent, namely, the deliverance of mind through sympathetic joy. Friends, this is the escape from lust, namely, the deliverance of mind through equanimity." For each one of these, there are two enemies apiece, by way of nearness and distance. For the loving-kindness divine abiding, lust is the near enemy, like an enemy who lives close by to a man, because of their shared quality of dwelling on someone's good qualities; it gains entry easily, and therefore loving-kindness must be guarded against it especially carefully. Ill will is the far enemy, like an enemy who has taken refuge in a mountain thicket or some such distant place, being of both a similar and a dissimilar nature to it; therefore loving-kindness may be developed fearlessly with regard to that. For it is simply not possible that one should, at one and the same time, be extending loving-kindness and also be producing anger.

For the compassion divine abiding, the worldly grief that arises according to the passage beginning, "for one who regards as a non-obtaining the non-obtainment of forms cognizable by the eye that are desirable, agreeable, pleasing, charming, connected with worldly gain, or who recollects as past, ceased, and changed something previously obtained — grief arises; such grief is called worldly grief" (M.N. 3.307), is the near enemy, because of their shared quality of contemplating misfortune. Cruelty is the far enemy, being of both a similar and a dissimilar nature. Therefore compassion may be developed fearlessly with regard to that. For it is simply not possible that one should, at one and the same time, be practicing compassion and also be causing harm with one's hands or the like.

For the sympathetic-joy divine abiding, the worldly joy that arises according to the passage beginning, "for one who regards as an obtaining ... the obtainment of forms cognizable by the eye that are desirable ... connected with worldly gain, or who recollects as past, ceased, and changed something previously obtained — joy arises; such joy is called worldly joy" (M.N. 3.306), is the near enemy, because of their shared quality of contemplating good fortune; discontent is the far enemy, being of both a similar and a dissimilar nature. Therefore sympathetic joy may be developed fearlessly with regard to that. For it is simply not possible that one should, at one and the same time, be rejoicing sympathetically and also feel weary of secluded lodgings or of the higher wholesome states.

For the equanimity divine abiding, the unknowing worldly equanimity that arises according to the passage beginning, "on seeing a form with the eye, equanimity arises in a foolish, deluded ordinary person who has not overcome his limitations, who has not overcome the results of action, who does not see the danger, and who is unlearned; such equanimity does not go beyond mere form, and is therefore called worldly equanimity" (M.N. 3.308), is the near enemy, because of their shared quality of failing to examine faults and virtues. Lust and resentment are the far enemies, being of both a similar and a dissimilar nature. Therefore equanimity may be developed fearlessly with regard to those. For it is simply not possible that one should, at one and the same time, be practicing equanimity and also be inflamed with lust or struck with resentment.

Reading note — Ru-Yi

The near-enemy/far-enemy distinction here receives one of its clearest formulations anywhere in the Pāli commentarial corpus, and the logic behind why the "far enemy" poses no real danger is worth making explicit: precisely because ill will feels nothing like loving-kindness, or lust nothing like equanimity, no meditator is ever genuinely at risk of mistaking one for the other — the danger is exclusively at the near end, where an unskilled meditator can go on for a long time believing he is cultivating loving-kindness when he has actually settled into ordinary affectionate attachment, or believing he has attained equanimity when he has merely stopped paying attention.

The recurring formula "it is simply not possible" (aṭṭhānametaṃ), closing each of the four analyses, makes a strong claim worth noting precisely: it is not merely unlikely or inadvisable that the genuine abiding and its far enemy coexist, but strictly impossible, in exactly the same sense that two mutually exclusive mental states cannot occupy a single moment of consciousness — a modest but important piece of Abhidhamma psychology underwriting the whole discussion.

Expanding the Object, Farmer by Farmer, and Why Equanimity Is the Roof

Two further points close out the miscellaneous discussion of structure common to all four abidings. The first describes, through the image of a skillful farmer who marks out his field before plowing it, the exact stepwise sequence by which the object of these meditations is deliberately expanded — one dwelling, then two, then a whole village, district, kingdom, and finally an entire world-system — rather than attempted all at once. The second explains, through the image of a peaked roof that cannot be set in place until pillars and cross-beams already stand beneath it, why equanimity can never be developed as a starting point but only as the natural "outflow" of the third jhāna already attained in one of the other three divine abidings.

Reading note — Ru-Yi

The farmer simile makes explicit something the treatise has assumed rather than stated throughout: that expanding a meditation's object is a deliberate, incremental discipline rather than a sudden leap of ambition — a farmer who tried to plow more ground than he had properly marked would simply do a poor job of the whole field, and a meditator who tried to pervade "all beings everywhere" before first mastering a single dwelling-place's worth of beings would, by the same logic, fail to genuinely establish the mind on any of them.

The roof-and-pillars simile pins down a structural claim already implicit in the earlier remark that equanimity is the "outflow" of the other three: it is not simply easier, pedagogically, to attempt equanimity last, but architecturally necessary — precisely because equanimity's fourth jhāna and the other abidings' third jhāna share the same object (as established two sections earlier), the earlier attainment is not merely useful preparation but a literal structural precondition, the beam-frame without which the roof quite simply has nothing to rest upon.

Why "Divine Abidings"? Why Exactly Four? What Order, and Why "Measureless"?

The chapter now poses, and answers, four pointed questions at once: why these four states are called "divine abidings" in the first place; why there are exactly four of them rather than some other number; what determines the fixed order in which they are always listed; and why the Abhidhamma calls them "measureless states." The answer to "why divine" turns on the sense of "best" — these are the best possible ways of conducting oneself toward other beings, making anyone who practices them, for the duration of that practice, "equal to Brahmā." The answer to "why four," "what order," and "why measureless" all converge on a single memorable image: a mother's four different responses to four different children — an infant, a sick child, one who has just reached the flush of youth, and one busy with his own affairs — mapped exactly onto loving-kindness, compassion, sympathetic joy, and equanimity in that fixed sequence.

Here one might ask: why, exactly, are these — loving-kindness, compassion, sympathetic joy, and equanimity — called "divine abidings"? And why exactly four of them? And what is their order? And why are they called "measureless states" in the Abhidhamma? It is answered thus. First, their status as "divine abidings" should be understood in the sense of "best," by way of their being free of fault. For these are the best ways of abiding in relation to beings, by way of right conduct toward them. And just as Brahmās dwell with minds free of fault, so too yogis endowed with these abidings dwell having become equal to Brahmā — thus, in the sense of "best," and by way of their fault-free nature, they are called "divine abidings."

As for the question beginning "why exactly four" — this is its answer.

These are four, by way of being the path of purification and so on; and their order proceeds by way of the mode of promoting welfare and the rest; and because they operate in a measureless field, for that reason they are the "measureless states."

Among these: loving-kindness is the path of purification for one full of ill will; compassion, for one full of cruelty; sympathetic joy, for one full of discontent; equanimity, for one full of lust — this is why there are four. And because attention toward beings is precisely fourfold: by way of bringing about welfare, removing what is not welfare, rejoicing in good fortune, and remaining unconcerned — this too is why there are four. And just as a mother, with regard to four children — an infant, a sick child, one who has reached the flush of youth, and one occupied with his own affairs — wishes growth for the infant, wishes the removal of illness for the sick one, wishes long persistence of good fortune for the one who has reached youth, and remains unconcerned in some manner toward the one occupied with his own business, so too one who dwells in the measureless states should conduct himself, by way of loving-kindness and the rest, toward all beings. Therefore, on this basis — by way of being the path of purification and so on — there are exactly four measureless states.

And because one wishing to develop all four of these should first proceed toward beings by way of the mode of promoting welfare, and loving-kindness has precisely the characteristic of proceeding in that mode — therefore loving-kindness comes first. Then, having seen, heard, or surmised that beings for whom welfare has thus been wished are overwhelmed by suffering, one should proceed by way of the mode of removing suffering, and compassion has precisely the characteristic of proceeding in that mode — therefore compassion comes next. Then, having seen the good fortune of those same beings, for whom welfare was wished and from whom the wished-for suffering has departed, one should proceed by way of rejoicing in that good fortune, and sympathetic joy has precisely the characteristic of gladdening — therefore sympathetic joy comes next. After that, since there is nothing further that needs to be done, one should proceed by way of the neutral mode reckoned as the state of looking on with equanimity, and equanimity has precisely the characteristic of proceeding in that neutral mode — therefore equanimity comes last. Therefore, on this basis — by way of the mode of promoting welfare and the rest — loving-kindness is said to come first, then compassion, sympathetic joy, and equanimity: this order should be understood.

And because all four of these operate in a measureless field — for beings, being measureless, form the field for them — and because they proceed by way of pervading the whole of that field, without taking hold of any measure such as "loving-kindness and the rest should be developed only toward a single being within this much space" — for this reason it was said —

These are four, by way of being the path of purification and so on; and their order proceeds by way of the mode of promoting welfare and the rest; and because they operate in a measureless field, for that reason they are the "measureless states."

Reading note — Ru-Yi

The mother-of-four-children image is doing unusually efficient work: it answers all three remaining questions — the number four, the fixed order, and the measurelessness — with a single, ordinary domestic scene, grounding what might otherwise seem like an arbitrary scholastic taxonomy in something every reader already recognizes from lived experience. A mother's responses genuinely do come in exactly this order and no other: concern for growth precedes concern for illness only in the sense that welfare must first be wished before its absence (suffering) can be noticed and addressed, and equanimity toward the self-sufficient older child is possible only once the other three concerns have already been discharged.

The mapping of each abiding onto a specific besetting vice — loving-kindness for the ill-willed, compassion for the cruel, sympathetic joy for the envious, equanimity for the lustful — quietly reframes the entire chapter's structure retroactively: rather than four independent virtues that happen to be grouped together, they now read as a deliberately designed fourfold antidote kit, each component engineered against one of four specific corruptions of how a mind relates to other beings.

Why the First Three Stop Short of the Fourth Jhāna

Although all four divine abidings share the same measureless field and the same single defining characteristic of taking beings as their object, they nonetheless divide sharply along one specific line: loving-kindness, compassion, and sympathetic joy can never rise beyond the third jhāna, while equanimity can occur in no jhāna but the fourth. The reason given is a precise point of Abhidhamma feeling-theory: the first three are inseparably bound up with joyful feeling, since each one is defined as the specific escape from a displeasure-based defilement, while equanimity, by its very nature as a "neutral" state toward beings, simply cannot occur at all except conjoined with the equanimous feeling unique to the fourth jhāna.

The two states are not points on a single continuous scale but categorically different attainments, distinguished by which of two mutually exclusive feelings, joy or equanimity, necessarily accompanies them.

Refuting a Misreading of the Book of Eights

The chapter anticipates and refutes an objection to what has just been established. Someone might point to a passage in the Book of Eights, where the Buddha instructs a monk to develop "this concentration" through every possible jhāna variation — with and without applied and sustained thought, with and without joy, with pleasant or with equanimous feeling — and conclude that all four measureless states must therefore be capable of the full range up through the fourth or fifth jhāna, contradicting what was just said about equanimity alone occupying that position. The chapter rejects this reading sharply, on the grounds that taking the passage this literally would produce absurd results elsewhere in the canon, and then supplies the sutta's actual narrative context to show what the passage really means: general instructions to a specific backsliding monk on deepening a basic, already-established concentration, not a claim that every named meditation subject spans every jhāna.

Ru-Yi Translation

But someone might say: "Because it was stated by the Blessed One, without distinction, regarding all four measureless states, in the Book of Eights — 'Then you, monk, should develop this concentration with applied and sustained thought; you should develop it without applied thought but with sustained thought only; you should develop it without applied or sustained thought; you should develop it with joy; you should develop it without joy; you should develop it accompanied by pleasant feeling; you should develop it accompanied by equanimous feeling' (A.N. 8.63) — therefore the four measureless states occur in the first-through-fourth and first-through-fifth jhāna reckonings." Such a person should by no means be granted his conclusion. For if this were so, then the foundations of mindfulness such as contemplation of the body would also occur in the first-through-fourth and first-through-fifth jhāna reckonings, and, with regard to feeling and the rest, there would not even be a first jhāna at all, let alone a second and the rest that follow. Therefore one should not seize upon the mere shadow of the wording and thereby misrepresent the Blessed One — for the Buddha's word is profound, and it must be grasped, by attending upon one's teachers, according to its actual intention.

For here is that actual intention. It is told that a certain monk had requested, "It would be good, venerable sir, if the Blessed One would teach me the Dhamma in brief, so that, having heard the Blessed One's Dhamma, I might dwell alone, withdrawn, diligent, ardent, and resolute." Now, because this monk, even previously, upon hearing the Dhamma, would simply go on dwelling right there without going off to do the ascetic's actual work, the Blessed One, rebuking him, said, "Just so, indeed, here some foolish men keep imploring me alone, and, once the Dhamma has been spoken, imagine that all that is needed is to go on following after me." But then, because that monk was nevertheless possessed of the supporting conditions for arahantship, admonishing him further, the Blessed One said, "Therefore, monk, you should train yourself thus: 'my mind, within, shall become steady, well composed, and no arisen evil, unwholesome states shall overpower it and remain occupying it.' Just so, monk, should you train yourself."

Reading note — Ru-Yi

The refutation's method is worth noting for its generality: rather than arguing directly against the objector's reading of this one passage, the chapter shows that the same interpretive method, applied consistently elsewhere, produces results everyone already agrees are false — the four foundations of mindfulness spanning jhānas they manifestly do not span. A reading that only works when applied selectively to the passage convenient for one's argument, and produces absurdities everywhere else it is applied, is thereby shown to rest on a misunderstanding of what kind of passage this actually is.

The rebuked monk's situation quietly illustrates a recurring theme of this treatise: hearing the Dhamma, even repeatedly and from the Buddha himself, is explicitly said to accomplish nothing on its own if the listener never goes off to do "the ascetic's actual work" — and yet the same monk, despite this exact failing, is judged to have the supporting conditions for arahantship, a reminder that a rebuke for laziness and a genuine capacity for the goal are treated here as fully compatible facts about the same person.

Tracing the Sutta to Its Actual Conclusion: A Root Concentration Grown to Arahantship

The chapter now finishes tracing through the sutta it began quoting in the previous section, showing exactly how a single "root concentration" — nothing more, at first, than a steadied, well-composed mind free of arisen unwholesome states — is instructed to grow: first by taking loving-kindness as its object and being developed there through every jhāna variation, then by taking the remaining three divine abidings as its object in the same way, and only then by taking up the four foundations of mindfulness, beginning with contemplation of the body, all the way to the sutta's climactic image of an arahant who now walks, stands, sits, and lies down in nothing but perfect ease. Read in its full context, the passage never claims that loving-kindness or compassion themselves span all five jhānas — only that the same underlying root concentration, redirected across a whole series of different objects in turn, does.

Ru-Yi Translation

By this admonition, a root concentration amounting to mere one-pointedness of mind by way of one's own internal state was described for him. Then, in order to show that "having attained just this much, one should not be satisfied but should go on developing this same concentration further," it was said: "Monk, when your mind, within, has become steady and well composed, and no arisen evil, unwholesome states overpower it and remain occupying it, then, monk, you should train yourself thus: 'the deliverance of mind through loving-kindness will be developed by me, made much of, made a vehicle, made a basis, firmly established, consolidated, and properly undertaken.' Just so, monk, should you train yourself." Having thus described the development by way of loving-kindness for him, it was said further: "Monk, when this concentration has thus been developed and made much of by you, then, monk, you should develop this root concentration with applied and sustained thought as well … and so on … you should develop it accompanied by equanimous feeling as well."

The meaning of this is: when this root concentration has thus been developed by you, monk, by way of loving-kindness, then, without being satisfied with just that much, you should develop this same root concentration in application to other objects as well, bringing it to the first-through-fourth and first-through-fifth jhānas, by the method beginning "with applied and sustained thought."

And having said this, in order to show further that the development should be carried out, by way of the first-through-fourth and first-through-fifth jhānas applied to other objects as well, with compassion and the remaining divine abidings going before it in turn, it was said: "Monk, when this concentration has thus been developed and made much of by you, then, monk, you should train yourself thus: 'the deliverance of mind through compassion…'" and so on.

Having thus described the development going before with loving-kindness and the rest by way of the first-through-fourth and first-through-fifth jhānas, then, in order to show it going before with contemplation of the body and the rest, it was said: "Monk, when this concentration has thus been developed and made much of by you, then, monk, you should train yourself thus: 'I shall dwell contemplating the body in the body'" and so on — and the teaching was brought to its conclusion at the very peak of arahantship, with: "Monk, when this concentration has thus been developed, thoroughly developed, then, monk, wherever you go, you will go only in comfort; wherever you stand, you will stand only in comfort; wherever you sit, you will sit only in comfort; wherever you lie down, you will lie down only in comfort." Therefore it should be understood that loving-kindness and the rest occur only within the first-through-third and first-through-fourth jhāna reckonings, while equanimity occurs only in the one remaining jhāna. And they are analyzed in exactly this way in the Abhidhamma as well (Dhs. 251 and what follows; Vibh. 673 and what follows).

Reading note — Ru-Yi

The sutta's actual architecture, once traced through in full, turns out to be a single expanding curriculum rather than a list of four separately jhāna-graded meditations: one root concentration, established once through basic mental steadiness, is then successively redirected onto loving-kindness, then the remaining divine abidings, then the foundations of mindfulness beginning with the body — with the full range of jhāna variations belonging to this redirecting process itself, not to any one of the individual objects it happens to land on along the way.

The closing image — an arahant who moves, stands, sits, and lies down "only in comfort" — is a quietly understated way to describe the goal of the entire spiritual path: not ecstatic experience or supernormal power, but simply the complete absence of the friction, striving, and discomfort that ordinarily accompanies even the most basic physical postures of an unliberated mind.

Four Different Summits: How Each Abiding Points Beyond Itself

Beyond the jhāna-level distinction just established, the four divine abidings differ from one another in a further, more surprising way: each one, according to the Haliddavasana Sutta, has its own distinct "utmost point" — not itself, but a specific further attainment it uniquely predisposes the mind toward. Loving-kindness's utmost is the beautiful liberation attained through pure, unrepulsive colors; compassion's utmost is the base of infinite space; sympathetic joy's utmost is the base of infinite consciousness; and equanimity's utmost is the base of nothingness. The chapter then explains, one by one, exactly why each specific pairing holds, tracing a plausible psychological mechanism by which sustained practice of each divine abiding leaves the mind specifically primed for the particular formless attainment named as its own culmination.

Ru-Yi Translation

Thus, although these stand divided in two by way of the first-through-third-and-fourth jhāna reckoning and the one remaining jhāna, a distinctive power, unlike one another, by way of having the beautiful and so forth as their utmost point, should also be understood among them. For in the Haliddavasana Sutta these are specifically distinguished by way of having the beautiful and so forth as their utmost. As it is said — "Monks, I say that the deliverance of mind through loving-kindness has the beautiful as its utmost. Monks, I say that the deliverance of mind through compassion has the base of infinite space as its utmost. Monks, I say that the deliverance of mind through sympathetic joy has the base of infinite consciousness as its utmost. Monks, I say that the deliverance of mind through equanimity has the base of nothingness as its utmost." (S.N. 5.235)

Why, then, are these described in this way? Because each is a supporting condition for that specific attainment. For beings become non-repulsive to one who dwells in loving-kindness. Then, from that familiarity with what is non-repulsive, when he directs his mind toward non-repulsive, purely colored objects such as blue and the rest, his mind plunges into them with little difficulty. Thus loving-kindness is a supporting condition for the beautiful liberation, but not for anything beyond that; therefore it is said to have the beautiful as its utmost.

But for one who dwells in compassion, since compassion arises through contemplating suffering that has befallen beings by way of the sign of form — such as being struck with a stick and the like — the danger inherent in form becomes thoroughly known to him. Then, since the danger in form has thus become thoroughly known to him, when, having set aside one of the kasiṇas such as the earth kasiṇa, he directs his mind toward space, the escape from form, his mind plunges into it with little difficulty. Thus compassion is a supporting condition for the base of infinite space, but not for anything beyond that; therefore it is said to have the base of infinite space as its utmost.

But for one who dwells in sympathetic joy, since sympathetic joy arises through contemplating the consciousness of beings who have become joyful for this reason or that, his mind becomes accustomed to the apprehending of consciousness. Then, having gradually attained the base of infinite space, when he passes beyond it and directs his mind toward the consciousness having as its field the sign of space, his mind plunges into it with little difficulty. Thus sympathetic joy is a supporting condition for the base of infinite consciousness, but not for anything beyond that; therefore it is said to have the base of infinite consciousness as its utmost.

But for one who dwells in equanimity, owing to the absence of any active concern such as "may beings be happy, or be freed from suffering, or not fall away from the happiness they have attained," and owing to a disposition averse to grasping at pleasure, pain, and the other ultimate realities, his mind becomes one that finds it difficult to grasp what does not, in an ultimate sense, exist. Then, for one whose mind has thus become accustomed to this aversion from grasping at ultimate realities, and which finds it difficult to grasp what does not ultimately exist, when, having gradually attained and then passed beyond the base of infinite consciousness, he directs his mind toward the non-existence, in its essential nature, of that very consciousness which is itself an ultimate reality, his mind plunges into it with little difficulty. Thus equanimity is a supporting condition for the base of nothingness, but not for anything beyond that; therefore it is said to have the base of nothingness as its utmost.

Reading note — Ru-Yi

The chain of four explanations traces a coherent psychological logic that carries the meditator progressively further from the physical world: loving-kindness's habituation to seeing beings as "non-repulsive" transfers naturally onto pure visual colors (the beautiful liberation); compassion's exposure to suffering caused specifically through physical form leaves the mind primed to seek an escape from form altogether, into space; sympathetic joy's contemplation of others' joyful consciousness leaves the mind practiced at apprehending consciousness itself as an object; and equanimity's disciplined refusal to actively grasp at anything leaves the mind uniquely prepared for a base whose entire content is the absence of anything to grasp at all.

It is worth noting that loving-kindness's "utmost," the beautiful liberation, is not one of the four formless attainments the other three abidings point toward, but a fine-material-sphere attainment associated with the pure-color kasiṇas — a small but telling asymmetry suggesting that loving-kindness, alone among the four, remains oriented toward the beauty of embodied, visible things even at its most refined, while the other three abidings each point, in sequence, one step further into the formless.

Brahmavihāraniddesa Complete

The chapter closes by drawing all four divine abidings together into a single, sweeping claim: that these four states, cultivated together, are what actually fulfill every one of the great perfections a bodhisatta traverses on the path to Buddhahood — giving, virtue, renunciation, wisdom, energy, patience, truthfulness, resolve, loving-kindness itself, and equanimity — because a mind genuinely wishing well to all beings, unable to bear their suffering, delighting in their good fortune, and remaining unshaken in the face of what it cannot control, is simply the mind out of which every other virtuous action already naturally flows. The chapter ends with the traditional colophon marking the ninth chapter's completion.

Reading note — Ru-Yi

The chapter's closing move is characteristic of how the Visuddhimagga treats even its most narrowly technical meditation instructions: having spent an entire chapter on jhāna mechanics, near and far enemies, and precise arithmetic of pervasion-modes, it steps back at the very end to insist that none of this is separable from ethical life as a whole — a mind that has genuinely mastered these four abidings is said to already contain, in embryo, the motivational structure behind every one of the ten perfections, from giving to equanimity itself, which closes the list by naming the very quality the chapter has just spent its final sections analyzing.

The specific claim that great beings "act as benefactors who help first, without waiting to be asked" through loving-kindness is a small but pointed ethical detail: it distinguishes a genuinely cultivated divine abiding from mere responsive kindness, locating the highest form of the virtue in unprompted initiative rather than in simply answering requests for help when they happen to arise.

Key vocabulary — for Pāli learners
pāramī

a perfection — one of the ten transcendent virtues (giving, virtue, renunciation, wisdom, energy, patience, truthfulness, resolve, loving-kindness, equanimity) a bodhisatta fulfills across countless lifetimes on the path to Buddhahood, all shown here to flow from the four divine abidings.

aṭṭhārasa buddhadhamma

the eighteen qualities distinctive to a Buddha — aṭṭhārasa (eighteen) + buddhadhamma (a Buddha's quality); a traditional list of qualities unique to a fully awakened Buddha, named here as the outermost horizon of what the four divine abidings, fully perfected, ultimately fulfill.

Brahmavihāraniddesa

the Description of the Divine Abidings — brahmavihāra (divine abiding) + niddesa (description, exposition); the traditional title of this ninth chapter, as given in its own closing colophon.

Brahmavihāraniddesa Complete

This completes Ru-Yi's translation of the Visuddhimagga's ninth chapter, Brahmavihāraniddesa — the four divine abidings of loving-kindness, compassion, sympathetic joy, and equanimity, from the preliminary handling of resentment through the nine-step sequence for dissolving it, the precise mechanics of boundary-breaking and absorption, and the chapter's closing miscellaneous discussion of etymology, near and far enemies, and the perfections these four abidings are said to fulfill. The tenth chapter, Āruppaniddesa — the Description of the Immaterial States — follows next, treating the four formless attainments: the bases of infinite space, infinite consciousness, nothingness, and neither-perception-nor-non-perception.
