The Five Aggregates

This page carries Ru-Yi's translation of the Visuddhimagga's chapter 14, Khandhaniddesa ("The Discussion of the Aggregates") — the opening chapter of the treatise's third and final division, paññā (wisdom), marking a decisive shift from meditative attainment to direct analytical understanding. It defines wisdom itself, surveys its many classifications, lays out the architecture of ground, root, and body governing everything still to come, and then catalogues the five aggregates — form, feeling, perception, formations, and consciousness — in full Abhidhamma detail.

What Wisdom Actually Is: The Money-Changer and the Two Coin-Gazers

The fourteenth chapter opens the Visuddhimagga's third and final major division — paññā, wisdom — by picking up exactly where concentration left off: the verse that framed the entire treatise back in chapter one spoke of a wise man grounded in virtue developing "mind and wisdom" together, and mind, under the heading of concentration, has now been fully treated. Wisdom remains. Buddhaghosa poses six questions to organize the whole undertaking — what is wisdom, in what sense is it "wisdom," what are its characteristic, function, manifestation, and proximate cause, how many kinds of wisdom are there, how is it developed, and what is the benefit of developing it — and begins by narrowing an intentionally broad term: among wisdom's countless forms, only insight-knowledge associated with a wholesome mind is meant here. Its defining sense, "wisdom" from "knowing thoroughly" (pajānana), is unpacked through a vivid three-person comparison — an unlettered child, an ordinary villager, and a money-changer's expert eye — each seeing the very same pile of coins with a strikingly different depth of understanding, mapped directly onto the three degrees of knowing found in perception, consciousness, and wisdom itself.

Ru-Yi Translation

Now, since a monk endowed with a firmer development of concentration, achieved with the benefit of the direct knowledges as already described, is meant by the phrase "a man, grounded in virtue and wise, should develop mind and wisdom" — herein, the concentration indicated under the heading of "mind" has now been developed in every respect. Wisdom is to be developed next. Because it was taught there with such extreme brevity, it is not easy even to understand, let alone to develop. Therefore, in order to show its detail and its method of development, the following is the task at hand: what is wisdom, in what sense is it "wisdom," what are its characteristic, function, manifestation, and proximate cause, how many kinds of wisdom are there, how should it be developed, and what is the benefit of developing wisdom?

Here is the answer. "What is wisdom?" Wisdom is manifold, of various kinds. An answer that set out to describe all of it would fail to accomplish the intended purpose, and would only lead to further distraction. Therefore, we speak here with reference only to what is intended: insight-knowledge associated with a wholesome mind is wisdom.

"In what sense is it wisdom?" It is wisdom in the sense of knowing thoroughly. What is this "knowing thoroughly"? It is knowing in a variety of ways, distinguished by the mode of perceiving and of cognizing. For although perception, consciousness, and wisdom share the state of knowing, perception is merely the perceiving of an object as "blue" or "yellow" — it is unable to bring about the penetration of its characteristic as "impermanent, suffering, not-self." Consciousness knows the object as "blue" or "yellow," and does bring about the penetration of its characteristic, but, however far it strains, it is unable to bring about the manifestation of the path. Wisdom, in the manner already stated, both knows the object and brings about the penetration of its characteristic, and, straining further still, brings about the manifestation of the path as well.

For just as, when a pile of coins is placed on a money-changer's counter, and three people — an unlettered child, an ordinary villager, and a money-changer — look at it: the unlettered child knows only the coins' varied, elaborate, long, square, or round shape, but does not know, "this is deemed a treasure, for people's use and enjoyment." The ordinary villager knows their varied, elaborate shape, and also that, "this is deemed a treasure, for people's use and enjoyment" — but does not know this further distinction: "this one is genuine, this one is counterfeit, this one is half-alloyed." The money-changer knows all of these ways, and, knowing, knows by looking at the coin, by hearing the sound when it is struck, by smelling it, by tasting it, by holding it in the hand — and knows, "it was made in such-and-such a village, market-town, city, mountain, or riverbank," and even, "it was made by such-and-such a craftsman" — exactly so should this matter be understood.

For perception is like the unlettered child's seeing of the coin, since it grasps only the mere manner of the object's appearing by way of blue and the like. Consciousness is like the villager's seeing of the coin, since it grasps the mode of the object by way of blue and the like, and, further still, brings about the penetration of its characteristic. Wisdom is like the money-changer's seeing of the coin, since, having grasped the mode of the object by way of blue and the like, and having brought about the penetration of its characteristic, it brings about, further still, the manifestation of the path. Therefore, this knowing in a variety of ways, distinguished by the mode of perceiving and of cognizing, should be understood as this "knowing thoroughly." It is with reference to this that it was said, "it is wisdom in the sense of knowing thoroughly."

But this wisdom does not occur, absolutely, wherever perception and consciousness occur. When it does occur, however, it is so inseparable from those states that, on account of the impossibility of separating them out and saying, "this is perception, this is consciousness, this is wisdom," it is subtle and hard to see. Hence the Venerable Nāgasena said, "a difficult thing, great king, was done by the Blessed One." "What difficult thing, venerable Nāgasena, was done by the Blessed One?" "A difficult thing, great king, was done by the Blessed One, in that he declared the determination of the formless mental and mind-associated states occurring with respect to a single object: 'this is contact, this is feeling, this is perception, this is volition, this is consciousness.'"

"What are its characteristic, function, manifestation, and proximate cause?" Herein, wisdom has the characteristic of penetrating the true nature of things. Its function is to destroy the darkness of delusion that conceals the true nature of things. It is manifested as non-confusion. But since it is said, "one who is concentrated knows and sees things as they really are," its proximate cause is concentration.

Reading note — Ru-Yi

The three-person coin simile does more than illustrate a hierarchy of knowing — it insists that perception, consciousness, and wisdom are not separate faculties operating on different objects, but three depths of engagement with the very same object, each capable of everything the previous one could do, plus something more: the villager doesn't lose the child's shape-recognition, and the money-changer doesn't lose the villager's practical recognition, but adds an expert's further discrimination on top.

Nāgasena's remark to King Milinda — that separating contact, feeling, perception, volition, and consciousness within a single moment of experience was itself "a difficult thing" for the Buddha to have done — is a striking admission that this analytical vocabulary, however precisely it gets defined in the pages that follow, describes something that ordinary introspection cannot actually observe as separate; the distinctions are real, but they require the Buddha's own extraordinary penetration to draw at all.

Key vocabulary — for Pāli learners
pajānana

knowing thoroughly, discerning — pa- (intensive prefix) + jānana (knowing); the etymological basis for "paññā" (wisdom) itself, distinguished here from the shallower sañjānana (perceiving) and vijānana (cognizing).

heraññika

a money-changer, a coin-appraiser — the expert figure in this section's central simile, whose ability to authenticate a coin by sight, sound, smell, taste, and touch alike models wisdom's own multi-faceted penetration of an object's true nature.

asammohapaccupaṭṭhāna

manifested as non-confusion — asammoha (non-delusion) + paccupaṭṭhāna (manifestation); one of wisdom's four defining features in the standard characteristic/function/manifestation/proximate-cause framework used throughout this treatise.

Classifying Wisdom: From a Single Kind to Six Different Pairs

The chapter's fifth question — how many kinds of wisdom are there — opens a long classificatory survey, moving methodically from the simplest possible answer to increasingly fine subdivisions. Taken purely by its own defining characteristic, wisdom is of a single kind: the penetration of things' true nature. But it admits six distinct two-fold divisions depending on which contrast is drawn — mundane against supramundane, associated with the taints against free of them, delimiting name against delimiting form, accompanied by joy against accompanied by equanimity, and belonging to the "seeing" stage of the path against the subsequent "developing" stages.

Ru-Yi Translation

"How many kinds of wisdom are there?" By its characteristic of penetrating the true nature of things, it is, firstly, of a single kind. By way of the mundane and the supramundane, it is of two kinds. Likewise by way of being associated with the taints or otherwise, by way of delimiting name and form, by way of being accompanied by joy or equanimity, and by way of the "seeing" and "developing" stages. It is of three kinds by way of being born of thinking, of learning, or of development. Likewise by way of taking a limited, exalted, or measureless object, by way of skill in gain, in loss, and in means, and by way of an internal, external, or both internal-and-external orientation. It is of four kinds by way of knowledge with respect to the four truths, and by way of the four kinds of analytical knowledge.

Herein, the single-kind division is self-evident. In the two-fold division: that associated with the mundane path is mundane; that associated with the supramundane path is supramundane. Thus it is of two kinds by way of the mundane and the supramundane.

In the second pair: that which is itself an object of the taints is "with taints"; that which is not is "without taints." In meaning, this is the same as the mundane-supramundane pair. That associated with the taints is "with taints"; that dissociated from them is "without taints" — the same method applies throughout. Thus it is of two kinds by way of being with or without taints.

In the third pair: the wisdom that delimits the four formless aggregates, for one wishing to begin insight, is "wisdom delimiting name." The wisdom that delimits the form aggregate is "wisdom delimiting form." Thus it is of two kinds by way of delimiting name and form.

In the fourth pair: the wisdom in the two sense-sphere wholesome minds, and in the sixteen path-minds pertaining to the fourfold-jhāna method, is "accompanied by joy." The wisdom in the two sense-sphere wholesome minds, and in the four path-minds pertaining to the fifth jhāna, is "accompanied by equanimity." Thus it is of two kinds by way of being accompanied by joy or by equanimity.

In the fifth pair: the wisdom of the first path is the "seeing" stage. The wisdom of the remaining three paths is the "developing" stage. Thus it is of two kinds by way of the seeing and developing stages.

Reading note — Ru-Yi

The sheer number of independent two-way cuts offered here — mundane/supramundane, with/without taints, name-delimiting/form-delimiting, joy/equanimity, seeing/developing — makes a point by its very structure: wisdom is not a single scale running from "less" to "more," but a single capacity that can be sliced along several genuinely different axes at once, each cut answering a different question about the very same underlying faculty.

The name/form pairing quietly announces this chapter's own coming agenda: distinguishing what is form (rūpa) from what is mind (nāma) is presented here as a basic classificatory fact about wisdom itself, before the chapter goes on to spend the bulk of its length doing exactly that work in exhaustive technical detail for each of the five aggregates.

Key vocabulary — for Pāli learners
sāsava / anāsava

with taints / without taints — sa- (with) or an- (without) + āsava (a taint, an influx); one of the standard mundane/supramundane distinctions applied throughout Abhidhamma classification, here to wisdom itself.

dassanabhūmi / bhāvanābhūmi

the ground of seeing / the ground of developing — dassana (seeing) and bhāvanā (developing, cultivation) + bhūmi (ground, stage); referring to the first path-moment (stream-entry) versus the three remaining paths that refine what has already been seen.

nāmavavatthāpana / rūpavavatthāpana

delimiting name / delimiting form — vavatthāpana (determination, delimitation); the specific analytical task, central to beginning insight meditation, of distinguishing the four mental aggregates from the material aggregate.

Three-Fold Wisdom: Born of Thinking, of Learning, and of Development

The survey continues into wisdom's three-fold divisions, opening with what may be its single most influential classification: wisdom born of one's own reflection, wisdom born of having heard it from another, and wisdom born of actual meditative development reaching full absorption — a threefold hierarchy the Vibhaṅga itself defines with care, before the chapter moves through three further triads: wisdom whose object is limited (ordinary mundane insight), exalted (still mundane, but ranging over fine-material and immaterial states), or measureless (supramundane insight taking Nibbāna itself as object); skill in what leads to gain, skill in what leads to loss, and skill in the situational means that make either gain or loss occur; and finally wisdom that begins from one's own aggregates, from another's, or from both together.

Ru-Yi Translation

Among the triads, in the first: wisdom gained without hearing it from another, being accomplished by one's own reflection, is "born of thinking." Wisdom gained by hearing it from another, being accomplished by way of learning, is "born of learning." Wisdom accomplished, in either case, by way of development and reaching absorption is "born of development." For it was said: "herein, what is wisdom born of thinking? With respect to fields of work or crafts undertaken through effort, or fields of specialized knowledge undertaken through effort, or with respect to the ownership of one's deeds, or in conformity with the truths — 'form is impermanent,' or 'feeling ... perception ... formations ... consciousness is impermanent' — whatever conforming acceptance, view, liking, opinion, reflective preference, or approval of that kind one gains without having heard it from another, this is called wisdom born of thinking ... having heard it, this is called wisdom born of learning. All the wisdom of one who has attained absorption is wisdom born of development." Thus it is of three kinds by way of being born of thinking, of learning, or of development.

In the second triad: wisdom occurring with respect to sense-sphere phenomena has a limited object. That occurring with respect to fine-material and immaterial-sphere phenomena has an exalted object — this is mundane insight. That occurring with respect to Nibbāna has a measureless object — this is supramundane insight. Thus it is of three kinds by way of a limited, exalted, or measureless object.

In the third triad: "gain" means increase, and this is of two kinds — by the removal of harm and by the arising of benefit. Skill in that is "skill in gain." As it is said: "herein, what is skill in gain? For one attending to these things, unarisen unwholesome states do not arise, and arisen unwholesome states are abandoned; or, for one attending to these things, unarisen wholesome states arise, and arisen wholesome states proceed to growth, fullness, development, and completion — whatever wisdom, discernment ... non-delusion, investigation of states, right view there is with respect to this, this is called skill in gain."

"Loss," on the other hand, means decrease, and this too is of two kinds — by the removal of benefit and by the arising of harm. Skill in that is "skill in loss." As it is said: "herein, what is skill in loss? For one attending to these things, unarisen wholesome states do not arise" and so on.

But in every case, the situational skill that occurs, at that very moment, with respect to the means that bring about this or that particular state, is called "skill in means." As it is said: "all wisdom that serves as a means there is skill in means." Thus it is of three kinds by way of skill in gain, in loss, and in means.

In the fourth triad: insight-wisdom begun by taking hold of one's own aggregates has an internal orientation. That begun by taking hold of another's aggregates, or external form not bound up with a faculty, has an external orientation. That begun by taking hold of both has an internal-and-external orientation. Thus it is of three kinds by way of an internal, external, or internal-and-external orientation.

Reading note — Ru-Yi

The cintāmayā/sutamayā/bhāvanāmayā triad is quietly egalitarian about where genuine wisdom can start: it explicitly allows for wisdom reached by one's own unaided reflection, with no teacher's testimony involved at all, placing it on equal footing with learning from another — the real hierarchy in this triad is not about the wisdom's origin but about whether it has been carried all the way through to actual meditative absorption, the only criterion that promotes either starting point into "wisdom born of development."

The gain/loss/means triad is a rare case of this treatise defining a virtue purely in terms of consequences tracked in real time: "skill in means" isn't a separate technique alongside skill in gain and loss, but the moment-by-moment situational judgment of which specific action, right now, will actually produce the gain or avoid the loss already identified in the abstract.

Four-Fold Wisdom: The Four Truths, and the Four Kinds of Analytical Knowledge

The classification survey reaches its final, four-fold divisions: knowledge with respect to each of the Four Noble Truths in turn, and the four kinds of analytical knowledge (paṭisambhidā) — knowledge of meaning, of the underlying condition, of language, and of ready wit. The commentary defines "meaning" precisely as a matter of effect (whatever arises from a condition, Nibbāna, the sense of what is spoken, and the fruit and function of kamma), and "the underlying condition" as a matter of cause (any cause producing a result, the noble path, speech itself, and both wholesome and unwholesome kamma) — a pairing then confirmed directly from the Abhidhamma's own treatment of the Four Truths, where knowledge of suffering and of cessation is knowledge of meaning (both are effects), while knowledge of suffering's origin and of the path is knowledge of the underlying condition (both are causes). Knowledge of language is analytical mastery of the single, invariant Māgadhī root-language of all beings, capable of recognizing correct grammatical form the instant it is heard; knowledge of ready wit is knowledge that takes the other three kinds of knowledge themselves as its own object.

Ru-Yi Translation

Among the tetrads, in the first: knowledge occurring with respect to the truth of suffering is "knowledge of suffering." Knowledge occurring with respect to the origin of suffering is "knowledge of the origin of suffering." Knowledge occurring with respect to the cessation of suffering is "knowledge of the cessation of suffering." Knowledge occurring with respect to the path leading to the cessation of suffering is "knowledge of the path leading to the cessation of suffering." Thus it is of four kinds by way of knowledge with respect to the four truths.

In the second tetrad: the four analytical knowledges are the four knowledges reaching their own distinct classification with respect to meaning and the rest. For it was said: "knowledge with respect to meaning is analytical knowledge of meaning. Knowledge with respect to the underlying condition is analytical knowledge of the underlying condition. Knowledge with respect to the correct linguistic expression of that meaning and that condition is analytical knowledge of language. Knowledge with respect to those very knowledges is analytical knowledge of ready wit."

Herein, "meaning," in brief, is a term for effect. For since an effect is reached, attained, arrived at, in accordance with its cause, it is called "meaning." By way of classification, however, these five things should be understood as "meaning": whatever arises from a condition, Nibbāna, the sense of what is spoken, resultant fruit, and functional consciousness. The knowledge that reaches its own distinct classification with respect to that meaning, for one reviewing that meaning, is analytical knowledge of meaning. "The underlying condition," too, in brief, is a term for a cause. For since a cause establishes, causes to occur, or enables the attainment of this or that effect, it is called "the underlying condition." By way of classification, however, these five things should be understood as "the underlying condition": any cause that produces a fruit, the noble path, speech, and both wholesome and unwholesome kamma. The knowledge that reaches its own distinct classification with respect to that underlying condition, for one reviewing it, is analytical knowledge of the underlying condition.

For this very meaning is shown, analyzed, in the Abhidhamma thus: "knowledge with respect to suffering is analytical knowledge of meaning. Knowledge with respect to the origin of suffering is analytical knowledge of the underlying condition. Knowledge with respect to a cause is analytical knowledge of the underlying condition. Knowledge with respect to the fruit of a cause is analytical knowledge of meaning ... knowledge with respect to aging-and-death is analytical knowledge of meaning. Knowledge with respect to the origin of aging-and-death is analytical knowledge of the underlying condition ... knowledge with respect to the cessation of formations is analytical knowledge of meaning. Knowledge with respect to the path leading to the cessation of formations is analytical knowledge of the underlying condition. Here, a monk knows the teaching — the discourses, the mixed prose-and-verse ... the questions-and-answers. This is called analytical knowledge of the underlying condition. He knows the meaning of each of these various utterances: 'this is the meaning of this utterance, this is the meaning of that utterance.' This is called analytical knowledge of meaning," and so on.

"Knowledge with respect to the correct linguistic expression of that meaning and condition" is the invariant, natural linguistic usage with respect to that meaning and that condition. Upon merely hearing that expression, that speech, that utterance pronounced, the knowledge that reaches its own distinct classification with respect to that natural, so-called grammatically-correct expression — the Māgadhī root-language of all beings — recognizing, "this is natural correct usage, this is not natural correct usage," is analytical knowledge of language. For one who has attained analytical knowledge of language, upon merely hearing an expression such as "phasso, vedanā" ("contact, feeling"), knows, "this is natural correct usage" — but upon hearing something like "phassā vedano," knows, "this is not natural correct usage."

"Knowledge with respect to those very knowledges": the knowledge that, everywhere, takes knowledge itself as its object, for one reviewing it, is knowledge with knowledge as its object; or, the knowledge that ranges in detail over the aforementioned three knowledges, by way of its own proper domain and function, is analytical knowledge of ready wit. This is the meaning.

Reading note — Ru-Yi

The elegant cause/effect symmetry mapping the Four Truths onto just two of the four paṭisambhidā — suffering and cessation as "meaning" (both effects), origin and path as "the underlying condition" (both causes) — reveals that the Four Truths themselves are structured as two cause-effect pairs rather than four independent items: origin causes suffering, path causes cessation, and analytical knowledge of meaning versus condition is simply the general framework of which this specific doctrinal structure is one instance.

The claim that Māgadhī functions as "the root-language of all beings," such that its correct forms can be recognized instantly and infallibly by anyone possessing this particular analytical knowledge, reflects this commentarial tradition's own linguistic self-understanding — the language of the canon is treated not as one human language among others but as reality's own natural grammatical order, which incorrect usage (like the example given) simply fails to match.

How Analytical Knowledge Is Actually Reached: Two Lists of Conditions

Having defined the four analytical knowledges, the commentary asks where exactly they arise, and offers a genuinely important qualification: they belong not only to fully accomplished Arahants, but also to trainees still on the path, as the named examples of Elder Ānanda, the householder Citta, the lay devotee Dhammika, the householder Upāli, and the laywoman Khujjuttarā make clear. Five distinguishing conditions are named — attainment, mastery of the texts, hearing the Dhamma with due respect, thorough questioning about difficult points, and prior practice reaching all the way to the threshold of the path in a former Buddha's dispensation — before an alternative list of seven conditions is offered by other teachers, adding broad learning, mastery of regional dialects, association with learned elders, and good friendship to the mix. Buddhas and Paccekabuddhas reach these knowledges through prior practice and attainment alone; disciples may reach them through any combination of these causes, but never through a dedicated meditation subject set aside specifically for that purpose.

Reading note — Ru-Yi

The explicit naming of lay figures — the householders Citta and Upāli, the laywoman Khujjuttarā — alongside Elder Ānanda as trainees who have nonetheless attained the analytical knowledges is a quiet but firm correction to any assumption that this highest tier of discriminative understanding belongs to monastics alone; the text's own examples insist that stream-entry or a higher trainee stage, not ordination, is the operative qualification.

The insistence that "there is no separate pursuit of a meditation subject dedicated specifically to the attainment of analytical knowledge" is a significant methodological point: unlike the jhānas or the direct knowledges treated in the two preceding chapters, each with its own dedicated kasiṇa or technique, the analytical knowledges arise only as a by-product of a broader constellation of causes — learning, practice, and noble attainment — never as the direct target of a specific exercise.

Ground, Root, and Body: The Architecture of the Whole Wisdom Section

Before answering the sixth and final question — how wisdom should actually be developed — the commentary steps back to lay out the structural plan governing everything from here through the end of the entire treatise, using a striking bodily metaphor. The states classified under aggregates, sense bases, elements, faculties, truths, and dependent origination form wisdom's "ground" — the terrain that must first be surveyed and learned. The two purifications already established, purification of virtue and purification of mind, form its "root." And the five remaining purifications — of view, of overcoming doubt, of knowledge and vision of what is and is not the path, of knowledge and vision of the way, and of knowledge and vision itself — form its "body." Development proceeds by first gaining full familiarity with the ground through learning and inquiry, then perfecting the two root purifications, and only then building up the five purifications that constitute the body proper — a plan that, in effect, is nothing less than a table of contents for the remainder of the Visuddhimagga.

Reading note — Ru-Yi

This single passage functions as a map of the entire remainder of the Visuddhimagga, and its bodily metaphor is worth taking seriously as an actual argument, not mere decoration: a body cannot stand without ground beneath it, and a body without roots planted in that ground has nothing to draw on — the sequence is not arbitrary pedagogy but a claim about genuine dependency, where each later purification presupposes everything named before it.

Naming purification of virtue and purification of mind — already fully treated across the treatise's first thirteen chapters — as merely the "root" of wisdom, rather than wisdom's own substance, quietly reframes everything covered so far: virtue and concentration were never the destination of this whole project, only the necessary foundation making the five remaining purifications, and genuine liberating insight, possible at all.

The Aggregate of Form: Twenty-Eight Kinds, and What Really Distinguishes an Eye From an Ear

The detailed treatment of the ground begins with the form aggregate — anything at all having the characteristic of being "deformed" by cold, heat, and the like. Form divides first into the four great elements themselves (already treated in chapter eleven) and twenty-four kinds of "derived" form depending on them, starting with the five sense faculties. Each faculty receives an identical four-part definition — characteristic, function, manifestation, proximate cause — built from its own specific sensitivity to a specific class of object. A genuine scholarly debate then follows: what actually makes the eye sensitive to color, the ear to sound, and so on, when all five are equally composed of the same four elements? Two rival theories (that a predominance of the fire-element produces the eye, or that a predominance of the space-element does) are politely but firmly dismissed — asked to produce their canonical citation, and unable to — before the correct answer is given: it is kamma alone, not any variation in the elements themselves, that differentiates one faculty from another, since a genuine elemental difference would prevent the delicate faculty from arising at all.

Their characteristics, functions, and manifestations were stated in the section on determining the four elements. Derived form is of twenty-four kinds: the eye, the ear, the nose, the tongue, the body, visible form, sound, smell, taste, the female faculty, the male faculty, the life faculty, the heart-basis, bodily intimation, verbal intimation, the space-element, buoyancy of form, malleability of form, wieldiness of form, growth of form, continuity of form, aging of form, and impermanence of form, and physical nutriment.

Its function is to draw in tangible objects. It is manifested as the support of body-consciousness. Its proximate cause is element-produced matter born of kamma originating in the desire to touch.

Now, some say: "the eye is the sensitivity of elements in which fire predominates; the ear, nose, and tongue are the sensitivities of elements in which wind, earth, and water respectively predominate; the body is the sensitivity of all of them." Others say: "the eye is the sensitivity of elements in which fire predominates; the ear, nose, tongue, and body are the sensitivities of elements in which cavity-wind, water, and earth predominate." They should be told, "bring the canonical text." Assuredly, they will not find any such text. Some, however, show as their reason: "because color, and the rest, are supported by the qualities of fire and the other elements." They should be told: "but who says that color and the rest are qualities of fire and the other elements in the first place? For among elements that occur inseparably, it is not possible to say, 'this is this one's quality, this is that one's quality.'" Since neither of these positions holds, this special theorizing about the supporting elements should be abandoned; rather, it should be accepted that "just as, even without any real difference, the color, taste, and so forth of the elements within a single, undifferentiated cluster are dissimilar to one another, so too the eye's sensitivity and the rest occur even without there being any special differentiating cause elsewhere."

What, then, is it that is not shared in common among them? Kamma alone is their differentiating cause. Therefore, their difference is due to a difference in kamma, not to a difference in the elements. For where there is a difference in the elements, sensitivity itself does not even arise — sensitivity belongs to what is uniform, not to what is non-uniform: so say the ancient teachers.

And among these, thus differentiated by a difference in kamma, the eye and the ear grasp an object that has not reached them, since they are the cause of consciousness only with respect to an object not in contact with their own support. The nose, tongue, and body grasp an object that has reached them, both by way of their support and by themselves, since they are the cause of consciousness only with respect to an object that has come into contact with their own support.

Reading note — Ru-Yi

The debate's resolution method is a small model of how this commentarial tradition adjudicates disputes generally: the first move is always "bring the canonical text," and only when no text can be produced does the discussion move to reasoned argument — and even then, the winning argument is not a positive elemental theory at all, but a demonstration that elemental composition cannot possibly explain the difference, since the elements in any single cluster of matter are inseparable and cannot be individually assigned as "belonging" to one derived quality over another.

Locating the true differentiating cause in kamma alone, rather than in any physical or elemental variation, is a striking metaphysical commitment: two physically identical clusters of the four elements can give rise to an eye in one case and an ear in another purely because of the different kamma that produced each — a genuinely non-physicalist explanation for what looks, on the surface, like a physical difference.

The Eye Like a Blue Lotus Petal: A Physical Portrait of the Five Senses

Turning from function to physical description, the commentary paints an almost anatomical portrait of each sense organ, beginning with the eye familiar from ordinary description — dark lashes, black and white circles, resembling a blue lotus petal — before locating the actual sensitive matter itself: a minute point, no larger than a louse's head, positioned at the center of the black circle, sustained like a royal prince by four attending nurses (the four elements performing their supporting, binding, maturing, and moving functions), nourished by season, mind, and food, and maintained by life. Elder Sāriputta's own verse is quoted to drive home just how minute this sensitive point really is, before parallel descriptions follow for the ear (in the shape of a finger-ring, within a ring of fine brown hairs), the nose (shaped like a goat's hoof), the tongue (shaped like the tip of a lotus petal), and the body (pervading the entire organism like oil soaked into a cotton swab) — each one, the passage closes, naturally inclined toward its own proper domain, the way a snake inclines toward an anthill or a vulture toward carrion.

Reading note — Ru-Yi

The four-nurses simile for the elements' supportive role toward the eye's sensitivity is a small but telling instance of this text's recurring technique: rather than describing an abstract physical or causal relationship directly, it reaches for a human social relationship — royal attendants caring for a prince — that makes the abstract dependency immediately vivid and, crucially, hierarchical: the sensitivity itself is the prince, precious and delicate, while the elements are merely its attendants.

Sāriputta's own verse, quoted as an authoritative confirmation rather than mere ornament, closes the description of the eye specifically because its claim (the actual sensitive point is louse-head-sized) is the single most surprising and counter-intuitive detail in the whole passage — the visible eye everyone can see is not where the real physiological action happens at all.

Visible Form, Sound, Smell, Taste — and the Faculties of Femininity and Masculinity

The survey turns from the five sense faculties to their objects — visible form, sound, smell, and taste each receiving the same characteristic/function/manifestation/proximate-cause treatment as their corresponding faculty, defined by its capacity to strike against and become the domain of its matching consciousness. (Tangible object, notably, needs no separate definition here, since it was already treated as three of the four great elements themselves back in chapter eleven.) The chapter then turns to the two faculties of sex — femininity and masculinity — each defined by its characteristic of producing the corresponding physical state, its function of proclaiming "this is a woman" or "this is a man," and its manifestation as the cause of the sex-linked bodily marks, signs, occupation, and demeanor that make gender outwardly recognizable. Both faculties, the commentary notes with careful precision, pervade the entire body exactly as body-sensitivity does, yet occupy no single delimitable location the way body-sensitivity does not occupy any single "here" either — and, unlike color and the other sense objects, do not intermix or interfere with one another at all.

Life, the Heart-Basis, and the Two Intimations: Body-Gesture and Speech

Three more kinds of derived form follow. The life faculty maintains co-arisen material states exactly as already explained for its mental counterpart in the previous chapter — the sole difference being that this version sustains matter rather than mind — while the heart-basis is identified as the physical support for the mind-element and mind-consciousness-element, located within the heart itself, nourished and maintained the same way every other sense-basis is. The two "intimations" then follow: bodily intimation is the specific alteration in the wind-element, arisen from mind, that makes the body stiffen, hold steady, or move in a way that communicates one's actual intention — recognized, tellingly, through the very bodily movement it produces, the way water's presence in a forest is inferred from a raised, waterlogged patch of ground rather than seen directly; verbal intimation is the parallel alteration in the earth-element that produces the audible sound of speech, similarly inferred from the sound it produces rather than perceived as some separate, hidden thing.

Space, Buoyancy, and the Riverbank Well: The Remaining Derived Forms

The catalogue continues through eight further kinds of derived form. Space-element delimits matter, making possible the very distinctions of "above," "below," and "across" among material things. Buoyancy, malleability, and wieldiness of matter — three qualities that, despite always occurring together, remain genuinely distinct — respectively counteract heaviness, stiffness, and unfitness for action, illustrated by a healthy person's lightness, well-worked leather's softness, and refined gold's workability. Growth and continuity of matter are shown, through a memorable riverbank simile, to be simply two stages of the very same underlying process: growth is matter's first arising, exactly like water first appearing in a newly dug well beside a river; continuity is that same matter's repeated re-arising thereafter, exactly like the well's water reaching its full level and then continuing to flow. Aging and impermanence close the list of derived-matter qualities proper — the former shown only through visible marks like broken teeth, since more subtle aging remains hidden — before physical nutriment completes the twenty-four, defined simply as the nutritive essence by which beings are sustained.

Twenty-Eight Kinds of Matter, No More and No Fewer — and Five Ways to Sort Them

With the twenty-four kinds of derived form now fully catalogued, the commentary firmly closes the list at twenty-eight (adding back the four great elements), explicitly rejecting a rival commentarial tradition's proposal to add strength, generative matter, birth-matter, disease-matter, and torpor-matter as separate categories — torpor is dismissed outright by direct canonical citation, while the other four are shown to already be subsumed under categories already listed. The chapter then works through five successive classification schemes applied to this settled inventory of twenty-eight: a single all-encompassing classification (matter has no root-cause and no association with root-causes); a series of overlapping two-fold divisions (internal/external, gross/subtle, far/near, produced/unproduced, sensitive/non-sensitive, faculty/non-faculty, clung-to/not-clung-to); three-fold divisions (visible-and-impinging, invisible-and-impinging, invisible-and-non-impinging; and separately, kamma-born, mind-born, food-born, or season-born); four-fold divisions (seen, heard, sensed, or cognized; matter proper, delimiting matter, alterable matter, or characteristic matter; basis, door, both, or neither); and finally a five-fold division by the number of originating causes, from matter arising from one single cause up to matter that arises from no cause at all, since it is itself merely a characteristic of arising, persisting, and passing away.

By way of the triad beginning with "kamma-born": what is born from kamma is "kamma-born"; what is born from another condition is "non-kamma-born"; what is born from no cause at all is "neither kamma-born nor non-kamma-born." What is born from mind is "mind-born"; what is born from another condition is "non-mind-born"; what is born from no cause at all is "neither mind-born nor non-mind-born." What is born from nutriment is "nutriment-born"; what is born from another condition is "non-nutriment-born"; what is born from no cause at all is "neither nutriment-born nor non-nutriment-born." What is born from season is "season-born"; what is born from another condition is "non-season-born"; what is born from no cause at all is "neither season-born nor non-season-born." Thus it is of three kinds by way of the triad beginning with "kamma-born."

Again, it is of four kinds by way of the tetrad beginning with "seen" and the tetrad beginning with "matter proper" and the tetrad beginning with "basis." Herein, the visible-form base is called "seen," being the object of sight; the sound base is called "heard," being the object of hearing; the triad of smell, taste, and the tangible is called "sensed," being the object of the faculties that grasp what has reached them; the rest is called "cognized," being the object of consciousness alone. Thus, firstly, it is of four kinds by way of the tetrad beginning with "seen." Herein, produced matter is called "matter proper"; the space-element is called "delimiting matter"; bodily intimation and the rest, up to and including wieldiness, are called "alterable matter"; birth, aging, and dissolution are called "characteristic matter." Thus it is of four kinds by way of the tetrad beginning with "matter proper." What is here called heart-matter is "basis," not "door." The two intimations are "door," not "basis." Sensitive matter is both "basis" and "door." The rest is "neither basis nor door." Thus it is of four kinds by way of the tetrad beginning with "basis."

Again, it is of five kinds by way of "born from one," "born from two," "born from three," "born from four," and "born from no cause." Herein, what is kamma-born alone, and what is mind-born alone, is called "born from one." Among these, faculty-matter, together with the heart-basis, is kamma-born alone. The two intimations are mind-born alone. What is born both from mind and from season is called "born from two" — this is the sound base alone. What is born from season, mind, and nutriment is called "born from three" — this is only the triad of buoyancy and the rest. What is born from all four — kamma and the rest — is called "born from four"; this is the remainder, excluding characteristic matter. Characteristic matter, however, is "born from no cause." Why? Because there is no arising of an arising, and the other two are merely the alteration and maturation of what has already arisen.

Reading note — Ru-Yi

The rejection of "torpor-matter" is settled by a single decisive citation — a verse describing the Buddha as free of all hindrances, torpor (middha) among them — showing that if torpor is genuinely absent from an awakened being while their body still continues, torpor cannot itself be a form of matter, since matter would have to persist regardless; this is a clean example of doctrine constraining physiology, rather than the reverse.

The final observation about characteristic matter — that "there is no arising of an arising" — is a small but genuinely deep logical point: birth, aging, and dissolution are themselves the marks of every other kind of matter's arising and passing, and so cannot themselves be said to arise from a further cause without triggering an infinite regress; the commentary simply declares them uncaused, exempting the markers of causation from needing a cause of their own.

The Aggregate of Consciousness: Twenty-One Kinds of Wholesome Mind

The commentary now turns to consciousness, treated first among the remaining four aggregates precisely because understanding it makes everything else easier to understand. Feeling, perception, and formations are each briefly defined by their own characteristic mark — feeling by what is felt, perception by what is perceived, formations by what is put together — before consciousness itself is defined by cognizing, and immediately divided into three moral categories: wholesome, unwholesome, and indeterminate. Wholesome consciousness divides again by plane into sense-sphere, fine-material, immaterial, and supramundane. Sense-sphere wholesome consciousness alone yields eight distinct minds, generated by every possible combination of three independent variables — accompanied by joy or by equanimity, associated with knowledge or dissociated from it, and unprompted or prompted — illustrated by the different mental qualities of someone giving alms cheerfully and confidently versus half-heartedly or only after being urged by others. Fine-material consciousness yields five minds corresponding to the five jhāna factors progressively dropped away; immaterial consciousness, four, one for each formless attainment; and supramundane consciousness, four, one for each of the four paths — twenty-one wholesome minds in total.

Reading note — Ru-Yi

The four examples given for sense-sphere wholesome consciousness — confident unprompted giving, hesitant unprompted giving, spontaneous joyful giving to a monk one happens to know, and giving only after relatives insist — form a small ladder of decreasing spiritual quality within generosity itself, all still genuinely wholesome, showing that this eightfold classification isn't sorting good deeds from bad ones but discriminating fine gradations of quality even among deeds that are uniformly good.

The decision to begin the whole khandha-by-khandha exposition with consciousness, "since, once the aggregate of consciousness is understood, the others become easy to understand," reverses the canonical listing order (rūpa, vedanā, saññā, saṅkhāra, viññāṇa) already used earlier in this very chapter — a pedagogical choice the commentary makes explicit rather than silently changing, trusting that consciousness's centrality to the other three mental aggregates justifies treating it first even where the standard order treats it last.

Twelve Kinds of Unwholesome Mind: Greed, Hatred, and Delusion Rooted

Unwholesome consciousness, unlike its wholesome counterpart, occupies the sense sphere alone — there is no unwholesome fine-material, immaterial, or supramundane mind — and divides by root into greed-rooted, hatred-rooted, and delusion-rooted. Greed-rooted consciousness yields eight minds through the same joy/equanimity, view-associated/dissociated, and prompted/unprompted variables already seen for generosity, but now illustrated by wrong-view-driven sensual indulgence or outright theft, whether pursued keenly of one's own accord or half-heartedly at another's urging. Hatred-rooted consciousness yields only two minds, since aversion is inherently accompanied by displeasure alone, distinguished only by whether it arises sharply or mildly, unprompted or prompted, in acts like killing. Delusion-rooted consciousness likewise yields only two minds, one associated with doubt and one with restlessness, arising respectively at the moment of failing to reach a decision and at the moment of scattered distraction — twelve unwholesome minds in total.

Reading note — Ru-Yi

The stark asymmetry between greed's eight minds and hatred's mere two is itself a piece of moral psychology worth noticing: greed, in this analysis, is genuinely compatible with joy — one can steal or indulge cheerfully, even happily — while hatred, almost by definitional necessity, can never be accompanied by anything but displeasure, since its very nature as aversion rules out any pairing with joy from the outset.

Delusion-rooted consciousness's two forms — doubt and restlessness — quietly identify two very different ways a mind can fail to function properly: doubt is a failure to settle on any conclusion at all, while restlessness is a failure to stay anchored to any single object, and both, unlike the sharper defilements of greed and hatred, are given only the mild, equanimity-accompanied register, since delusion by itself lacks the intensity that either pleasure or aversion would require.

Indeterminate Consciousness: Thirty-Six Kinds of Result, Twenty Kinds of Function

The third moral category, indeterminate consciousness, splits into resultant consciousness (the ripening of past kamma) and functional consciousness (activity that is neither kammically wholesome nor unwholesome, nor itself a result). Resultant consciousness in the sense sphere further divides into rootless and rooted: the eight rootless resultants comprise the five kinds of sense-consciousness (eye-, ear-, nose-, tongue-, and body-consciousness), each defined by simply cognizing its own corresponding object, plus a receiving mind-element and two investigating mind-consciousness-elements — the pleasant-object investigator occurring at no fewer than five distinct points in the cognitive process, the neutral-object investigator at all five points plus rebirth, life-continuum, and death. The eight rooted resultants mirror the eight sense-sphere wholesome minds exactly, joined by five fine-material, four immaterial, and four supramundane resultants, for thirty-six resultant minds spanning every plane. Functional consciousness, occurring only in Arahants (apart from its two rootless kinds, common to everyone), similarly totals twenty across the three planes where it can occur.

Its proximate cause is the departure of the functional mind-element that has visible form as its object. Ear-, nose-, tongue-, and body-consciousness have the characteristic of cognizing sound and the rest by way of the ear and the other faculties. Their function is to have sound and the rest alone as object. They are manifested as facing toward sound and the rest. Their proximate cause is the departure of the functional mind-elements that have sound and the rest as their object.

Its function is investigating and the like. It is manifested as that very state. Its proximate cause is the heart-basis. The other, on account of occurring with respect to a desirable-neutral object, is associated with equanimity, and, occurring by way of investigation, registration, rebirth-linking, the life-continuum, and death, has five points of occurrence.

Supramundane resultant consciousness is of four kinds, being the fruit-consciousness associated with the four paths. The rooted, however, is of eight kinds by way of joy and the rest, just like the wholesome. Thus, altogether, sense-sphere functional consciousness is of eleven kinds. Fine-material and immaterial functional consciousness, however, are of five and four kinds respectively, just like the wholesome. Thus, altogether, functional consciousness across the three planes is of twenty kinds.

Reading note — Ru-Yi

The two rootless investigating minds' occurrence at "two points" versus "five points" quietly reveals how thoroughly this Abhidhamma model treats a single, unified-seeming moment of perception as an elaborate assembly line: the very same functional role (investigating an object) is performed by structurally distinct mind-moments depending on whether the object was pleasant or merely neutral, and the neutral-object version turns out to do double duty as the resultant consciousness underlying rebirth, the passive background life-continuum, and death itself — three of the most consequential moments in a being's existence, handled by the least emotionally marked of all resultant minds.

Functional consciousness's restriction to Arahants (apart from its two rootless, universally shared kinds) captures something genuinely distinctive about awakened experience in this system: an Arahant still acts, still experiences sense contact, still even smiles — the smile-producing mind is explicitly named — but none of this activity is any longer kammically wholesome or unwholesome, since the very possibility of accumulating further kamma has already ceased for them.

Eighty-Nine Minds in All — and How Rebirth-Linking Actually Happens

Twenty-one wholesome, twelve unwholesome, thirty-six resultant, and twenty functional minds together make eighty-nine kinds of consciousness in all — and the commentary now turns from static classification to dynamic occurrence, tracing exactly how these eighty-nine minds actually arise across fourteen distinct functional modes, beginning with rebirth-linking itself. Which of the resultant minds actually performs rebirth-linking depends entirely on the kind of existence being entered: beings reborn among devas or humans through the power of sense-sphere wholesome kamma take one of eight rooted resultants as their rebirth-consciousness, or, in cases of congenital sexlessness or similar deficiency, a specific weaker two-rooted variant; beings reborn in the fine-material or immaterial realms take one of the corresponding higher resultants, their rebirth-object being the kamma-sign itself rather than the kamma or destiny-sign; and beings reborn in states of woe through unwholesome kamma take the single unwholesome-resultant rootless mind-consciousness-element as their sole rebirth-linking consciousness — nineteen resultant minds capable of performing this function in total.

Reading note — Ru-Yi

The three possible objects at the moment of death — the kamma itself, a sign of the kamma, or a sign of the destination about to be entered — describe three different ways a dying person's mind can be occupied in its final moments, and the commentary's insistence that fine-material and immaterial rebirth always takes only the kamma-sign (never kamma itself or a destiny-sign) reflects the more purely meditative character of the kamma that produces such rebirths, as opposed to the more varied kinds of kamma that can lead to a sense-sphere destination.

From the River's Undisturbed Flow to the Ship Pursued Downstream: The Full Process of Cognition

Having explained rebirth-linking, the commentary traces the remaining thirteen functional modes as a single continuous, moment-by-moment account of ordinary experience. Once rebirth-linking has ceased, the very same kind of resultant consciousness continues to arise again and again, taking the identical object, as the "life-continuum" — compared to an uninterrupted river's flow, or to the countless mind-moments passing, uncounted, through a dreamless sleep. When the senses become receptive to a fresh object, an actual process begins: contact at the eye-door (or any other door) disturbs the life-continuum, an adverting consciousness arises to "cut off" that continuum and orient toward the new object, and then, in strict sequence, seeing (or hearing, smelling, tasting, touching) occurs, followed by receiving, investigating, and determining. Only after determining does a full javana sequence run — six or seven wholesome, unwholesome, or functional mind-moments realizing the object's full impact — and, if the object was exceptionally vivid, one or two final "registration" moments may follow, compared to a boat pursued and briefly caught by the current after the oarsman's rowing has stopped. Life continues in this pattern, life-continuum interrupted by process after process, until the very last life-continuum moment of an existence itself becomes "death" — falling away from that life — after which the entire cycle begins again with a fresh rebirth-linking, except for the Arahant, for whom the falling-away of the death-consciousness is simply the falling-away of consciousness itself, forever.

The Aggregate of Feeling: Five Kinds, Sixty-Two Minds Deep

Feeling — that which is felt — is likewise of a single kind by its own nature, but of three kinds by moral class (wholesome, unwholesome, indeterminate, matching whatever consciousness it accompanies) and, more distinctively, of five kinds by its own intrinsic character: bodily pleasure, bodily pain, mental joy, mental displeasure, and equanimity. The commentary tallies precisely how many of the eighty-nine minds each of these five feelings can accompany — joy alone spans a remarkable sixty-two different consciousnesses, displeasure only the two hatred-rooted minds, and equanimity the remaining fifty-five — before defining each with the same four-part precision used throughout: bodily pleasure nourishes what is associated with it the way water nourishes a plant, bodily pain withers it, mental joy is a properly mental savoring of a desirable object, mental displeasure its mirror-image withering, and equanimity a peaceful middle state, neither elating nor withering what accompanies it.

The Aggregate of Perception: The Carpenter, the Blind Men, and the Deer That Mistook a Scarecrow

Perception — that which perceives — receives the same brief moral classification as feeling (wholesome, unwholesome, indeterminate, always exactly matching the consciousness it accompanies, since no consciousness exists without an accompanying perception) before being defined through three vivid similes. Its function, making a mental sign that becomes the condition for perceiving the same object again later, is compared to carpenters and other craftsmen marking timber so that the same piece can be recognized on a later occasion. Its manifestation, seizing upon whatever aspect an object has been apprehended under, is compared to the born-blind men in the Udāna's famous parable, who each insist an elephant is exactly whatever single part of it they happened to touch. And its proximate cause, a presently appearing object however it happens to appear, is compared to the young deer who mistake a farmer's straw scarecrow for an actual man, purely on the basis of how the object happened to present itself to them.

Reading note — Ru-Yi

The Aggregate of Formations: Contact, Volition, and the Ministers Who Serve the King

The last of the four mental aggregates, formations — that which puts together the formed — comprises everything mental besides feeling, perception, and consciousness itself: fully thirty-six distinct factors accompany the very first kind of sense-sphere wholesome consciousness alone, twenty-seven appearing by their own specific name in the canonical text, four more as occasional additions, and five occurring only some of the time. The exposition opens with contact, the "touching" that brings consciousness together with its object even though it is itself formless, compared to a hideless cow who cannot help but be affected by whatever touches her exposed flesh; and volition, the coordinating, organizing factor compared to a senior pupil or master carpenter who accomplishes both his own task and directs everyone else's, becoming especially evident in urgent action or vivid recollection. Initial thought, sustained thought, and joy are simply cross-referenced to their full treatment back in chapter four's account of the first jhāna, since nothing further needs to be added here. Energy, life, and concentration close this opening cluster — energy being the very state of being vigorous, sustaining its companions against collapse; life sustaining formless states exactly as its material counterpart sustains matter; and concentration placing the mind evenly and rightly on its object, compared to water binding bath-powder into a cohesive mass, or a lamp-flame standing undisturbed in a windless place.

One has faith by means of it, or it itself has faith, or it is simply mere having-faith — hence "faith." It has the characteristic of having faith, or the characteristic of resolute trust. Its function is clarifying, like the water-clearing gem, or launching forward, like one crossing a flood. It is manifested as non-turbidity, or as resolve. Its proximate cause is a thing worthy of faith, or the factors of stream-entry beginning with hearing the true Dhamma. It should be regarded as a hand, as wealth, and as a seed.

One remembers by means of it, or it itself remembers, or it is simply mere remembering — hence "mindfulness." It has the characteristic of not floating away. Its function is non-confusion. Reading note — Ru-Yi

The paired similes for moral shame and moral dread — a well-born wife avoiding misconduct for her own dignity, a courtesan avoiding it for fear of another's censure — are unusually direct about social class and reputation as they actually function in ordinary life, using two contrasting social positions to make a genuinely fine psychological distinction: the same abstention from wrongdoing can be motivated either from within, by self-respect, or from without, by concern for how one will be seen.

Non-greed, non-hatred, and non-delusion are named here, explicitly, as "the root of every wholesome state" — not merely three items among many cetasikas but the causal foundation from which all other wholesome mental factors ultimately grow, giving these three a structural priority that the parallel list of unwholesome roots (greed, hatred, delusion) will mirror exactly when the chapter turns to unwholesome consciousness.

Six Pairs, Twelve Factors: Tranquility, Buoyancy, Malleability, Wieldiness, Proficiency, and Rectitude

Twelve more factors follow, arranged as six mirrored pairs, one member for the "mental body" — the three aggregates of feeling, perception, and formations taken together — and one for consciousness proper. Tranquility calms agitation in both, opposing restlessness and the like. Buoyancy counters heaviness, opposing sloth and torpor. Malleability counters rigidity, opposing the stiffening effects of views and conceit. Wieldiness counters unworkability, opposing the remaining hindrances that make a mind resist being directed toward what is beneficial — likened to the purity of well-refined gold, ready for any use. Proficiency counters sickliness, opposing the faithlessness and other defilements that leave mental states somehow unwell. And rectitude counters crookedness, opposing the deceit and fraud that twist mental states out of true. Each pair, though occurring inseparably from its own kind, is genuinely two things, not one, differentiated by whether it operates on the mental body or on consciousness itself.

Desire-to-Act, Resolve, Attention, and Neutrality — Plus Compassion and the Three Abstinences

The remaining nine factors complete the thirty-six accompanying the first wholesome mind. Desire-to-act, the wish to do something, reaches for its object the way a hand reaches out; resolve settles decisively upon its object like an immovable boundary-post, refusing to waver. Attention comes in three varieties — the kind that channels a mind-door process, the kind that channels a five-door process, and the kind that actually directs consciousness toward its object, a genuine part of the formations aggregate and likened to a charioteer directing well-trained thoroughbreds. Neutrality of mind holds consciousness and its associated states in perfect balance, neither favoring nor opposing, like a charioteer who simply watches evenly matched horses run true without needing to intervene. Compassion and sympathetic joy, treated fully back in the chapter on the divine abidings, appear here only in their sense-sphere form (their absorption-level versions belong instead to fine-material consciousness); and the three abstinences from bodily misconduct, verbal misconduct, and wrong livelihood are shown to share a single characteristic — simply not transgressing their respective misdeed — while remaining distinct in their specific proximate causes. All thirty-six factors, the section closes, accompany this first wholesome mind together; the second differs only in requiring prompting.

Tracking the Thirty-Six Across All Twenty-One Wholesome Minds

Having fully catalogued the thirty-six formations accompanying the first sense-sphere wholesome mind, the commentary now tracks precisely which factors drop away, and which remain, across the remaining twenty wholesome minds. The third and fourth minds, lacking knowledge, lose non-delusion alone; the fifth and sixth, lacking joy, lose the factor of joy itself; the seventh and eighth lose both non-delusion and joy together. The five fine-material minds, moving through the jhāna factors, successively lose the three abstinences (never relevant once absorption is reached), then initial thought, then sustained thought, then joy, arriving at last at an equanimous fifth jhāna; the four immaterial minds follow an identical pattern, differing only in their sphere. The four supramundane path-minds inherit this same fine-material structure but permanently lose compassion and sympathetic joy (their object being restricted to Nibbāna, not living beings) while gaining the fixed, unwavering quality of their three abstinences and their genuinely supramundane status.

Greed-Rooted Formations: The Ape's Sticky Trap, and the Swollen River Rushing to Ruin

Turning to unwholesome formations, the commentary catalogues the seventeen factors accompanying the first greed-rooted mind: thirteen coming down by name, four occasional additions shared with the wholesome list. Moral shame and moral dread are replaced by their negations, defined as simply their opposites; greed itself is compared to sticky ape-glue trapping whatever touches it, to meat thrown in a hot pan clinging to its own destruction, and, most memorably, to a swift-flowing river that, ever swelling with the tributary streams of craving, carries itself straight into the ocean of a woeful rebirth. Delusion is a genuine blindness of mind, concealing an object's true nature the way darkness conceals a room, and stands as the root of every unwholesome state whatsoever. Wrong view clings, unreasoningly, to a mistaken position; restlessness churns like water struck by wind, or ash scattered by a stone's impact. Across the second through eighth greed-rooted minds, the same seventeen factors persist with only small, precise variations — conceit appearing only where wrong view is absent, since the two can never coexist in a single mind.

Among the unwholesome, in the greed-rooted, associated with the first: there are, firstly, thirteen fixed factors coming down by their own name, and four occasional additions — seventeen in all. Herein, the thirteen coming down by name are: contact, volition, initial thought, sustained thought, joy, energy, life, concentration, moral shamelessness, moral recklessness, greed, delusion, and wrong view. The four occasional additions are: desire-to-act, resolve, restlessness, and attention.

Herein, one lacking moral shame is "shameless." The state of being shameless is "moral shamelessness." One lacking moral dread is "moral recklessness." Among these, moral shamelessness has the characteristic of a lack of disgust at bodily misconduct and the like, or the characteristic of shamelessness. Moral recklessness has the characteristic of a lack of timidity toward those same things, or the characteristic of fearlessness.

One is greedy by means of it, or it itself is greedy, or it is simply mere greediness — hence "greed." One is deluded by means of it, or it itself is deluded, or it is simply mere confusion — hence "delusion." Among these, greed has the characteristic of grasping an object, like ape-glue. Its function is sticking, like a piece of meat thrown into a hot pan. It is manifested as non-relinquishment, like the dye of lamp-black. Its proximate cause is seeing enjoyment in things that lead to bondage. It should be regarded as growing, by way of becoming a river of craving, carrying one, like a swift-flowing river, straight to a woeful destination, as to a great ocean.

Delusion has the characteristic of the mind's blindness, or the characteristic of unknowing. Its function is non-penetration, or the concealing of an object's true nature. It is manifested as wrong practice, or as darkness. Its proximate cause is unwise attention. It should be regarded as the root of every unwholesome state.

One sees wrongly by means of it, or it itself sees wrongly, or it is simply mere wrong seeing — hence "wrong view." It has the characteristic of unwise, obstinate adherence. Its function is misapprehension. It is manifested as wrong adherence. Its proximate cause is an unwillingness to see the noble ones and the like. It should be regarded as the worst of all faults.

The state of being agitated is "restlessness." It has the characteristic of non-calm, like water disturbed by the wind's impact. Its function is instability, like a banner or flag fluttering, struck by the wind. It is manifested as turmoil, like ash scattered up by the impact of a stone. Its proximate cause is unwise attention to the mind's lack of calm. It should be regarded as a scattering of the mind.

Thus these seventeen formations should be understood to enter into association with the first unwholesome consciousness. Just as with the first, so too with the second — the only difference here being the state of requiring prompting, and the not-fixed status of sloth and torpor. With the third, excluding wrong view from those stated for the first, the remainder should be understood. Conceit, however, is not fixed here. This is the difference: it has the characteristic of loftiness. Its function is self-assertion. It is manifested as a longing for a banner, as it were. Its proximate cause is greed dissociated from views. It should be regarded as a kind of madness. With the fourth, excluding wrong view from those stated for the second, the remainder should be understood. Here too, conceit occurs among the not-fixed factors. Excluding joy from those stated for the first, the remainder enters into association with the fifth. Just as with the fifth, so too with the sixth — the only difference here being the state of requiring prompting, and the not-fixed status of sloth and torpor. With the seventh, excluding view from those stated for the fifth, the remainder should be understood. Conceit, however, is not fixed here. With the eighth, excluding view from those stated for the sixth, the remainder should be understood. Here too, conceit occurs among the not-fixed factors.

Reading note — Ru-Yi

The observation that conceit occurs only where wrong view is absent encodes a genuinely interesting piece of moral psychology: the commentary treats self-assertion and the specific conviction of a fixed, mistaken doctrinal position as mutually exclusive states in any single mind-moment — one can be arrogant without believing anything false in particular, or hold a wrong view without any accompanying self-inflation, but the two, on this analysis, cannot occur together.

The river-of-craving simile for greed closes the definition with genuine narrative momentum: rather than a static image, it describes an accelerating process — a river that only ever grows larger as it absorbs tributary after tributary, ending not in gentle dissolution but in being swallowed by "a great ocean" that turns out to be a woeful rebirth, making explicit that greed's natural trajectory, left unchecked, is not stasis but escalation toward ruin.

Hatred, Envy, Stinginess, and Doubt: The Remaining Unwholesome and Indeterminate Formations

The catalogue of unwholesome formations closes with hatred-rooted and delusion-rooted minds. Hatred itself is compared to a struck snake, spreading like poison, burning its own support like a forest fire — a state that, once given the chance, seizes it the way an enemy seizes an opening. Envy begrudges another's success purely out of inability to bear it; stinginess conceals one's own possessions, refusing to share them, becoming positively ugly in the mind that harbors it; remorse gnaws over both the bad done and the good left undone, a genuine servitude to the past. Among the delusion-rooted minds, doubt wavers, unable to reach any settled grasp on its object, obstructing genuine practice. The chapter then closes its entire formations survey by tracing the same formations across every kind of indeterminate consciousness — resultant and functional alike, sense-sphere, fine-material, and immaterial — showing that each simply mirrors the corresponding wholesome or unwholesome pattern already established, before formally closing the detailed exposition of all four remaining aggregates.

Ru-Yi Translation

Among the two hatred-rooted minds, associated with the first there are, firstly, eleven fixed factors coming down by name, four occasional additions, and three not-fixed — eighteen in all. Envy, stinginess, and remorse are the three not-fixed factors.

Herein, one is spoiled by means of it, or it itself is spoiled, or it is simply mere spoiling — hence "hatred." It has the characteristic of ferocity, like a struck snake. Its function is spreading, like a drop of poison, or burning its own support, like a forest fire. It is manifested as persecuting, like an enemy who has found his chance. Its proximate cause is the grounds for animosity. It should be regarded as like poison mixed with putrid urine.

The state of envying is "envy." It has the characteristic of begrudging others' successes. Its function is displeasure at those very successes. It is manifested as an aversion toward them. Its proximate cause is others' successes. It should be regarded as a fetter.

The state of being a miser is "stinginess." It has the characteristic of concealing one's own successes, whether already obtained or yet to be obtained. Its function is an inability to bear sharing them in common with others. It is manifested as a shrinking back, or as a state of harsh meanness. Its proximate cause is one's own successes. It should be regarded as a deformity of mind.

A deed badly done is a "bad deed." The state of that is "remorse." It has the characteristic of subsequent regret. Its function is grieving over what was done and what was left undone. It is manifested as repentance. Its proximate cause is what was done and what was left undone. It should be regarded as a kind of servitude.

Thus these eighteen formations should be understood to enter into association with the first hatred-rooted mind. Just as with the first, so too with the second — the only difference here being the state of requiring prompting, and the arising of sloth and torpor among the not-fixed factors.

Among the two delusion-rooted minds, associated with the one accompanied by doubt, there are, firstly, eleven fixed factors coming down by name: contact, volition, initial thought, sustained thought, energy, life, mental steadiness, moral shamelessness, moral recklessness, delusion, and doubt; and two occasional additions, restlessness and attention — thirteen in all.

Herein, "mental steadiness" is a weak concentration amounting to no more than mere occurrence and persistence. The absence of a settled resolution is "doubt." It has the characteristic of wavering. Its function is trembling. It is manifested as indecision, or as the grasping of multiple, uncertain positions. Its proximate cause is unwise attention with respect to the matter doubted. It should be regarded as an obstruction to practice. The rest are exactly as already stated.

Among those associated with restlessness: excluding doubt from those stated for the one associated with doubt, the remainder is twelve. Here, in the absence of doubt, resolve arises. Together with that, there are exactly thirteen, and, on account of the presence of resolve, concentration becomes stronger here. As for restlessness here, it comes down by its own name; resolve and attention occur as occasional additions. Thus should the unwholesome formations be understood.

Among the indeterminate, the resultant indeterminate is, firstly, of two kinds by way of rootless and rooted. Among these, that associated with rootless resultant consciousness is rootless. Herein, associated with wholesome- or unwholesome-resultant eye-consciousness, there are, firstly, four fixed factors coming down by name — contact, volition, life, and mental steadiness — and one occasional addition, attention, making five. The same applies to those associated with ear-, nose-, tongue-, and body-consciousness. With the mind-element performing both kinds of resultant function, these same factors, together with initial thought, sustained thought, and resolve, make eight; likewise with all three kinds of rootless mind-consciousness-element. Where this is accompanied by joy, joy should be understood as an additional factor there.

That associated with rooted resultant consciousness is rooted. Among these, that associated with the eight sense-sphere resultants is exactly like the formations associated with the eight sense-sphere wholesome minds. But the compassion and sympathetic joy found among the not-fixed factors of the wholesome do not occur among the resultants, since resultant consciousness is restricted to a strictly limited object; and not only these, but the abstinences too do not occur among the resultants. That associated with fine-material, immaterial, and supramundane resultant consciousness, however, is exactly like the formations associated with the corresponding wholesome consciousness.

The functional indeterminate, too, is of two kinds by way of rootless and rooted. That associated with rootless functional consciousness is rootless. This is similar to that associated with the wholesome-resultant mind-element and the two rootless mind-consciousness-elements — except that, among the two mind-consciousness-elements, energy is an additional factor, and, on account of the presence of energy, concentration becomes strengthened. That associated with rooted functional consciousness is rooted. Among these, that associated with the eight sense-sphere functional minds is, excluding the abstinences, exactly like the formations associated with the eight sense-sphere wholesome minds. That associated with fine-material and immaterial functional consciousness, however, is in every respect exactly like that associated with the corresponding wholesome consciousness. Thus should the indeterminate formations, too, be understood.

Reading note — Ru-Yi

The exclusion of compassion, sympathetic joy, and the abstinences from every resultant mind, restated twice for emphasis, points to a structural fact worth dwelling on: resultant consciousness is, by its very nature, the passive ripening of past kamma rather than a fresh moral choice, and both compassion/sympathetic-joy and deliberate abstention are inherently active, chosen responses — they simply have no place in a mind-moment that is not itself doing anything, only receiving the fruit of something already done.

Remorse's proximate cause, "what was done and what was left undone," captures something genuinely universal about guilt's structure: it is never only about a bad act committed, but equally about a good act neglected, and the servitude simile at its close — being enslaved to a past that cannot be changed — names exactly why remorse, unlike genuine ethical reflection, tends to trap rather than free the mind that dwells in it.

Past, Present, Future: Four Ways to Measure Time Within a Single Aggregate

Having fully catalogued each aggregate's own internal contents, the commentary turns to the canonical formula that actually defines a khandha as such — the Buddha's own words gathering "whatever form, past, future, or present, internal or external, gross or subtle, inferior or superior, far or near" into a single heap called "the aggregate of form," and likewise for the other four. Working through this formula phrase by phrase, the chapter shows that matter counts as "past," "future," or "present" according to no fewer than four distinct measures at once — by era (relative to a single lifetime's conception and death), by continuity (matched groupings of same-origin matter), by occasion (a given part of the day or night), and by moment (the three sub-instants of arising, persisting, and dissolving) — before closing with the formula's final instruction: all of this variously-designated matter, gathered together under its single shared characteristic of being deformed, is what "the aggregate of form" actually names. No form exists apart from this heaping-together; there is no separate, additional "aggregate" lurking behind the individual instances.

Reading note — Ru-Yi

The four measures of time — era, continuity, occasion, and moment — are not four competing definitions of "past" and "present" but four genuinely different scales at which the same question can be asked, ranging from an entire lifetime down to the three sub-instants within a single mind-moment's own arising and passing; the same physical matter can correctly be called "present" by the era-measure while simultaneously being "past" by the far finer moment-measure, and the commentary treats this as no contradiction at all.

The closing insistence that "there is no aggregate of form apart from form itself" is a quietly important metaphysical clarification: the word "khandha" names a mode of gathering or heaping together many individual instances under one shared characteristic, not some further, additional entity standing behind or above those instances — a warning against reifying the aggregates into something more than what they actually are.

Gross and Subtle Feeling: Four Ways to Rank Intensity Without Ever Mixing the Criteria

The classification survey now applies the same past/future/present, gross/subtle framework to feeling — with feeling's own genuinely distinctive contribution being a careful warning against a subtle logical trap. Feeling counts as gross or subtle by no fewer than four entirely independent measures: by moral kind (unwholesome feeling is gross next to wholesome, which is gross next to indeterminate), by its own intrinsic nature (painful feeling is gross next to pleasant, which is gross next to neutral), by the person experiencing it (an unconcentrated person's feeling is gross next to a concentrated person's), and by mundane-versus-supramundane status. The trap the commentary flags is real: since unwholesome feeling happens to be indeterminate in moral kind yet gross in intrinsic nature, a careless reader might wrongly conclude that indeterminate feeling is simultaneously gross and subtle — the fix is simply never to cross criteria, always asking "gross or subtle by which specific measure?" before comparing across categories. The section closes by noting that everything just established for feeling applies in exactly the same way to perception and the remaining aggregates, following the same fourfold time-scheme and gross/subtle logic without further repetition.

Why Teach Them in This Order? Why Exactly Five? Aggregates Versus Clinging-Aggregates

The chapter's closing discussion opens with a summarizing verse promising a rigorous, six-fold method for understanding the aggregates once more — by order, by distinction, by neither too few nor too many, by simile, by two ways of viewing, and by the benefit of seeing rightly. Five different possible orderings of the aggregates are surveyed and, one by one, rejected as inapplicable — order of arising (the aggregates don't emerge like an embryo's successive stages), order of abandoning (indeterminate states are never actually abandoned), order of practice (unwholesome states are never actually practiced), order of plane (feeling and the others span all four planes equally) — leaving only order of teaching as the genuine explanation for why form comes first, then feeling, then perception, then formations, then consciousness: form is taught first because it is the coarsest and most obviously graspable, feeling next because it registers form as pleasant or painful, perception next because it apprehends the very same object feeling has already registered, formations next because they are what actually organizes perception's raw material into structured response, and consciousness last as their shared support and governing master. The discussion then turns to the crucial distinction between "aggregates" and "aggregates subject to clinging" (the latter narrower, restricted to what is actually tainted and clingable), before settling the deeper question of why the canonical count stops at exactly five — neither fewer nor more — grounded in a single, comprehensive principle: these five, and no others, exhaust every possible basis on which the mistaken view "this is mine, this I am, this is my self" could ever take hold.

the utmost limit, the final extent — eta (this) + parama (highest, ultimate); describing the five aggregates as the absolute outer boundary of anything that could ever serve as a basis for self-view, with nothing beyond them left to grasp at.

desanākkama

the order of teaching — desanā (teaching, exposition) + kkama (order, sequence); the single surviving explanation, among five candidates considered, for why the aggregates are always listed form-feeling-perception-formations-consciousness.

A Foam-Lump, a Water-Bubble, a Mirage: Five Similes for Five Aggregates

The chapter's closing movement supplies its promised similes, then its two ways of seeing, then the tangible benefit each brings. By way of a hospital-ward comparison: the form-aggregate subject to clinging resembles the sickroom itself, since consciousness dwells there; feeling resembles the sickness, being what actually afflicts; perception resembles the origin of that sickness, since sensual and other kinds of perception, tangled with lust, are literally what "construct feeling for the sake of feeling"; formations resemble the pursuit of unsuitable food, since they are the very cause of feeling's own sickness; and consciousness resembles the patient himself, never free of that sickness. More briefly still, all five together are compared to an executioner with drawn sword, a burden, a devourer, and something impermanent, painful, and not-self, all at once. In greater detail, form is like a lump of foam, crumbling under the slightest pressure; feeling, like a bubble on water, delighting for a mere instant; perception, like a mirage, deceiving whoever approaches it; formations, like a banana trunk, hollow and without any real core when peeled apart; and consciousness, like a magic trick, deceiving through its own sheer cleverness. Seeing each aggregate in this way — form as foul, feeling as suffering, perception and formations as not-self, consciousness as impermanent — crosses each of the four floods in turn, breaks the corresponding body-knot, and abandons the corresponding clinging, bringing the chapter, and the treatise's entire ground-laying survey of the aggregates, to its close.

Ru-Yi Translation

"By simile": here, the form-aggregate subject to clinging is like a sickroom, since it is the dwelling-place, by way of basis, door, and object, for the consciousness-aggregate subject to clinging, which is like the sickness itself. The feeling-aggregate subject to clinging is like the sickness, on account of its being the actual affliction. The perception-aggregate subject to clinging is like the origin of the sickness, on account of the presence of feeling associated with lust and the rest by way of sensual perception and the like. The formations-aggregate subject to clinging is like the pursuit of unsuitable things, on account of being the source of feeling's own sickness — for it is said, "they form together feeling for the sake of feeling." The consciousness-aggregate subject to clinging is like the sick person himself, on account of never being free from feeling's sickness. Moreover, these may be compared to a prison, a cause for punishment, a wrongdoer subject to punishment, and to a bowl, food, a seasoning, a server, and an eater, respectively. Thus should the method of determination by simile be understood.

"By being seen in two ways": here, the method of determination by being seen in these two ways — briefly and in detail — should be understood. Briefly: the five aggregates subject to clinging should be regarded, in the manner stated in the Snake Simile, as an enemy with drawn sword; by way of the Burden Discourse, as a burden; by way of the Discourse on the Analogy of Eating, as a devourer; by way of the Pairs Discourse, as impermanent, suffering, not-self, conditioned, and a murderer. In detail: form should be regarded as like a lump of foam, on account of its inability to withstand pressure. Feeling, like a bubble on water, on account of being enjoyable for only a moment. Perception, like a mirage, on account of its deceptiveness. Formations, like a banana trunk, on account of being without core. Consciousness, like a magic trick, on account of its deceptiveness. In particular, even the most excellent internal form should be regarded as foul. Feeling should be regarded as suffering, on account of never being free from the three kinds of suffering. Perception and formations should be regarded as not-self, on account of not being subject to one's control. Consciousness should be regarded as impermanent, on account of its nature of arising and passing away.

"By the accomplishment of benefit for one who sees them thus": from this too, the method of determination should be understood — namely, the accomplishment of benefit that comes to one who sees them in these two ways, briefly and in detail. Thus: briefly, one who sees the five aggregates subject to clinging as an enemy with drawn sword and the rest is not afflicted by the aggregates. In detail, one who sees form and the rest as resembling a lump of foam and the rest does not take what is without core to have a core.

And in particular: one who sees internal form as foul fully understands physical nutriment, abandons the distorted perception of the foul as fair, crosses the flood of sensual desire, is disjoined from the yoke of sensual desire, becomes free of the taint of sensual desire, breaks the body-knot of covetousness, and does not cling to the clinging to sensual pleasure.

One who sees feeling as suffering fully understands the nutriment of contact, abandons the distorted perception of suffering as pleasure, crosses the flood of becoming, is disjoined from the yoke of becoming, becomes free of the taint of becoming, breaks the body-knot of ill will, and does not cling to the clinging to rites and rituals.

One who sees perception and formations as not-self fully understands the nutriment of mental volition, abandons the distorted perception of not-self as self, crosses the flood of views, is disjoined from the yoke of views, becomes free of the taint of views, breaks the body-knot of adherence to "this alone is truth," and does not cling to the doctrine of self.

One who sees consciousness as impermanent fully understands the nutriment of consciousness, abandons the distorted perception of the impermanent as permanent, crosses the flood of ignorance, is disjoined from the yoke of ignorance, becomes free of the taint of ignorance, breaks the body-knot of adherence to rules and observances, and does not cling to the clinging to views.

Reading note — Ru-Yi

The hospital-ward simile assigns each aggregate a role in a single unfolding medical crisis — consciousness the patient, feeling the sickness, perception the sickness's origin, formations the harmful behavior that caused it, form the room where it all takes place — and its real force is diagnostic: understanding a person as five aggregates is not an abstract philosophical exercise but is structured exactly like correctly diagnosing an illness, where naming each contributing factor precisely is the necessary first step toward any actual cure.

The five detailed similes — foam, a water-bubble, a mirage, a banana trunk, a magic trick — share a single unifying theme director's attention would reward noticing: every one of them describes something that looks solid, substantial, or real from a normal viewing distance, and reveals its actual emptiness only under closer examination or the passage of a moment's time, making the five similes not five unrelated images but five variations on exactly one insight applied to five different aggregates.

Khandhaniddesa Complete — Wisdom's Ground Begins

With the aggregate of consciousness, the four remaining mental aggregates, and their full classification by time, place, and simile now complete, the fourteenth chapter of the Visuddhimagga closes — the first chapter of the treatise's third and final division, paññā, wisdom. Opening with a precise account of what wisdom actually is and how it differs from mere perception and bare consciousness, the chapter worked through wisdom's own many classifications before laying out the architecture governing everything still to come: the six categories forming wisdom's "ground," the two purifications already achieved forming its "root," and the five purifications yet to be developed forming its "body." The bulk of the chapter then delivered an exhaustive, technical portrait of the first item of that ground — the five aggregates — cataloguing twenty-eight kinds of form, eighty-nine kinds of consciousness and their fourteen-stage process of occurrence, feeling's five kinds and fifty-two accompanying mental factors in full, and the precise sense in which each aggregate is properly called past, future, present, gross, subtle, inferior, superior, far, or near.

The fifteenth chapter, the Āyatanadhātuniddesa ("Discussion of the Sense Bases and Elements"), verified from the Visuddhimagga's own table of contents, continues wisdom's ground by turning from the five aggregates to two further, closely related analytical schemes for the very same reality — the twelve sense bases and the eighteen elements — completing the threefold classification (aggregates, bases, elements) that the earliest Buddhist analytical tradition uses to dissect the totality of experience from three complementary angles.
