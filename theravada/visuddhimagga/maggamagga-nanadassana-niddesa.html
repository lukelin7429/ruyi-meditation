Chapter 20 · Maggāmaggañāṇadassanavisuddhiniddesa · Complete

Path and Not-Path

This page carries Ru-Yi's translation of the Visuddhimagga's chapter 20, Maggāmaggañāṇadassanavisuddhiniddesa ("The Description of Purification by Knowledge and Vision of What Is Path and What Is Not Path") — the third of the five "body" purifications, and by far the most detailed treatment yet of insight meditation's actual moving parts. It works through comprehension by groups and its forty canonical aspects, traces matter's and mind's arising through exhaustive Abhidhamma detail (with the unforgettable image of a villager seeing a lamp's flame in ever-finer slices), develops full knowledge of the rise and fall of all five aggregates, and closes with the treatise's most celebrated cautionary teaching: the ten "corruptions of insight" — illumination, rapture, tranquility, and the rest — each pleasant enough to be mistaken for the path itself, illustrated by the classic story of an accomplished elder who briefly believed himself an arahant.

Purification by Knowledge and Vision of Path and Not-Path Defined; the Three Kinds of Full Understanding

The twentieth chapter opens, as the previous two did, with a one-sentence definition: knowing both path and not-path, and standing firm in that knowledge. Before showing how this knowledge is reached, Buddhaghosa first locates it within a threefold scheme of "full understanding" already introduced by the Paṭisambhidāmagga — full understanding as the known, by scrutiny, and by abandoning — mapping each onto a specific stretch of the meditator's progress, and showing that the first of the three has already been completed by the previous two chapters.

Ru-Yi Translation

The knowledge that stands, having known both path and not-path thus: "this is the path, this is not the path" — this is called "purification by knowledge and vision of what is path and what is not path."

One desiring to accomplish this must first make the effort at the methodical insight reckoned as comprehension by groups. Why? Because knowledge of path and not-path comes about for one who has embarked on insight once illumination and the rest have arisen; and for one who has embarked on insight, illumination and the rest arise once comprehension by groups — insight's own starting point — has been undertaken. This is why this topic has been set out immediately after purification by overcoming doubt. Moreover, since knowledge of path and not-path arises while full understanding by scrutiny is in progress, and full understanding by scrutiny follows immediately upon full understanding as the known, for this reason too, one desiring to accomplish purification by knowledge and vision of path and not-path must first make the effort at comprehension by groups.

Herein is the determination: there are three kinds of mundane full understanding — full understanding as the known, full understanding by scrutiny, and full understanding by abandoning. With reference to these it was said: "the wisdom of direct knowledge is knowledge in the sense of the known. The wisdom of full understanding is knowledge in the sense of scrutiny. The wisdom of abandoning is knowledge in the sense of relinquishment." Herein, the wisdom that occurs by way of discerning the individual characteristic of each and every state — thus: "matter has the characteristic of being deformed, feeling has the characteristic of being felt" — is called full understanding as the known. The wisdom of insight that has a state's characteristic as its object, occurring by imposing the general characteristic on those same states, by the method beginning "matter is impermanent, feeling is impermanent" — this is called full understanding by scrutiny. But the wisdom of insight that has a state's characteristic as its object, occurring, with respect to those same states, by way of abandoning the perception of permanence and the rest — this is called full understanding by abandoning.

Herein, the domain of full understanding as the known runs from the delimitation of formations up to the grasping of conditions; for within this stretch, the penetration of states' individual characteristics alone predominates. The domain of full understanding by scrutiny, however, runs from comprehension by groups up to the contemplation of rise and fall; for within this stretch, the penetration of the general characteristic alone predominates. The domain of full understanding by abandoning begins with the contemplation of dissolution and continues beyond it; for from that point on, contemplating as impermanent, one abandons the perception of permanence; contemplating as suffering, the perception of pleasure; contemplating as not-self, the perception of self; growing disenchanted, one abandons delight; becoming dispassionate, one abandons lust; causing cessation, one abandons origination; relinquishing, one abandons grasping — thus the seven contemplations that accomplish the abandoning of the perception of permanence and the rest predominate here. Thus, since the delimitation of formations and the grasping of conditions have already been accomplished within these three kinds of full understanding, this yogin has already attained full understanding as the known, and the remaining two are still to be attained. Hence it was said: "since knowledge of path and not-path arises while full understanding by scrutiny is in progress, and full understanding by scrutiny follows immediately upon full understanding as the known, for this reason too, one desiring to accomplish purification by knowledge and vision of path and not-path must first make the effort at comprehension by groups."

Reading note — Ru-Yi

This opening section is essentially a map of the whole remaining journey through insight, given before the journey itself begins. The three kinds of full understanding correspond to three different objects of attention: individual characteristics (matter's own hardness or feeling's own being-felt), the general characteristics shared by all conditioned things (impermanence, suffering, not-self), and finally the active abandoning of the wrong perceptions those general characteristics overturn. What makes this section reassuring rather than intimidating is its final move: the meditator arriving at this chapter has, in a sense, already done the hardest conceptual work. Chapters eighteen and nineteen's determination of mind-and-matter and its conditions already constitute the entire domain of "full understanding as the known" — what remains is to turn that same understanding, already secured, toward the general characteristics themselves.

Key vocabulary — for Pāli learners
ñātapariññā / tīraṇapariññā / pahānapariññā

full understanding as the known / by scrutiny / by abandoning — ñāta (known), tīraṇa (scrutiny, judging), and pahāna (abandoning) each + pariññā (full understanding); the Paṭisambhidāmagga's own threefold scheme for insight's progressive deepening, mapped here onto three successive stretches of the meditator's path.

paccattalakkhaṇa / sāmaññalakkhaṇa

individual characteristic / general characteristic — paccatta (particular, one's own) and sāmañña (common, general) + lakkhaṇa (characteristic); the two levels of characteristic insight discerns in sequence — first what makes each state unique (matter's hardness, feeling's being-felt), then what all conditioned states share (impermanence, suffering, not-self).

kalāpasammasana

comprehension by groups — kalāpa (a bundle, group) + sammasana (comprehending, handling); insight's own starting technique, named here as the necessary first step toward everything this chapter will go on to describe, and given its own detailed treatment in the section immediately following.

The Paṭisambhidāmagga's Method of Comprehension by Groups

The chapter now quotes, in full, the Paṭisambhidāmagga's own canonical definition of "comprehension" — determining every possible category of state, past, future, and present, as impermanent, suffering, and not-self, by four increasingly specific formulas. A long peyyāla then unpacks exactly which categories are compressed under this citation's abbreviations, cross-referenced against the Saṃyutta Nikāya's own "everything is to be directly known" teaching, before a closing instruction: the meditator should simply begin with whatever categories are already evident and come easily to hand.

Ru-Yi Translation

Herein, this is the canonical text:

"How is there knowledge of comprehension, which is the wisdom of determining, by way of summary, past, future, and present states? Whatever matter, past, future, or present, internal or … and so on … far or near, all such matter is determined as impermanent — this is one comprehension. It is determined as suffering — this is one comprehension. It is determined as not-self — this is one comprehension. Whatever feeling … and so on … whatever consciousness … and so on … is determined as not-self — this is one comprehension.

"The eye … and so on … aging-and-death, past, future, or present, is determined as impermanent — this is one comprehension. It is determined as suffering, as not-self — this is one comprehension.

"Matter, past, future, or present, is impermanent in the sense of ending, suffering in the sense of being fearsome, not-self in the sense of having no core" — this is knowledge of comprehension, the wisdom of determining by way of summary. Feeling … consciousness … the eye … and so on … aging-and-death … and so on … is knowledge of comprehension.

"Matter, past, future, or present, is impermanent, conditioned, dependently arisen, subject to ending, subject to passing away, subject to fading away, subject to cessation" — this is knowledge of comprehension, the wisdom of determining by way of summary. Feeling … consciousness … the eye … aging-and-death, past, future, or present, is impermanent, conditioned … and so on … subject to cessation" — this is knowledge of comprehension, the wisdom of determining by way of summary.

"Dependent on birth is aging-and-death; when birth is absent, there is no aging-and-death" — this is knowledge of comprehension, the wisdom of determining by way of summary. "In the past time too, in the future time too, dependent on birth is aging-and-death; when birth is absent, there is no aging-and-death" — this is knowledge of comprehension. "Dependent on being is birth … and so on … dependent on ignorance are the formations; when ignorance is absent, there are no formations" — this is knowledge of comprehension. "In the past time too, in the future time too, dependent on ignorance are the formations; when ignorance is absent, there are no formations" — this is knowledge of comprehension.

"That is knowledge in the sense of the known. It is wisdom in the sense of understanding. Hence it is called: knowledge of comprehension, the wisdom of determining, by way of summary, past, future, and present states."

Herein, by this abbreviation "the eye … and so on … aging-and-death," it should be understood that the following categories of states are compressed: the states occurring at the doors together with their doors and objects, the five aggregates, the six doors, the six objects, the six kinds of consciousness, the six contacts, the six feelings, the six perceptions, the six volitions, the six cravings, the six kinds of applied thought, the six kinds of sustained thought, the six elements, the ten kasiṇas, the thirty-two parts, the twelve bases, the eighteen elements, the twenty-two faculties, the three elements, the nine modes of being, the four jhānas, the four boundless states, the four immaterial attainments, and the twelve factors of dependent origination.

For this was said in the Description of What Is to Be Directly Known:

"Bhikkhus, everything is to be directly known. And what, bhikkhus, is everything that is to be directly known? The eye, bhikkhus, is to be directly known. Visible forms … eye-consciousness … eye-contact … and whatever feeling — pleasant, painful, or neither-painful-nor-pleasant — arises dependent on eye-contact, that too is to be directly known. The ear … and so on … and whatever feeling arises dependent on mind-contact, that too is to be directly known. Matter … and so on … consciousness … the eye … and so on … the mind … visible forms … and so on … mind-objects … eye-consciousness … and so on … mind-consciousness … eye-contact … and so on … mind-contact …. Feeling born of eye-contact … and so on … feeling born of mind-contact … perception of visible form … and so on … perception of mind-objects … volition regarding visible form … and so on … volition regarding mind-objects … craving for visible form … and so on … craving for mind-objects … applied thought about visible form … and so on … applied thought about mind-objects … sustained thought about visible form … and so on … sustained thought about mind-objects …. The earth element … and so on … the consciousness element … the earth kasiṇa … and so on … the consciousness kasiṇa … the hairs … and so on … the urine … the brain …. Since this was stated there in detail, here it is all compressed by way of abbreviation. Now, among these compressed categories, whatever supramundane states have been included should not be taken up in this context, since they are not susceptible to comprehension. And even among those that are susceptible to comprehension, one should begin one's comprehension with whichever of them are evident to oneself and come easily within one's grasp.

Reading note — Ru-Yi

The four formulas quoted here move from the most general to the most specific: first a bare determination as impermanent, suffering, not-self; then the same determination anchored in a technical reason for each term (khayaṭṭha, "in the sense of ending," for impermanence; bhayaṭṭha, "in the sense of being fearsome," for suffering; asārakaṭṭha, "in the sense of having no core," for not-self — the same three technical glosses chapter nineteen's own opening reasoning already relied on); then a fuller string of near-synonyms for impermanence alone (conditioned, dependently arisen, subject to ending, and the rest); and finally dependent origination's own conditional formula, applied specifically to birth-and-aging-death and to ignorance-and-formations as the chain's two ends. Together the four formulas give the meditator an entire toolkit of phrasings to apply to whatever category of experience is currently in view.

The closing instruction — begin with whatever is already evident and comes easily to hand — is a small but important piece of practical guidance easy to miss inside such a dense citation. Buddhaghosa is not asking a meditator to work systematically through this entire, genuinely enormous list of categories (five aggregates, six doors, thirty-two body parts, eighteen elements, and so on) before comprehension can begin; rather, the list exists so that whatever a given meditator's mind naturally settles on can be correctly located within it and confidently worked with.

Key vocabulary — for Pāli learners
khayaṭṭhena / bhayaṭṭhena / asārakaṭṭhena

"in the sense of ending" / "of being fearsome" / "of having no core" — khaya (destruction, ending), bhaya (fear), and asāraka (coreless, without essence) each + aṭṭha (sense, meaning); the three technical glosses that specify exactly why formations are impermanent, suffering, and not-self respectively, first introduced at this level of precision here.

abhiññeyya

Eleven Aspects of Impermanence, Suffering, and Not-Self, Applied to Matter

Turning from theory to actual practice, the meditator now applies the canonical formulas to matter itself (with the same method understood to extend to feeling and the remaining aggregates): eleven overlapping ways of slicing up "whatever matter" — past, future, present; internal, external; gross, subtle; inferior, superior; far, near — each one shown to end exactly where it stands, never crossing over into any other category, and each therefore impermanent in the sense of ending, suffering in the sense of being fearsome, and not-self in the sense of having no core.

To this extent, this monk, having delimited all matter indefinitely designated as "whatever matter" by eleven occasions — the threefold past-future-present together with the four pairs beginning with internal-and-external — determines all matter as impermanent; he comprehends it as "impermanent."

How? By the method stated further on. For this was said: "matter, past, future, or present, is impermanent in the sense of ending."

Therefore: whatever matter is past — since it has ended in just the past, and has not reached this present existence — is impermanent in the sense of ending.

Whatever is future will arise in the immediately following existence, and will end right there, without going on to any existence beyond that — thus it is impermanent in the sense of ending.

Whatever matter is present ends right here, and does not go on from here — thus it is impermanent in the sense of ending.

Whatever is internal ends as internal alone, and does not become external — thus it is impermanent in the sense of ending.

Whatever is external … and so on … gross … and so on … subtle … and so on … inferior … and so on … superior … and so on … far … and so on … near, ends right there too, and does not become distant — thus he comprehends it as impermanent in the sense of ending.

All of this together, by way of "impermanent in the sense of ending," is one comprehension. By way of its divisions, however, it is elevenfold.

And all of that same matter is suffering in the sense of being fearsome. "In the sense of being fearsome" means: because it carries danger within it. For whatever is impermanent brings fear, just as it did for the gods in the Lion Simile Sutta. Thus this too, by way of "suffering in the sense of being fearsome," is one comprehension. By way of its divisions, however, it is elevenfold.

And just as it is suffering, so too is all of that same matter not-self in the sense of having no core. "In the sense of having no core" means: because of the absence of the core of a self as conceived thus — "a self that dwells within, that acts, that experiences, that is self-determining." For whatever is impermanent and suffering cannot even ward off its own impermanence or the affliction of rising and falling — how then could it have the nature of an agent and the rest? Hence it was said: "for if, bhikkhus, this matter were self, this matter would not lead to affliction" and so on. Thus this too, by way of "not-self in the sense of having no core," is one comprehension. By way of its divisions, however, it is elevenfold. This same method applies to feeling and the rest.

But since whatever is impermanent is, by rule, also divided into conditioned and so on, therefore, in order to show a further method for that, or to show attention proceeding by way of various aspects, the canonical text was stated once again: "matter, past, future, or present, is impermanent, conditioned, dependently arisen, subject to ending, subject to passing away, subject to fading away, subject to cessation." This same method applies to feeling and the rest.

Reading note — Ru-Yi

The eleven occasions work by systematically closing off every direction a piece of matter might seem to "travel" in — through time (past into present, present into future), through space (internal into external, near into far), or through quality (gross into subtle, inferior into superior) — and showing, in each case, that no such travel actually occurs: whatever exists in one of these categories simply ends there, without transition. This is the same insight chapter eighteen's chariot and house similes established for "a being," now generalized into an exhaustive procedural sweep across every possible way of categorizing matter.

The definition of "not-self in the sense of having no core" is worth pausing on for its precision: a "self" is defined here not vaguely but by four specific imagined powers — dwelling (persisting through change), acting (initiating without being caused), experiencing (receiving results as a subject), and self-determination (being immune to conditions). The argument then simply observes that matter cannot even protect itself from its own impermanence, let alone exercise any of these four powers — a tightly logical route to not-self that requires no appeal to authority, only to what has already been established about matter's own instability.

Reading note — Ru-Yi

The forty terms are not forty independent ideas but forty different angles of approach onto exactly three underlying facts, as the closing tally makes explicit: ten of them (impermanent, disintegrating, fickle, breaking up, unstable, subject to change, coreless, subject to annihilation, conditioned, subject to death) are all restatements of impermanence; five (alien, empty, vain, void, not-self) are restatements of not-self; and the remaining twenty-five are all restatements of suffering. The apparent proliferation into two hundred contemplations (forty terms times five aggregates) is therefore not two hundred separate insights but the same three insights, deliberately multiplied across enough different images and emphases that whichever framing best matches a given meditator's own temperament and habitual way of thinking will be available to them.

Several of the images repay a second look for their sheer vividness within an otherwise highly technical list: "a boil" swollen, ripened by aging, and finally bursting; "a murderer" who strikes precisely by wearing a trusted friend's face, capturing how ordinary attachment to the aggregates disguises the harm they eventually inflict; and the etymological aside on ādīnava (wretchedness, but literally "what one goes toward"), likened to a destitute person, which briefly turns a purely doctrinal term into a small character sketch.

Seeing Matter's Arising: Kamma-Born and Mind-Born Matter

The fourth subhead turns from comprehending matter's characteristics to actually seeing how matter arises in the first place, tracked through its four origins. Kamma-born matter comes first: the precise thirty material phenomena that spring into being at the very instant of rebirth-linking, the striking contrast in durability between matter (slow to cease) and mind (so quick to change the Buddha found nothing else comparable to it), and the exact technical division of kamma's own fourfold conditioning role. Mind-born matter follows, with a verse tallying exactly which of the eighty-nine states of consciousness can generate matter, posture, or bodily intimation, and which cannot.

Reading note — Ru-Yi

The relative durability of matter and mind, carefully quantified here, is one of the Abhidhamma's most consequential pieces of timekeeping: a single instance of matter lasts through the arising, persisting, and dissolving of a full sixteen mind-moments, meaning consciousness genuinely does flicker in and out of existence roughly sixteen times faster than the physical basis it depends on. Buddhaghosa grounds this arithmetic directly in a canonical quotation praising mind's changeability above every other single thing — turning what might read as dry technical bookkeeping into a restatement of one of the Buddha's own most-quoted observations about the mind's sheer instability.

The mind-born matter verse's fourfold tally (thirty-two / twenty-six / nineteen / sixteen states of consciousness) rewards close attention: it establishes that not every state of consciousness can move the body or produce speech — the sixteen "weakest" states (the five-fold sense-consciousnesses, rebirth-linking, an arahant's death-consciousness, and the four immaterial resultants) are purely receptive or purely transitional, incapable of generating matter at all, while only the thirty-two most kammically active states can produce the bodily and verbal intimation that make voluntary movement and speech possible in the first place.

Reading note — Ru-Yi

The striking disparity between an ordinary meal's seven-day sustaining power and divine nutritive essence's month-or-two endurance is one of several small details throughout the Visuddhimagga where cosmology and physiology intersect — the same nutritive-essence-octad mechanism operates identically across all realms of existence, differing only in potency according to the refinement of the nutriment involved. The detail about a nursing infant receiving nutriment indirectly through its mother's own digested food is a small but genuinely tender observation embedded within an otherwise highly technical passage.

Temperature's capacity to keep generating matter "even standing on the not-clung-to side" is a precise technical point: unlike kamma-born matter, which requires a living being's own kammic continuity to sustain it, temperature-originated matter continues operating in purely inanimate matter as well — which is, in fact, the entire physical basis for why external objects (wood, stone, metal) persist and change over time according to ordinary physical laws, without any kamma of their own being involved at all.

Seeing the Immaterial's Arising at the Six Doors

The fifth subhead turns to the immaterial's own arising, traced first at rebirth-linking (where nineteen kinds of resultant consciousness spring up by way of past kamma, exactly as explained in the Description of Dependent Origination), then through an entire ordinary cognitive process at the eye-door — worked through moment by moment, from the initial physical impact through adverting, seeing, receiving, investigating, determining, impulsion, and registration — establishing the complete mechanics that make this chapter's later, more advanced knowledge of rise and fall possible.

Ru-Yi Translation

And just as, for one comprehending matter, matter's arising should be seen, so too, for one comprehending the immaterial, the immaterial's arising should be seen. And this, indeed, only by way of the arising of the eighty-one mundane states of consciousness.

That is: this immaterial, at rebirth-linking, first arises with a division into nineteen kinds of consciousness, by way of the kamma accumulated in a previous existence. The manner of its arising should be understood exactly by the method already stated in the Description of Dependent Origination. That same nineteen-fold consciousness, from the moment immediately following the rebirth-linking consciousness, occurs by way of the life-continuum, and, at the end of the lifespan, by way of the death-consciousness. Whatever among these is sense-sphere consciousness occurs, at the six doors, by way of the registration consciousness, when the object is powerful.

But in the course of life, since the eye is not yet engaged and visible forms have not yet entered its range, eye-consciousness arises, dependent on light, with attention as its cause, together with its associated states. For, when the eye-sensitivity is at its standing moment, matter that has itself reached its standing phase strikes against the eye. When that impact occurs, the life-continuum arises and ceases twice. Then, taking that very object, the functional mind-element arises, accomplishing the function of adverting. Immediately after that, eye-consciousness — either wholesome-resultant or unwholesome-resultant — arises, seeing that same visible form. Then the resultant mind-element, receiving that same visible form. Then the rootless resultant mind-consciousness-element, investigating that same visible form. Then the rootless functional mind-consciousness-element, accompanied by equanimity, determining that same visible form. After that, among the sense-sphere wholesome, unwholesome, and functional states of consciousness, either one rootless state of consciousness accompanied by equanimity, or five or seven impulsions occur. Then, among the eleven registration-consciousnesses possible for sense-sphere beings, whatever registration consciousness is appropriate to the impulsions occurs. This same method applies at the remaining doors as well. At the mind-door, however, even exalted states of consciousness can arise. Thus, at the six doors, the arising of the immaterial should be seen. For one who thus sees the arising of the immaterial is said to comprehend the immaterial "at another time."

Thus, having comprehended matter at one time and the immaterial at another, and having applied the three characteristics, one such person, proceeding step by step, accomplishes the development of wisdom.

Reading note — Ru-Yi

This full walk-through of a single eye-door cognitive process — impact, two moments of life-continuum vibration, adverting, seeing, receiving, investigating, determining, up to five or seven impulsions, and finally registration — is one of the Abhidhamma's foundational pieces of psychological modeling, and its appearance here is deliberate: rather than treating "seeing a visible object" as one undifferentiated mental event, the meditator is being trained to notice that it is actually a rapid sequence of at least seven or eight functionally distinct mind-moments, each with its own specific job, arising and vanishing in immediate succession. Seeing this fine-grained structure directly, rather than merely accepting it as doctrine, is what genuine comprehension of "the immaterial's arising" requires.

The closing sentence's alternation — comprehending matter "at one time," the immaterial "at another" — is a practical instruction easy to pass over but important for anyone attempting to follow this method themselves: rather than trying to hold both matter's arising and mind's arising in view simultaneously, which the chapter's own account of their radically different lifespans (a single unit of matter outlasting sixteen mind-moments) would make extremely difficult, the meditator is advised to work each one thoroughly in its own turn.

The Sevenfold Comprehension of Matter Begins: Taking Up and Laying Down

The sixth subhead opens a new, celebrated approach to comprehension: seven successively finer-grained ways of viewing matter, beginning with the broadest possible span — an entire hundred-year lifetime, delimited only by its two endpoints, "taking up" (rebirth-linking) and "laying down" (death) — to which the three characteristics are applied in their most general form.

Herein, "taking up" is rebirth-linking; "laying down" is death. Thus the meditator, having delimited a single hundred-year span by these two, "taking up" and "laying down," applies the three characteristics to the formations. How? Within this span, all formations are impermanent. Why? Because they occur through arising and passing away, because they are subject to alteration, because they are temporary, and because they contradict permanence. But since formations, once arisen, reach the persisting stage; wear down through aging in the persisting stage; and, having reached aging, inevitably break up — they are therefore suffering, because they are continually being afflicted, because they are a basis for suffering, and because they contradict pleasure. And since, in these three respects — "let arisen formations not reach the persisting stage, let those that have reached standing not age, let those that have reached aging not break up" — no one has any power to have their way, they are empty of any such power; therefore they are not-self, because they are empty, because they have no owner, because they do not submit to one's will, and because they contradict a self.

Reading note — Ru-Yi

The seven approaches named in the opening verse form a deliberate progression from the coarsest to the subtlest way of viewing matter — a progression this whole subhead and the one following it will trace out in increasingly fine-grained detail, before the final three approaches (nutriment-made, temperature-made, kamma-born and mind-originated, and nature-matter) return to broader categorical divisions already familiar from earlier in the chapter. "Taking up and laying down," the coarsest of the seven, simply brackets an entire life between its two boundary events, applying the three characteristics at the largest possible scale before the meditator begins subdividing that same span ever more minutely.

The three-part reasoning behind each characteristic — impermanent because of arising-and-passing-away, alteration, temporariness, and opposition to permanence; suffering because of continual affliction, being a basis for suffering, and opposition to pleasure; not-self because of emptiness, having no owner, non-submission to will, and opposition to self — gives a compact template that will recur, essentially unchanged, at every successively finer level of division through the rest of this subhead.

Life's Three Stages and Ten Decades

The hundred-year span is now divided, first into three roughly equal life-stages (first, middle, and last, each about thirty-three years), then into ten decades bearing their own descriptive names — the decade of infancy, of sport, of beauty, of strength, of wisdom, of decline, of stooping, of bending, of dotage, and of lying down — each one vividly characterized before the same threefold reasoning is applied across every boundary between them.

Reading note — Ru-Yi

The ten named decades read almost as a small, self-contained essay on the human lifespan, and their vivid, unsparing physical detail (a frame "as bent as a plough's tip," dotage marked by forgetting what one has just done) belongs to the same unflinching descriptive tradition as chapter eight's treatment of aging within the recollection of death, now redeployed for an analytical rather than a purely contemplative purpose. What makes this list distinctive within the chapter's broader progression is its psychological realism: rather than treating "old age" as a single undifferentiated category, it names ten genuinely distinct phases, each with its own characteristic capacities and losses, sharpening the meditator's eye for exactly where and how impermanence actually operates across an ordinary life.

Evaṃ āropetvā puna tasmiṃyeva rūpe abhikkamapaṭikkamaālokanavilokanasamiñjanapasāraṇavasena – "abhikkame pavattarūpaṃ paṭikkamaṃ appatvā tattheva nirujjhati. Paṭikkame pavattarūpaṃ ālokanaṃ. Ālokane pavattarūpaṃ vilokanaṃ. Vilokane pavattarūpaṃ samiñjanaṃ. Samiñjane pavattarūpaṃ pasāraṇaṃ appatvā tattheva nirujjhati. Tasmā aniccaṃ dukkhamanattā"ti tilakkhaṇaṃ āropeti.

Tato ekapadavāraṃ uddharaṇa atiharaṇavītiharaṇavossajjanasannikkhepanasannirumbhanavasena cha koṭṭhāse karoti.

Tattha uddharaṇaṃ nāma pādassa bhūmito ukkhipanaṃ. Atiharaṇaṃ nāma purato haraṇaṃ. Vītiharaṇaṃ nāma khāṇukaṇṭakadīghajātiādīsu kiñcideva disvā ito cito ca pādasañcāraṇaṃ. Vossajjanaṃ nāma pādassa heṭṭhā oropanaṃ. Sannikkhepanaṃ nāma pathavītale ṭhapanaṃ. Sannirumbhanaṃ nāma puna pāduddharaṇakāle pādassa pathaviyā saddhiṃ abhinippīḷanaṃ. Tattha uddharaṇe pathavīdhātu āpodhātūti dve dhātuyo omattā honti mandā, itarā dve adhimattā honti balavatiyo. Tathā atiharaṇavītiharaṇesu. Vossajjane tejodhātu vāyodhātūti dve dhātuyo omattā honti mandā, itarā dve adhimattā honti balavatiyo. Tathā sannikkhepanasannirumbhanesu. Evaṃ cha koṭṭhāse katvā tesaṃ vasena tasmiṃ vayovuḍḍhatthaṅgamarūpe tilakkhaṇaṃ āropeti.

Kathaṃ? So iti paṭisañcikkhati – "yā uddharaṇe pavattā dhātuyo, yāni ca tadupādāyarūpāni, sabbe te dhammā atiharaṇaṃ appatvā ettheva nirujjhanti, tasmā aniccā dukkhā anattā. Tathā atiharaṇe pavattā vītiharaṇaṃ. Vītiharaṇe pavattā vossajjanaṃ. Vossajjane pavattā sannikkhepanaṃ. Sannikkhepane pavattā sannirumbhanaṃ appatvā ettheva nirujjhanti. Iti tattha tattha uppannā itaraṃ itaraṃ koṭṭhāsaṃ appatvā tattha tattheva pabbaṃ pabbaṃ sandhi sandhi odhi odhi hutvā tattakapāle pakkhittatilā viya taṭataṭāyantā saṅkhārā bhijjanti. Tasmā aniccā dukkhā anattā"ti. Tassevaṃ pabbapabbagate saṅkhāre vipassato rūpasammasanaṃ sukhumaṃ hoti.

Ru-Yi Translation

Having thus applied the three characteristics by way of the ending of growth and aging through the decades, he next divides that same hundred years into twenty portions of five years each, and applies the three characteristics by way of the ending of growth and aging through these. How? He reflects thus: "matter occurring in the first five-year period, without reaching the second five-year period, ceases right there; therefore it is impermanent, suffering, not-self. Matter occurring in the second five-year period … and so on … in the nineteenth five-year period, without reaching the twentieth, ceases right there. Matter occurring in the twentieth five-year period has no capacity whatsoever to go on beyond death; therefore it too is impermanent, suffering, not-self."

Having thus applied the three characteristics by way of the ending of growth and aging through twenty portions, he next applies it by making twenty-five portions of four years each; then thirty-three portions of three years each; fifty portions of two years each; and a hundred portions of one year each. Then, dividing a single year into three portions by way of the three seasons — the rains, the cool season, and the hot season — he applies the three characteristics to that matter subject to the ending of growth and aging by way of each single season.

How? "Matter occurring during the four months of the rains, without reaching the cool season, ceases right there. Matter occurring in the cool season, without reaching the hot season, ceases right there. Matter occurring in the hot season, without reaching the rains once more, ceases right there; therefore it is impermanent, suffering, not-self." Having thus applied it, he further divides a single year into six portions: "matter occurring during the two months of the early rains, without reaching autumn, ceases right there. Matter occurring in autumn, without reaching the cool season. Matter occurring in the cool season, without reaching the frosty season. Matter occurring in the frosty season, without reaching spring. Matter occurring in spring, without reaching the hot season. Matter occurring in the hot season, without reaching the rains once more, ceases right there; therefore it is impermanent, suffering, not-self." Thus he applies the three characteristics to that matter subject to the ending of growth and aging.

Having applied it thus, he then applies the three characteristics by way of the dark and bright fortnights: "matter occurring in the dark fortnight, without reaching the bright fortnight, ceases there. Matter occurring in the bright fortnight, without reaching the dark fortnight, ceases right there; therefore it is impermanent, suffering, not-self." Then, by way of night and day: "matter occurring at night, without reaching the day, ceases right there. Matter occurring during the day, too, without reaching the night, ceases right there; therefore it is impermanent, suffering, not-self." Then, dividing that same day-and-night into six portions by way of morning and the rest: "matter occurring in the morning, without reaching midday, ceases there. Matter occurring at midday, without reaching the evening. Matter occurring in the evening, without reaching the first watch. Matter occurring in the first watch, without reaching the middle watch. Matter occurring in the middle watch, without reaching the last watch, ceases right there. Matter occurring in the last watch, without reaching the following morning, ceases right there; therefore it is impermanent, suffering, not-self." Thus he applies the three characteristics.

Having applied it thus, he next applies the three characteristics to that same matter by way of going forward, going back, looking ahead, looking aside, bending, and stretching: "matter occurring in going forward, without reaching going back, ceases right there. Matter occurring in going back, without reaching looking ahead. Matter occurring in looking ahead, without reaching looking aside. Matter occurring in looking aside, without reaching bending. Matter occurring in bending, without reaching stretching, ceases right there. Therefore it is impermanent, suffering, not-self."

Then he makes a single footstep into six portions: lifting, carrying forward, shifting sideways, lowering, placing down, and pressing down.

Herein, "lifting" is raising the foot from the ground. "Carrying forward" is moving it forward. "Shifting sideways" is moving the foot this way and that upon seeing some obstacle such as a stump, thorn, or long creature. "Lowering" is bringing the foot back down. "Placing down" is setting it on the ground's surface. "Pressing down" is pressing the foot firmly against the ground at the time of next lifting it. Herein, in lifting, the earth-element and the water-element are the lesser, weaker two elements, while the other two are the greater, stronger two; likewise in carrying forward and shifting sideways. In lowering, the fire-element and the wind-element are the lesser, weaker two, while the other two are the greater, stronger two; likewise in placing down and pressing down. Having made these six portions, he applies the three characteristics to that matter subject to the ending of growth and aging by way of them.

How? He reflects thus: "whatever elements occurred in lifting, and whatever derived matter depended on them — all those states, without reaching carrying forward, cease right here; therefore they are impermanent, suffering, not-self. Likewise, those occurring in carrying forward cease without reaching shifting sideways. Those occurring in shifting sideways cease without reaching lowering. Those occurring in lowering cease without reaching placing down. Those occurring in placing down, without reaching pressing down, cease right here. Thus, arising here and there, without reaching this or that further portion, becoming, in each and every place, joint by joint, link by link, boundary by boundary, the formations break up, crackling like sesame seeds thrown onto a hot pan; therefore they are impermanent, suffering, not-self." For one who thus contemplates the formations, gone joint by joint in this way, the comprehension of matter becomes subtle.

Reading note — Ru-Yi

The sheer relentlessness of this passage is itself the teaching method: by walking the same threefold reasoning through year, season, fortnight, day, watch, posture, and finally the six components of a single footstep, Buddhaghosa is training the meditator's attention to keep dividing without ever finding a floor — no matter how small the interval becomes, matter is shown to end exactly there and nowhere else, never actually "traveling" across the boundary into the next interval. The image of formations "crackling like sesame seeds thrown onto a hot pan" as they break apart joint by joint is one of the most viscerally memorable in the entire treatise, converting an abstract claim about momentary arising-and-ceasing into something almost audible.

The technical detail about which pair of elements predominates in each of the six micro-movements of a footstep (earth-and-water in lifting, carrying, and shifting; fire-and-wind in lowering, placing, and pressing) is a small but genuine piece of physical observation dressed in Abhidhamma terms: lifting and moving a limb does feel governed by solidity and cohesion overcoming inertia, while setting it down and pressing firmly does feel governed by heat and motion — Buddhaghosa is asking the meditator to notice this distinction directly in their own walking, not merely accept it as doctrine.

The Lamp-Flame Simile

A single extended simile now illuminates the entire progression just completed: a villager from the borderlands, seeing a city lamp for the first time, is told by successive bystanders that its flame can be understood more and more finely — first as the whole burning of the lamp from full oil to empty, then section by section along the wick, then finger-space by finger-space, then strand by individual strand — each level of refinement mapped directly onto one of the meditator's own successively finer divisions of a human lifetime.

Ru-Yi Translation

And here is a simile for the subtlety of this comprehension. There was, it is said, a man from the borderlands, accustomed to torches of wood and grass and the like, who had never before seen a lamp. Coming into a city, and seeing a lamp burning in the marketplace, he asked a certain man: "Sir, what is this thing, so pleasing?" That man said to him: "What is pleasing about it? This thing called a lamp — with the ending of its oil and the ending of its wick, its very path of burning will no longer be perceptible." Another man said to him: "This is still speaking too coarsely; for as this wick burns, section by section, the flame ceases, without reaching the next section, right where it is." Yet another said to him: "This too is too coarse; for, finger-space by finger-space, half-finger-space by half-finger-space, thread by thread, strand by strand, the flame ceases without ever reaching the next strand. And apart from a single strand, no flame at all could even be pointed to."

Herein: the first man's knowledge, that "with the ending of oil and the ending of wick, the very path of the lamp's burning will no longer be perceptible," is like the meditator's application of the three characteristics to matter delimited by a hundred years, by way of taking-up-and-laying-down. The second man's knowledge, that "as the wick burns, section by section, the flame ceases without reaching the next section," is like the meditator's application of the three characteristics to matter subject to the ending of growth and aging, delimited by a third portion of the hundred years. The third man's knowledge, that "finger-space by finger-space, the flame ceases without reaching the next," is like the meditator's application of the three characteristics to matter delimited by ten years, five years, four years, three years, two years, or one year. The knowledge that "half-finger-space by half-finger-space, the flame ceases without reaching the next," is like the meditator's application of the three characteristics to matter delimited by four months or two months, by way of dividing a single year into three or six parts by season. The knowledge that "thread by thread, the flame ceases without reaching the next," is like the meditator's application of the three characteristics to matter delimited by way of the dark and bright fortnights, by way of night and day, and by way of dividing a single day-and-night into six portions of morning and the rest. And the knowledge that "strand by strand, the flame ceases without reaching the next," is like the meditator's application of the three characteristics to matter delimited by way of going forward and the rest, and by way of each single portion among lifting and the rest.

Reading note — Ru-Yi

This is one of the Visuddhimagga's most quietly perfect similes, and its structure rewards close attention: the borderland villager is not wrong at any stage of the conversation — each successive bystander does not correct the previous one's answer but simply refines it, showing a level of detail the earlier answer had not yet reached. This mirrors exactly the meditator's own progression through this subhead: the hundred-year, decade, year, season, day, and posture-level analyses are none of them false, each is simply coarser than the one that follows, and the whole point of walking through all of them in sequence is to train the eye to keep finding a finer level still available, all the way down to a single footstep's six components.

The final line — "apart from a single strand, no flame at all could even be pointed to" — is the simile's real payoff, and it applies with full force to the formations themselves: just as "the flame" turns out, on sufficiently close inspection, to have no existence apart from countless individual burning strands each lasting only an instant, so too "a person," "a life," "a body" turns out to have no existence apart from the individual momentary formations that constitute it, none of which persists long enough to be pointed to as a stable, continuous thing.

aṃsu

a strand, a fiber — the finest unit the simile names within a lamp's wick, mapped directly onto the finest unit of matter's own ending-of-growth-and-aging this subhead reaches: the six micro-phases of a single footstep.

Comprehension by Nutriment, Temperature, Kamma, and Mind

The remaining four of the seven approaches move from time-based division to condition-based contrast: matter is seen through the visible difference between hunger and satiety, between hot and cold, across the six sense-doors from eye to mind, and between joy and sorrow — this last contrast leading into one of the treatise's most quoted verse sequences, on the sheer swiftness of a single moment of consciousness, so brief that not even a god who lives for eighty-four thousand aeons could remain joined to two such moments at once.

Pali Source

So evaṃ nānākārehi vayovuḍḍhatthaṅgamarūpe tilakkhaṇaṃ āropetvā puna tadeva rūpaṃ visaṅkharitvā āhāramayādivasena cattāro koṭṭhāse katvā ekekakoṭṭhāse tilakkhaṇaṃ āropeti. Ru-Yi Translation

Having thus applied the three characteristics to matter subject to the ending of growth and aging by way of its various aspects, he next dissolves that same matter into four portions, beginning with the nutriment-made, and applies the three characteristics to each single portion. Herein, nutriment-made matter becomes evident to him by way of hunger and satiety. For matter arisen at a time of hunger is withered, wearied, like a burnt stump, like a crow huddled by a charcoal pit — discolored, misshapen. Matter arisen at a time of satiety is well-nourished, plump, soft, smooth, and full of texture. Having grasped this, he applies the three characteristics thus: "matter occurring at a time of hunger, without reaching a time of satiety, ceases right here. That arisen at a time of satiety, too, without reaching a time of hunger, ceases right here; therefore it is impermanent, suffering, not-self."

Temperature-made matter becomes evident by way of cold and heat. For matter arisen at a hot time is withered, wearied, discolored. Matter arisen from cold temperature is well-nourished, smooth. Having grasped this, he applies the three characteristics thus: "matter occurring at a hot time, without reaching a cold time, ceases right here. That occurring at a cold time, without reaching a hot time, ceases right here; therefore it is impermanent, suffering, not-self."

Kamma-born matter becomes evident by way of the bases and doors. For at the eye-door, there are thirty kamma-born material phenomena by way of the eye-decad, the body-decad, and the sex-decad; and their supporting temperature-, mind-, and nutriment-originated phenomena number twenty-four, making fifty-four in all. Likewise at the ear-, nose-, and tongue-doors. At the body-door, by way of the body-decad and the sex-decad, and by way of the temperature-originated and the rest, there are forty-four. At the mind-door, by way of the heart-basis-decad, the body-decad, and the sex-decad, and by way of the temperature-originated and the rest, there are just fifty-four.

Having grasped all of this matter, he applies the three characteristics thus: "matter occurring at the eye-door, without reaching the ear-door, ceases right here. That occurring at the ear-door, without reaching the nose-door. That at the nose-door, without reaching the tongue-door. That at the tongue-door, without reaching the body-door. That occurring at the body-door, without reaching the mind-door, ceases right here; therefore it is impermanent, suffering, not-self."

Mind-originated matter becomes evident by way of joyful and sorrowful states. For matter arisen at a time of joy is smooth, soft, well-nourished, full of texture. That arisen at a time of sorrow is withered, wearied, discolored. Having grasped this, he applies the three characteristics thus: "matter occurring at a time of joy, without reaching a time of sorrow, ceases right here. That occurring at a time of sorrow, without reaching a time of joy, ceases right here; therefore it is impermanent, suffering, not-self."

For one who has thus grasped mind-originated matter, and applied the three characteristics to it, this meaning becomes evident —

"Life, personal existence, and all pleasure and pain together are bound up with a single moment of consciousness; swiftly does that moment proceed.

"Those gods who abide for an aeon of eighty-four thousand years, not even they abide joined with two moments of consciousness.

"Whether for one who is dying or one who remains standing here, the aggregates that have ceased are all alike — gone, without any linking-together.

"Those broken up in the immediately preceding moment, and those to be broken up in the future — between them, among those that have ceased, there is no difference in characteristic.

"Without what has not yet arisen, one is not born; by what is present, one lives; with the breaking-up of consciousness, the world dies — this is a designation in the ultimate sense.

"What has broken up has not gone into storage; there is no heap of what is yet to come; and even what has arisen and stands does so like a mustard seed balanced on the point of an awl.

"And for states that have arisen, their own breaking-up stands right before them; subject to disintegration, they stand unmixed with what came before.

"From invisibility they come; broken up, they go again to invisibility; just as a flash of lightning in the sky, they arise and they pass away."

Reading note — Ru-Yi

These eight verses (drawn from the Mahāniddesa, and among the most frequently cited passages in later Theravāda literature on momentariness) make explicit a claim the whole chapter has been building toward through ever-finer subdivision: a "moment of consciousness" is not merely brief in some ordinary sense but incomparably, almost unimaginably brief — briefer, the verses insist, than anything a listener could otherwise use as a yardstick, since even beings whose single lifespan spans eighty-four thousand aeons never remain joined to two such moments simultaneously. The mustard-seed-on-an-awl-point image captures the same claim spatially: whatever "stands" at any given instant does so with the barest, most precarious kind of balance imaginable, a hair's-breadth from toppling into cessation.

The verses' philosophical payoff arrives in the fifth stanza: "with the breaking-up of consciousness, the world dies — this is a designation in the ultimate sense." This is a genuinely radical claim, restating "death" not as a rare, dramatic, once-per-lifetime event but as something occurring at the end of literally every single mind-moment, with ordinary biological death simply being the one instance of this universal, constant process that happens to coincide with the end of a particular continuity of aggregates. Understood this way, the entire hundred-year lifespan this subhead began with dissolves into nothing but an unbroken sequence of these momentary deaths and rebirths, each one exactly as real, and exactly as final, as the last.

Comprehension of Nature-Matter: The Aśoka Shoot

The seventh and final approach within the sevenfold matter scheme turns outward, to matter entirely external to any living being — metals, gems, earth, stone, and plant life, present in the world since the very unfolding of the aeon began. A young aśoka shoot's slow, visible progression through a whole sequence of colors, from pale red through deepening shades of red and green to a final yellowed leaf that breaks from its stalk and falls, gives the meditator a directly observable model for applying the same three characteristics to the external world.

Ru-Yi Translation

Having thus applied the three characteristics to the nutriment-made and the rest, he next applies the three characteristics to nature-matter. Nature-matter is external matter, not bound up with the physical faculties, divided into iron, bronze, tin, lead, gold, silver, pearl, gem, beryl, conch-shell, stone, coral, ruby, cat's-eye, and the matter arising, from the very beginning of the aeon's unfolding, as earth, rock, mountain, grass, tree, creeper, and the rest. This becomes evident to him by way of an aśoka shoot and the like.

For an aśoka shoot is, at first, a pale red; then, after two or three days, a deep red; then, after another two or three days, a faded red; then the color of a young shoot-leaf; then the color of a matured shoot-leaf; then the color of a green leaf; then the color of a dark-green leaf. From the time of its dark-green-leaf color, continuing to produce a corresponding succession of matter, it becomes, within about a year, a yellowed leaf, and, breaking from its stalk, falls.

Having grasped this, he applies the three characteristics thus: "matter occurring at the time of pale red, without reaching the time of deep red, ceases. That occurring at the time of deep red, without reaching the time of faded red. That at the time of faded red, without reaching the time of a young shoot-leaf's color. That occurring at the time of a young shoot-leaf's color, without reaching the time of a matured shoot-leaf's color. That occurring at the time of a matured shoot-leaf's color, without reaching the time of a green leaf's color. That occurring at the time of a green leaf's color, without reaching the time of a dark-green leaf's color. That occurring at the time of a dark-green leaf's color, without reaching the time of a yellowed leaf. That occurring at the time of a yellowed leaf, without reaching the time of breaking from its stalk and falling, ceases right there; therefore it is impermanent, suffering, not-self." Having thus applied the three characteristics there, he comprehends all nature-matter by this same method.

This, then, is how one comprehends the formations, having applied the three characteristics by way of the sevenfold matter.

Reading note — Ru-Yi

Nature-matter (dhammatārūpa) is a deliberately expansive category, gathering together the most valuable substances human beings prize — gold, gems, pearls — with ordinary earth and stone and the whole of plant life, all traced back to a single common origin at "the very beginning of the aeon's unfolding," the same cosmological starting point chapter thirteen's account of world-cycles used. The effect is to place society's most treasured objects on exactly the same impermanent footing as a garden leaf, closing off any residual exemption a meditator's mind might otherwise grant to precious things.

The choice of a growing leaf rather than a decaying one is notable: earlier similes in this same subhead (the withered, discolored matter of hunger or cold) emphasized decline, but the aśoka shoot's color sequence runs through what looks, at every early stage, like vigorous growth and increasing vitality — pale red deepening, shoot-leaf maturing, green darkening — before the abrupt final turn to yellow and falling. This is a quietly important variation: impermanence is not only visible in decay but equally present, and equally real, throughout what appears to be flourishing, since every one of the leaf's earlier colors has already, by the time the next color appears, entirely ceased.

Comprehension by Groups and by Pairs

The seventh subhead turns from matter to a parallel sevenfold scheme for the immaterial — insight applied not just to what is seen but to the seeing itself. The first two of the seven methods introduce this reflexive turn: comprehension "by groups," directed at the whole cluster of mental factors arising together with each act of comprehension, and comprehension "by pairs," in which each act of insight is itself, in turn, comprehended by a further, following act of insight.

Pali Source

Ru-Yi Translation

Now, as to what was said — "by way of the sevenfold immaterial" — herein is the summary list: by groups, by pairs, by moments, by sequence, by the uprooting of view, by the uprooting of conceit, and by the exhausting of attachment.

Herein, "by groups" refers to the states headed by contact. How does one comprehend by groups? Here, a monk reflects thus: "these states headed by contact that arose in the comprehension 'hair is impermanent, suffering, not-self,' and these states headed by contact that arose in the comprehension 'body-hair … and so on … brain is impermanent, suffering, not-self' — all of these, without reaching one another, becoming, joint by joint and boundary by boundary, extinguished, crackling like sesame seeds thrown onto a hot pan; therefore they are impermanent, suffering, not-self." This, for now, is the method as given in the Discussion of Purification.

But in the Discussion of the Noble Lineages it was said: "comprehending, by a further state of consciousness, as 'impermanent, suffering, not-self,' the state of consciousness that occurred, in the seven contexts of the sevenfold matter above, as 'matter is impermanent, suffering, not-self' — this is called 'comprehending by groups.'" That is the more fitting explanation. We shall therefore analyze the remaining six by that same method.

By pairs: here, a monk, having comprehended matter delimited by taking-up-and-laying-down as "impermanent, suffering, not-self," comprehends that very state of consciousness too, by a further state of consciousness, as "impermanent, suffering, not-self." Having comprehended matter subject to the ending of growth and aging, the nutriment-made, the temperature-made, the kamma-born, the mind-originated, and nature-matter as "impermanent, suffering, not-self," he comprehends that very state of consciousness too, by a further state of consciousness, as "impermanent, suffering, not-self." This is called comprehending by pairs.

Reading note — Ru-Yi

Buddhaghosa's choice to cite two competing explanations of "comprehension by groups" — one from an otherwise-unnamed "Discussion of Purification," another from a "Discussion of the Noble Lineages" — and then explicitly to prefer the second as "more fitting" is a small but telling glimpse of the commentarial method at work: rather than presenting a single flattened account, the text preserves a genuine scholarly choice between two transmitted explanations, with reasons implied for the preference (the second interpretation keeps "comprehension by groups" cleanly parallel to the other six methods, all of which involve one act of consciousness taking a prior act of consciousness as its own object, rather than describing a fundamentally different operation).

Comprehension "by pairs" marks the chapter's decisive turn toward reflexivity: everything up to this point has been insight directed outward, at matter and its various divisions; from here on, insight increasingly takes itself as its own object, watching its own arising and ceasing exactly as it earlier watched a lamp's flame or an aśoka leaf's color. This reflexive move is what makes the following two methods — by moments, and by sequence — possible at all.

Comprehension Moment by Moment and in Sequence

The reflexive turn deepens further: comprehension "by moments" chains four successive acts of consciousness, each comprehending the one before it as impermanent, suffering, not-self; comprehension "by sequence" extends this chain up to an eleventh act of consciousness — with a practical caveat that only the first ten should actually be practiced, since by the tenth both the matter-based and immaterial-based meditation subjects have already become fully mastered.

One who thus comprehends, starting from the state of consciousness that grasps matter, four states of consciousness at a time, is said to comprehend by moments.

By sequence: having comprehended matter delimited by taking-up-and-laying-down as "impermanent, suffering, not-self," one comprehends that first state of consciousness by a second, the second by a third, the third by a fourth … and so on … the tenth by an eleventh, each time reflecting "this too is impermanent, suffering, not-self." Having comprehended matter subject to the ending of growth and aging, the nutriment-made, the temperature-made, the kamma-born, the mind-originated, and nature-matter as "impermanent, suffering, not-self," one comprehends that first state of consciousness by a second, the second by a third, the third by a fourth … and so on … the tenth by an eleventh — in this way, insight might proceed in sequence for an entire portion of the day. But it is only up to the comprehension of the tenth state of consciousness that both the matter-based and immaterial-based meditation subjects become fully mastered; therefore it was said that one should stop at exactly the tenth. One who comprehends in this way is said to comprehend by sequence.

Reading note — Ru-Yi

The difference between "by moments" (four states of consciousness chained together, then restarted from a fresh object) and "by sequence" (an unbroken chain running all the way to an eleventh state, in principle extendable for an entire portion of a day) is a difference of endurance and momentum rather than of underlying method — both simply iterate the same reflexive comprehension link by link. The practical caution attached to the sequence method is worth noting for what it reveals about how this text was actually meant to be used: it is not a purely theoretical scheme but working meditation instruction, complete with a specific, remembered stopping point (the tenth link) beyond which continuing brings no further benefit for mastering the meditation subject, even though the chain could technically be extended much further.

Uprooting View, Conceit, and Attachment

The final three of the seven immaterial methods are shown not to require any separate technique at all: simply seeing that mind-and-matter, comprehensively grasped, leaves no room for any further "being," and — crucially — attributing the very act of insight itself to bare formations rather than to a grasping "I," is what uproots wrong view, uproots conceit, and exhausts attachment. Two parallel explanations are given, from the same two sources cited in the previous section, converging on the same conclusion by slightly different routes.

Ru-Yi Translation

As for these three — the uprooting of view, the uprooting of conceit, and the exhausting of attachment — there is no separate method of comprehension for them. Rather, one who sees the matter grasped earlier and the immaterial grasped here does not see any other being beyond mind-and-matter. From the point of not seeing a being, the perception of a being is uprooted. For one who grasps the formations with a mind whose perception of a being has been uprooted, view does not arise. With view not arising, view is said to be "uprooted." For one who grasps the formations with a mind whose view has been uprooted, conceit does not arise. With conceit not arising, conceit is said to be "uprooted." For one who grasps the formations with a mind whose conceit has been uprooted, craving does not arise. With craving not arising, attachment is said to be "exhausted." This, so far, is what was said in the Discussion of Purification.

But in the Discussion of the Noble Lineages, having set out the summary list "by the uprooting of view, by the uprooting of conceit, by the exhausting of attachment," this method was shown:

For one who grasps it as "I see with insight, this insight is mine," there is no uprooting of view. But for one who grasps it as "formations alone see with insight, comprehend, determine, grasp, and delimit formations," there is uprooting of view.

For one who grasps it as "I see with insight well, I see with insight pleasingly," there is no uprooting of conceit. But for one who grasps it as "formations alone see with insight, comprehend, determine, grasp, and delimit formations," there is uprooting of conceit.

For one who savors insight, thinking "I am able to see with insight," there is no exhausting of attachment. But for one who grasps it as "formations alone see with insight, comprehend, determine, grasp, and delimit formations," there is exhausting of attachment.

If the formations were self, it would be fitting to grasp them as self; but, being not-self, they are grasped as self. Therefore, for one who sees that they are not-self by reason of not submitting to one's will, impermanent by reason of ceasing to exist after having been, and suffering by reason of being afflicted by arising and passing away, there is uprooting of view.

If the formations were permanent, it would be fitting to grasp them as permanent; but, being impermanent, they are grasped as permanent. Therefore, for one who sees that they are impermanent by reason of ceasing to exist after having been, suffering by reason of being afflicted by arising and passing away, and not-self by reason of not submitting to one's will, there is uprooting of conceit.

If the formations were pleasurable, it would be fitting to grasp them as pleasurable; but, being suffering, they are grasped as pleasurable. Therefore, for one who sees that they are suffering by reason of being afflicted by arising and passing away, impermanent by reason of ceasing to exist after having been, and not-self by reason of not submitting to one's will, there is exhausting of attachment.

Thus, for one who sees the formations as not-self, there is uprooting of view; for one who sees them as impermanent, there is uprooting of conceit; for one who sees them as suffering, there is exhausting of attachment. Thus this insight remains, each in its own respective place.

Thus he comprehends the formations, having applied the three characteristics by way of the sevenfold immaterial as well. To this extent, both the matter-based and the immaterial-based meditation subjects have become fully mastered for him.

Reading note — Ru-Yi

The Ariyavaṃsa-kathā's version cuts remarkably close to the bone: it does not merely warn against grasping the objects of insight as "I" or "mine" — a familiar enough caution — but explicitly targets the meditator's pride in the practice of insight itself. "I see with insight well, I see with insight pleasingly" and "I am able to see with insight" are subtler, more refined forms of self-view than ordinary sensory craving, precisely because they arise from something genuinely praiseworthy (skillful meditation) rather than something obviously blameworthy. The remedy offered is equally precise: reattributing the very act of comprehending — "formations alone see with insight, comprehend, determine, grasp, and delimit formations" — to formations themselves rather than to an observing self, closing off the last and most tempting place for "I" to reassert itself.

The second explanation's counterfactual structure ("if the formations were self, it would be fitting to grasp them as self; but they are not-self, and yet are grasped as self") is a compact logical device that will recur in various forms throughout the remainder of the Visuddhimagga: rather than arguing directly against self-view, it exposes self-view as a specific factual error — treating something one way when its own established nature (already proven not-self, impermanent, and suffering by every method this chapter has walked through) demands the opposite treatment.

The Eighteen Great Insights

With both the matter-based and immaterial-based meditation subjects now fully mastered, Buddhaghosa pauses to locate this achievement within a larger canonical map: eighteen "great insights," each pairing a specific contemplation with the specific wrong perception it abandons. A close look shows that several of these eighteen — impermanence, suffering, not-self, and their listed synonyms — have, in a real sense, already been reached by everything this chapter has covered so far, while the rest remain for later chapters to complete.

Reading note — Ru-Yi

This section functions as a progress report inserted directly into the middle of the chapter's own unfolding argument, and its honesty is worth noting: rather than claiming the comprehension work just completed accomplishes everything insight practice eventually will, Buddhaghosa carefully sorts the eighteen great insights into those already penetrated (impermanence, suffering, not-self, and their listed synonyms, plus insight-in-general and knowledge of things as they truly are, already secured back in purification by overcoming doubt) and those still to come, promising an explicit accounting "further on" — a promise that chapter twenty-one, treating the contemplation of dissolution and full understanding by abandoning in detail, will fulfill.

The pairing of each contemplation with a specific wrong perception it abandons is a small masterclass in precision: "disenchantment" abandons specifically "delight," not craving in general; "cessation" abandons specifically "origination," the arising side of dependent origination rather than suffering itself; "the signless" abandons specifically "the sign," the mental image or characteristic mark by which an object is normally recognized and clung to. Each contemplation is thus shown to have a exact, narrow target rather than functioning as an undifferentiated antidote to delusion in general.

Knowledge of Rise and Fall: The Lute Simile

The eighth subhead introduces a new and more advanced knowledge, contemplation of rise and fall, first undertaken "in brief" through a canonical definition: matter's coming-to-be is its rise, its change is its fall. A beautiful simile then dissolves any lingering sense that something might travel between non-existence and existence — just as a lute's sound has no storehouse it comes from or goes to, but simply arises dependent on the instrument and a player's effort, so too do all material and immaterial states come to be, not having been, and pass away, having been.

Reading note — Ru-Yi

Contemplation of rise and fall (udayabbayānupassanā) marks a genuine escalation from everything the chapter has covered so far: comprehension-knowledge established that formations are impermanent, suffering, and not-self by examining them at ever-finer intervals, but this new knowledge examines the very moments of arising and ceasing themselves, directly, as they happen — no longer working from inference across intervals but from the immediate characteristics of coming-to-be and change.

The lute simile deserves to be read alongside chapter eighteen's own drum-and-sound simile, which it closely resembles but subtly extends: where the drum simile established that mind and matter, though dependently arisen, are distinct and unmixed, the lute simile here adds the further claim that the dependently arisen thing itself — the sound, standing in for any and every formation — has absolutely no prior or subsequent existence anywhere at all, neither stored up beforehand nor persisting afterward in any hidden repository. This is the precise psychological content of "coming to be, not having been, and passing away, having been" (ahutvā sambhoti, hutvā paṭiveti), a phrase that will recur through the rest of this subhead as its defining refrain.

Rise and Fall in Full Detail: Fifty Characteristics Across the Five Aggregates

Having grasped rise and fall in brief, the meditator now works through it in full canonical detail: each aggregate's arising traced to the arising of its specific conditions (ignorance, craving, kamma, and either nutriment, contact, or mind-and-matter, depending on the aggregate), each aggregate's fall traced to the cessation of those same conditions, yielding exactly ten characteristics per aggregate and fifty in all — attended to, throughout, in two complementary ways: by condition, and by the bare moment of arising or ceasing itself.

Reading note — Ru-Yi

The arithmetic here is worth making explicit: each aggregate's arising is seen five ways (through the arising of its four specific conditions, plus through directly seeing its characteristic of coming-to-be), and its falling likewise five ways, for ten characteristics per aggregate; multiplied across all five aggregates, this yields the fifty characteristics the subhead's closing section will explicitly name. The consciousness-aggregate's own variation — mind-and-matter standing in where contact stands for the other mental aggregates — reflects dependent origination's own precise causal ordering, in which mind-and-matter conditions the six sense bases and only then contact, while consciousness itself is instead conditioned, one step earlier in the chain, directly by the formations and, in the reverse direction, itself conditions mind-and-matter.

The distinction between seeing rise and fall "by condition" and "by moment," introduced briefly at the end of this section, will structure everything that follows for the remainder of the subhead: the two are not competing methods but complementary lenses, one tracing causal dependency across time, the other examining the instantaneous characteristics of a single arising or ceasing — and, as the next section will show, each lens illuminates a different piece of the four noble truths and of dependent origination's own internal logic.

How the Four Noble Truths and Dependent Origination Become Evident

The two complementary modes of seeing rise and fall — by condition, by moment — now turn out to illuminate, almost as a byproduct, the entire structure of the four noble truths and both directions of dependent origination: seeing rise by condition reveals the truth of origination and forward dependent origination; seeing rise by moment reveals the truth of suffering; seeing fall by condition reveals the truth of cessation and reverse dependent origination; seeing fall by moment reveals suffering once more, this time as the suffering of death; and the very act of seeing all of this clearly reveals the mundane path itself.

Through seeing rise by way of moment, the truth of suffering becomes evident, by understanding the suffering of birth. Through seeing fall by way of condition, the truth of cessation becomes evident, by understanding the non-arising of conditioned things through the non-arising of their conditions. Through seeing fall by way of moment, the truth of suffering alone becomes evident, by understanding the suffering of death. And through his very seeing of rise and fall — "this is the mundane path" — the truth of the path becomes evident, by the dispelling of confusion regarding it.

Through his seeing fall by way of condition, dependent origination in reverse order becomes evident, by understanding "with the cessation of this, that ceases." And through his seeing rise and fall by way of moment, dependently arisen states become evident, by understanding the characteristic of the conditioned. Reading note — Ru-Yi

This section performs a remarkable feat of doctrinal integration: a single, unified act of attention — watching an aggregate rise and fall, alternately by condition and by moment — is shown to spontaneously disclose the entire architecture of the four noble truths and both directions of the twelve-factor chain, without requiring any separate analytical exercise for each. The truth of suffering appears twice, once through the moment of birth and once through the moment of death, which is a precise and deliberate doubling: suffering is not confined to any single dramatic event but is present at both boundaries of every single arising, no matter how momentary.

The closing observation on the "mundane path" (maggo lokiko) is easy to pass by but carries real weight: it identifies the very act of seeing rise and fall clearly, right now, as itself already a form of the path — not the supramundane noble path that lies ahead, but its direct precursor, operating by the same principle (dispelling confusion) that the noble path will later complete decisively. This is the same insight chapter twenty's own opening section named as "conformable acceptance," now shown concretely in operation.

The Four Methods and Four Characteristics

The same twofold seeing of rise and fall goes on to disclose two further sets of categories already familiar from chapter seventeen's closing "wheel of becoming": the four methods of unity, diversity, non-involvement, and "thus is its nature" (each paired here with a specific wrong view it abandons more thoroughly), and the defining characteristics of not-self, impermanence, suffering, and individual nature themselves.

Pali Source

Then he abandons the eternalist view more thoroughly. And through his seeing rise and fall by way of condition, the method of non-involvement becomes evident, by understanding states' not submitting to one's will. Then he abandons the view of self more thoroughly. And through his seeing rise by way of condition, the method of "thus is its nature" becomes evident, by understanding the arising of a result corresponding to its condition. Then he abandons the view of non-efficacy of action more thoroughly.

And through his seeing rise by way of condition, the characteristic of not-self becomes evident, by understanding states' being without any striving of their own and being bound up with their conditions for their very occurrence. Through his seeing rise and fall by way of moment, the characteristic of impermanence becomes evident, by understanding their ceasing to exist after having been, and by understanding their separation from what precedes and what follows. The characteristic of suffering, too, becomes evident, by understanding the affliction caused by rise and fall. The characteristic of individual nature, too, becomes evident, by understanding their being delimited by rise and fall. Reading note — Ru-Yi

Readers who followed chapter seventeen's closing treatment of the "four methods" (ekattanaya, nānattanaya, abyāpāranaya, evaṃdhammatānaya) will recognize this section as a direct application of that same framework, now shown arising naturally from the practice of watching rise and fall rather than from doctrinal analysis alone. The pairing with specific wrong views remains identical to chapter seventeen's own account — unity guards against annihilationism, diversity against eternalism, non-involvement against self-view, and "thus is its nature" against denying that actions have consequences — confirming that this chapter's meditative technique and that chapter's philosophical analysis are, in the end, two routes to exactly the same understanding.

The final observation, on the "temporariness of the characteristic of being conditioned," makes a subtle but important point about individual nature (sabhāvalakkhaṇa): even a state's own defining characteristic is not something it possesses continuously throughout its brief existence, but something delimited precisely by the boundaries of rise and fall themselves — fall is simply absent at the moment of rise, and rise is simply absent at the moment of fall, meaning even a single "moment" resolves, on sufficiently close inspection, into distinguishable phases with no single unchanging feature running through all of them.

Formations Appear Ever-New: The Closing Similes

The subhead closes with one of the treatise's richest clusters of imagery: formations, now seen through and through as rising and falling by both condition and moment, present themselves to the meditator as endlessly fresh, of the briefest possible duration, and entirely without substance — likened in quick succession to a dewdrop, a water-bubble, a line drawn on water, a mustard seed balanced on an awl's point, a lightning-flash, a magic trick, a mirage, a dream, a fire-wheel, a phantom city, foam, and a banana trunk. Having penetrated exactly fifty such characteristics, the meditator's insight is now firmly enough established to earn him a new title: "one who has embarked on insight."

And not only do they present themselves as ever-new, but also as of the briefest duration — like a dewdrop at sunrise, like a water-bubble, like a line drawn on water with a stick, like a mustard-seed balanced on the point of an awl, like a flash of lightning. And they present themselves too as without substance, without core — like a conjuring trick, a mirage, a dream, a fire-wheel, a phantom city, foam, or a banana-trunk.

To this extent, this meditator has attained, having penetrated exactly fifty characteristics in this manner — "only what has the nature to pass away arises, and what has arisen goes to its passing away" — the tender insight-knowledge called the contemplation of rise and fall, standing firm; and through attaining this, he comes to be reckoned as "one who has embarked on insight."

Reading note — Ru-Yi

The twelve similes divide naturally into two groups, each making a distinct point. The first five — dewdrop, water-bubble, water-line, mustard-seed, lightning — all emphasize sheer brevity: each names something proverbially short-lived, and their cumulative effect is to make "briefest duration" felt rather than merely asserted. The remaining seven — magic trick, mirage, dream, fire-wheel (a burning stick twirled fast enough to appear as a solid ring of fire), phantom city (a mirage-like illusion of a city seen by travelers), foam, and banana-trunk (which has no solid core when its layers are peeled away) — all emphasize insubstantiality rather than brevity: each names something that looks solid, real, or continuous but dissolves into nothing the moment it is closely examined, exactly paralleling what this entire subhead's method of ever-finer division has just demonstrated about formations themselves.

The new title earned here, āraddhavipassako, "one who has embarked on insight," is significant for what comes immediately next in the treatise: this is precisely the status chapter twenty's own opening section identified as the trigger for illumination and the other corruptions of insight to arise — meaning the meditator who has just reached this point, having worked through the entire elaborate structure of comprehension and rise-and-fall, is now standing at exactly the threshold where the chapter's next, most famous subhead begins.

The Ten Corruptions of Insight Named

The ninth subhead opens the chapter's most famous teaching: as tender knowledge of rise and fall matures, ten distinct and genuinely pleasant experiences can arise — illumination, knowledge, rapture, tranquility, bliss, resolution, exertion, establishment, equanimity, and attachment — each one capable, if misread, of derailing insight entirely. Tellingly, these corruptions do not afflict the lazy or those who have abandoned practice; they arise specifically for the diligent, properly practicing meditator, precisely because they are the genuine fruits of real progress.

And what are these ten corruptions? Illumination, knowledge, rapture, tranquility, bliss, resolution, exertion, establishment, equanimity, and attachment. For this was said:

"How is one whose mind is seized by agitation about the Dhamma? For one attending as impermanent, illumination arises; he adverts to the illumination, thinking 'illumination is the Dhamma'; from that, distraction — agitation. With his mind seized by that agitation, he does not know, as it truly is, its presentation as impermanent. As suffering … as not-self, he does not know its presentation as it truly is."

Likewise: "for one attending as impermanent, knowledge arises … and so on … rapture … tranquility … bliss … resolution … exertion … establishment … equanimity … attachment arises; he adverts to the attachment, thinking 'attachment is the Dhamma'; from that, distraction — agitation. With his mind seized by that agitation, he does not know, as it truly is, its presentation as impermanent. As suffering … as not-self, he does not know its presentation as it truly is."

Reading note — Ru-Yi

The Paṭisambhidāmagga's own formula, quoted here, names the precise mechanism of each corruption with remarkable psychological precision: it is not the arising of illumination, knowledge, or any of the other nine that itself constitutes the corruption, but the secondary act of adverting to it and mistaking it for "the Dhamma" — treating what is merely a byproduct of correct practice as though it were the goal or the path itself. That misidentification, not the pleasant experience underlying it, is what the text calls "agitation" (uddhacca), and it is this agitation that then blocks the very insight that produced the pleasant experience in the first place.

That these corruptions specifically spare both noble disciples (who have already directly seen Nibbāna and so can no longer mistake any conditioned experience for the path) and lazy or wrongly-practicing meditators (who never generate strong enough insight for these refined states to arise at all) makes an important point about who this teaching is actually for: it is a warning aimed precisely at competent, diligent, sincerely practicing meditators at a specific, advanced stage — arguably the single group most vulnerable to mistaking real progress for final attainment, precisely because their progress really is genuine.

Illumination: The Two Elders, and Monks Who Mistook Themselves for Arahants

The first corruption, illumination, is treated at greatest length — beginning with the meditator's characteristic false inference ("surely I have attained the path, attained the fruit"), a note on how dramatically its intensity varies from one meditator to another (from lighting only one's own seat to, in the Buddha's own case, illuminating an entire ten-thousandfold world-system), a charming story of two elders competitively describing what their own illumination lets them see through pitch darkness, and the sober observation that this particular corruption most often afflicts those who have already attained both serenity and insight — three respected elders, named outright, among them.

Herein, "illumination" is the illumination of insight. When it arises, the meditator thinks: "truly, no such illumination as this has ever arisen for me before; surely I have attained the path, attained the fruit" — and he grasps what is not the path as "the path," what is not the fruit as "the fruit." And here, for this variation, is a story: at Cittalapabbata, it is said, two elders sat within a two-walled dwelling. That day happened to be the uposatha of the dark fortnight; the quarters were covered with a bank of clouds; and during the night, a darkness endowed with all four factors of impenetrable darkness prevailed. Then one elder said: "Venerable sir, right now, on the shrine terrace, I can make out five-colored flowers on the lion-seat." The other said to him: "That is nothing remarkable to speak of, friend — I, just now, at a distance of a yojana out in the great ocean, can make out fish and turtles."

Since the defilements suppressed by attainment do not manifest, such a one forms the thought "I am an arahant" — like the elder Mahānāga who dwelt at Uccavālika, like the elder Mahādatta who dwelt at Haṅkana, and like the elder Cūḷasumana who dwelt in the meditation hut at Niṅkapeṇṇaka on Cittalapabbata.

Reading note — Ru-Yi

The two elders' exchange is one of the Visuddhimagga's most quietly comic moments, and its placement here is deliberate: rather than simply asserting that illumination varies in intensity, Buddhaghosa lets two accomplished monks demonstrate the corruption in real time, each casually topping the other's claim about what pitch darkness cannot hide from their meditative light. Neither elder is condemned outright for the exchange — the story illustrates the phenomenon's genuine variability rather than moralizing against it — but the passage's placement, immediately before three named elders who each briefly mistook themselves for arahants, makes the danger unmistakable: the same illumination that can be a harmless subject of collegial one-upmanship can also, unchecked, produce a false conviction of complete liberation.

Note the important qualifier: this corruption "generally arises for one who has attained both serenity and insight" (samathavipassanālābhī) — meaning it specifically afflicts meditators who have already suppressed the defilements through jhāna, so that ordinary greed, hatred, and delusion simply do not manifest in the moment. Mistaking the temporary absence of manifest defilement (which jhāna alone can produce) for its permanent eradication (which only the supramundane path can produce) is exactly the error the following story, of Dhammadinna and his own teacher, will dramatize in unforgettable detail.

"a darkness endowed with four factors" — a traditional description of the deepest, most impenetrable kind of darkness (moonless night, cloud-covered sky, dense forest, and the small hours before dawn, by various commentarial reckonings), invoked here to set up just how extraordinary the two elders' claimed illumination-powered vision actually was.

The Monk Who Thought He Was Enlightened: Dhammadinna and Mahānāga

One story is now told in full: the arahant Dhammadinna, worried that his own former teacher, the elder Mahānāga, might die still an unenlightened worldling despite believing himself an arahant, flies to him, asks a thousand probing questions the teacher answers without hesitation — and then, with a single test involving a conjured, charging elephant, exposes the fear still lurking beneath Mahānāga's mistaken confidence, prompting the older elder to humbly accept meditation instruction from his own former student and attain genuine arahantship within three paces of walking meditation.

Pali Source

Tatridaṃ ekavatthuparidīpanaṃ – talaṅgaravāsī dhammadinnatthero kira nāma eko pabhinnapaṭisambhido mahākhīṇāsavo mahato bhikkhusaṅghassa ovādadāyako ahosi. So ekadivasaṃ attano divāṭṭhāne nisīditvā "kinnu kho amhākaṃ ācariyassa uccavālikavāsīmahānāgattherassa samaṇabhāvakiccaṃ matthakaṃ pattaṃ, no"ti āvajjanto puthujjanabhāvamevassa disvā "mayi agacchante puthujjanakālakiriyameva karissatī"ti ca ñatvā iddhiyā vehāsaṃ uppatitvā divāṭṭhāne nisinnassa therassa samīpe orohitvā vanditvā vattaṃ dassetvā ekamantaṃ nisīdi. "Kiṃ, āvuso dhammadinna, akāle āgatosī"ti ca vutte "pañhaṃ, bhante, pucchituṃ āgatomhī"ti āha. Tato "pucchāvuso, jānamānā kathayissāmā"ti vutte pañhasahassaṃ pucchi.

Thero pucchitapucchitaṃ asajjamānova kathesi. Tato "atitikkhaṃ vo, bhante, ñāṇaṃ, kadā tumhehi ayaṃ dhammo adhigato"ti vutte "ito saṭṭhivassakāle, āvuso"ti āha. Samādhimpi, bhante, vaḷañjethāti, na yidaṃ, āvuso, bhāriyanti. Tena hi, bhante, ekaṃ hatthiṃ māpethāti. Thero sabbasetaṃ hatthiṃ māpesi. Idāni, bhante, yathā ayaṃ hatthī añcitakaṇṇo pasāritanaṅguṭṭho soṇḍaṃ mukhe pakkhipitvā bheravaṃ koñcanādaṃ karonto tumhākaṃ abhimukho āgacchati, tathā naṃ karothāti. Thero tathā katvā vegena āgacchato hatthissa bheravaṃ ākāraṃ disvā uṭṭhāya palāyituṃ āraddho. Tamenaṃ khīṇāsavatthero hatthaṃ pasāretvā cīvarakaṇṇe gahetvā "bhante, khīṇāsavassa sārajjaṃ nāma hotī"ti āha.

So tamhi kāle attano puthujjanabhāvaṃ ñatvā "avassayo me, āvuso, dhammadinna hohī"ti vatvā pādamūle ukkuṭikaṃ nisīdi. "Bhante, tumhākaṃ avassayo bhavissāmiccevāhaṃ āgato, mā cintayitthā"ti kammaṭṭhānaṃ kathesi. Thero kammaṭṭhānaṃ gahetvā caṅkamaṃ āruyha tatiye padavāre aggaphalaṃ arahattaṃ pāpuṇi. Thero kira dosacarito ahosi. Evarūpā bhikkhū obhāse kampanti.

Ru-Yi Translation

Here, then, is the illustration by one particular story: the elder Dhammadinna, who dwelt at Talaṅgara, it is said, was one who had attained the analytical knowledges, a great one whose cankers were destroyed, a giver of instruction to a great community of monks. One day, sitting in his own daytime dwelling-place and reflecting, "has the task of the ascetic life reached its culmination for our teacher, the elder Mahānāga who dwells at Uccavālika, or not?", he saw that he was still, in fact, an ordinary worldling, and, knowing that "if I do not go, he will die still an ordinary worldling," he rose up into the air by psychic power, descended near the elder, who was sitting in his own daytime dwelling-place, paid homage, showed the customary courtesy, and sat down to one side. When asked, "Why, friend Dhammadinna, have you come at an unusual hour?", he said, "Venerable sir, I have come to ask a question." Then, being told, "Ask, friend; if we know, we shall answer," he asked a thousand questions.

The elder answered whatever was asked without hesitation. Then, being told, "Your knowledge, venerable sir, is exceedingly sharp — when did you attain this Dhamma?", he said, "Sixty years ago, friend." "Venerable sir, do you also practice concentration?" "That is no great matter, friend." "Then, venerable sir, conjure up an elephant." The elder conjured up an entirely white elephant. "Now, venerable sir, make it so that this elephant, ears laid back, tail outstretched, trunk thrust into its mouth, comes toward you making a terrifying trumpeting roar." The elder did so; and, seeing the terrifying appearance of the elephant rushing swiftly toward him, he rose up and began to flee. Then the elder whose cankers were destroyed, stretching out his hand, caught him by the hem of his robe and said, "Venerable sir, does fear still arise even for one whose cankers are destroyed?"

He, at that moment, knowing his own worldling state, said, "Friend Dhammadinna, be my support," and sat down on his haunches at his feet. "Venerable sir, I have come precisely in order to be your support — do not be troubled," said Dhammadinna, and gave him his meditation subject. The elder, taking up the meditation subject and mounting the walking-path, attained the highest fruit, arahantship, on the third turn of his pacing. It is said the elder's temperament was one of aversion. Monks of this kind tremble at illumination.

Reading note — Ru-Yi

Every detail of this story is doing precise doctrinal work. Mahānāga answers a thousand rapid-fire questions "without hesitation" — genuine, extensive learning and analytical mastery, which is real and considerable, yet still no guarantee of actual liberation. His claim to have attained the Dhamma "sixty years ago" and his offhand dismissal of concentration practice as "no great matter" both display exactly the kind of settled, long-unchallenged confidence that makes a false conviction hardest to dislodge — he has believed himself an arahant for six decades. And the test itself is exquisitely chosen: not a question he could reason his way through, but a sudden, visceral threat bypassing deliberation entirely, the one situation reliably capable of revealing whether fear (bhaya) — one of the defilements jhāna can suppress but only the path can uproot — still lies dormant underneath.

The story's ending is as important as its dramatic middle: Mahānāga does not react with wounded pride or denial when exposed, but immediately, humbly accepts correction from his own former pupil, takes up an actual meditation subject, and attains genuine arahantship within three paces of walking meditation — a resolution that reframes the whole episode not as a story about failure but about the value of correction, and about how close genuine attainment can lie even for someone who has spent sixty years mistaking its counterfeit for the real thing. The closing note that Mahānāga's temperament was "one of aversion" (dosacarita) is a small technical aside explaining why fear specifically, rather than lust or pride, was the defilement chosen to expose him — each temperament type has its own characteristic vulnerability.

Key vocabulary — for Pāli learners
pabhinnapaṭisambhida

"one who had attained the analytical knowledges" — pabhinna (broken open, i.e. fully unfolded) + paṭisambhidā (the four analytical knowledges of meaning, doctrine, language, and ready wit); a standard honorific for an especially accomplished arahant, applied here to Dhammadinna to establish his own unquestionable attainment before the story tests his teacher's.

khīṇāsavassa sārajjaṃ nāma hoti

"does fear arise even for one whose cankers are destroyed?" — khīṇāsava (one whose cankers/taints are destroyed, i.e. an arahant) + sārajja (timidity, fear, nervousness); Dhammadinna's pointed rhetorical question, the exact moment the story's real point lands, since a genuine arahant's cankers include precisely this kind of instinctive fear.

dosacarita

"of aversion temperament" — dosa (hatred, aversion) + carita (habitual behavior, temperament); one of several traditional temperament types the Visuddhimagga catalogues elsewhere (alongside greed-, delusion-, faith-, intelligence-, and speculation-temperaments), invoked here to explain why an aversion-based defilement like fear was what exposed Mahānāga's remaining unenlightenment.

Knowledge, Rapture, and Tranquility: The Verse on Unearthly Delight

Three more corruptions are described in quick succession: knowledge, arising with the force and keenness of Indra's own thunderbolt; rapture, in its full canonical fivefold range from minor tingling to whole-body pervasion; and tranquility, bringing with it what the Dhammapada itself calls a distinctly "superhuman delight" — a joy available, the verse insists, specifically to one who correctly sees the rise and fall of the aggregates.

Pali Source

Ñāṇanti vipassanāñāṇaṃ. Tassa kira rūpārūpadhamme tulayantassa tīrentassa vissaṭṭhaindavajiramiva avihatavegaṃ tikhiṇaṃ sūraṃ ativisadaṃ ñāṇaṃ uppajjati.

Pītīti vipassanāpīti. Tassa kira tasmiṃ samaye khuddakāpīti, khaṇikāpīti, okkantikāpīti, ubbegāpīti, pharaṇāpītīti ayaṃ pañcavidhā pīti sakalasarīraṃ pūrayamānā uppajjati.

Passaddhīti vipassanāpassaddhi. Tassa kira tasmiṃ samaye rattiṭṭhāne vā divāṭṭhāne vā nisinnassa kāyacittānaṃ neva daratho, na gāravaṃ, na kakkhaḷatā, na akammaññatā, na gelaññaṃ, na vaṅkatā hoti, atha kho panassa kāyacittāni passaddhāni lahūni mudūni kammaññāni suvisadāni ujukāniyeva honti. So imehi passaddhādīhi anuggahitakāyacitto tasmiṃ samaye amānusiṃ nāma ratiṃ anubhavati. Yaṃ sandhāya vuttaṃ –

"Suññāgāraṃ paviṭṭhassa, santacittassa bhikkhuno; Amānusī rati hoti, sammā dhammaṃ vipassato.

"Yato yato sammasati, khandhānaṃ udayabbayaṃ; Labhatī pītipāmojjaṃ, amataṃ taṃ vijānata"nti.

Evamassa imaṃ amānusiṃ ratiṃ sādhayamānā lahutādisampayuttā passaddhi uppajjati.

Ru-Yi Translation

Knowledge is insight-knowledge. For one weighing and judging material and immaterial states, there arises, it is said, a knowledge like Indra's own thunderbolt hurled forth — of unchecked force, sharp, keen, exceedingly clear.

Rapture is insight-rapture. At that time, it is said, this fivefold rapture — minor rapture, momentary rapture, showering rapture, uplifting rapture, and pervading rapture — arises, filling the whole body.

Tranquility is insight-tranquility. At that time, it is said, for one sitting in his night-dwelling or day-dwelling place, there is in body and mind neither distress, nor heaviness, nor stiffness, nor unwieldiness, nor sickness, nor crookedness; rather, his body and mind are tranquil, light, malleable, wieldy, exceedingly clear, and upright indeed. Endowed with body and mind supported by this tranquility and the rest, he experiences, at that time, what is called a superhuman delight. With reference to this it was said:

"For a bhikkhu who has entered an empty dwelling, whose mind is at peace, there is a superhuman delight, as he sees with insight, correctly, the Dhamma.

"In whatever way he comprehends the rise and fall of the aggregates, he gains rapture and joy; that, for those who know, is the deathless."

Thus, for him, tranquility associated with lightness and the rest arises, accomplishing this superhuman delight.

Reading note — Ru-Yi

The fivefold rapture catalogued here (minor, momentary, showering, uplifting, and pervading) is a standard scheme drawn from this same treatise's earlier discussion of jhāna factors, now reappearing specifically in an insight context — a small but telling sign that the corruptions of insight are not some separate category of experience invented for this chapter, but the very same refined mental factors cultivated throughout the concentration section, now arising as a natural byproduct of sufficiently mature insight rather than of jhāna absorption itself.

The Dhammapada verses quoted for tranquility carry real interpretive weight precisely because of where they come from: this is canonical scripture praising, in unambiguous terms, the very delight this chapter is about to warn against overvaluing. Buddhaghosa's implicit point is subtle but important — the danger these ten states pose is never that they are false or unworthy experiences (the Buddha himself calls this delight genuinely "unearthly" and links it to "the deathless"), but only that a meditator might mistake this real, praiseworthy fruit of practice for practice's own final destination.

Key vocabulary — for Pāli learners
khuddikā / khaṇikā / okkantikā / ubbegā / pharaṇā pīti

minor / momentary / showering / uplifting / pervading rapture — the standard fivefold classification of pīti (rapture) by increasing intensity and bodily extent, from a slight hair-raising sensation up to a rapture capable of lifting the body off the ground, here recurring specifically as one of the ten insight corruptions.

amānusī rati

"superhuman delight" — a + mānusa (not human, superhuman) + rati (delight, pleasure); the Dhammapada's own term for the joy a meditator experiences upon correctly comprehending the aggregates' rise and fall, quoted here to show that tranquility's pleasant fruit is scripturally praised, not merely tolerated.

Bliss, Resolution, Exertion, Mindfulness, Equanimity, and Attachment

The remaining six corruptions round out the list: bliss suffusing the whole body; resolution, which turns out to be simply an intensified faith; exertion, a perfectly balanced energy neither slack nor strained; establishment, a mindfulness as unshakeable as a mountain king; equanimity, in two forms; and, last and most insidious, attachment itself — a delight in one's own advanced insight so subtle that, as the text bluntly states, it can scarcely even be recognized as a defilement at all.

Pali Source

Sukhanti vipassanāsukhaṃ. Tassa kira tasmiṃ samaye sakalasarīraṃ abhisandayamānaṃ atipaṇītaṃ sukhaṃ uppajjati.

Adhimokkhoti saddhā. Vipassanāsampayuttāyeva hissa cittacetasikānaṃ atisayapasādabhūtā balavatī saddhā uppajjati.

Paggahoti vīriyaṃ. Vipassanāsampayuttameva hissa asithilaṃ anaccāraddhaṃ supaggahitaṃ vīriyaṃ uppajjati.

Upaṭṭhānanti sati. Vipassanāsampayuttāyeva hissa supaṭṭhitā supatiṭṭhitā nikhātā acalā pabbatarājasadisā sati uppajjati. So yaṃ yaṃ ṭhānaṃ āvajjati samannāharati manasikaroti paccavekkhati, taṃ taṃ ṭhānamassa okkhanditvā pakkhanditvā dibbacakkhuno paraloko viya satiyā upaṭṭhāti.

Upekkhāti vipassanupekkhā ceva āvajjanupekkhā ca. Tasmiṃ hissa samaye sabbasaṅkhāresu majjhattabhūtā vipassanupekkhāpi balavatī uppajjati. Manodvāre āvajjanupekkhāpi. Sā hissa taṃ taṃ ṭhānaṃ āvajjantassa vissaṭṭhaindavajiramiva pattapuṭe pakkhitta tattanārāco viya ca sūrā tikhiṇā hutvā vahati.

Nikantīti vipassanānikanti. Evaṃ obhāsādipaṭimaṇḍitāya hissa vipassanāya ālayaṃ kurumānā sukhumā santākārā nikanti uppajjati. Yā nikanti kilesoti pariggahetumpi na sakkā hoti.

Yathā ca obhāse, evaṃ etesupi aññatarasmiṃ uppanne yogāvacaro "na vata me ito pubbe evarūpaṃ ñāṇaṃ uppannapubbaṃ, evarūpā pīti, passaddhi, sukhaṃ, adhimokkho, paggaho, upaṭṭhānaṃ, upekkhā, nikanti uppannapubbā, addhā maggappattosmi phalappattosmī"ti amaggameva "maggo"ti aphalameva ca "phala"nti gaṇhāti. Tassa amaggaṃ "maggo"ti aphalaṃ "phala"nti gaṇhato vipassanāvīthi ukkantā nāma hoti. So attano mūlakammaṭṭhānaṃ vissajjetvā nikantimeva assādento nisīdatīti.

Ru-Yi Translation

Bliss is insight-bliss. At that time, it is said, an exceedingly refined bliss arises, suffusing the whole body.

Resolution is faith. For him, a powerful faith arises, associated precisely with insight, an exceeding clarity belonging to consciousness and its mental factors.

Exertion is energy. For him, an energy associated precisely with insight arises — neither slack nor overstrained, well exerted.

Establishment is mindfulness. For him, a mindfulness associated precisely with insight arises — well established, well set, firmly planted, unmoving, like the king of mountains. Whatever point he adverts to, attends to, brings to mind, or reviews, that point presents itself to his mindfulness, having settled into and entered it, just as the other world presents itself to the divine eye.

Equanimity is both insight-equanimity and adverting-equanimity. At that time, for him, a powerful insight-equanimity, neutral with respect to all formations, arises; and also an adverting-equanimity at the mind-door. This, as he adverts to point after point, proceeds keen and sharp, like Indra's own thunderbolt hurled forth, like a red-hot arrow dropped into a leaf-cup.

Attachment is insight-attachment. Thus, for him, a subtle, peaceful-seeming attachment arises, taking delight in his insight now adorned with illumination and the rest — an attachment so subtle it cannot even be recognized as a defilement.

And just as with illumination, so too, when any one of these arises, the meditator thinks: "truly, no such knowledge as this — no such rapture, tranquility, bliss, resolution, exertion, establishment, equanimity, attachment — has ever arisen for me before; surely I have attained the path, attained the fruit" — and he grasps what is not the path as "the path," what is not the fruit as "the fruit." For one who thus grasps what is not the path as "the path" and what is not the fruit as "the fruit," the course of insight is said to be "cut off." He abandons his original meditation subject and sits there simply savoring the attachment.

Reading note — Ru-Yi

Attachment's placement as the tenth and final corruption is a deliberate structural choice, not an afterthought: every one of the preceding nine corruptions is, strictly speaking, an occasion for attachment rather than a defilement in itself — illumination, knowledge, rapture, and the rest are all, in their own right, wholesome and praiseworthy fruits of practice. Attachment alone is genuinely a defilement, and its subtlety is emphasized precisely because it is the one member of the list capable of contaminating any of the other nine, quietly converting a genuine achievement into a trap the very moment it is savored rather than simply noted and released.

The closing paragraph's structure — repeating, almost verbatim, the same false inference already given for illumination — is itself a teaching device: by the time a reader reaches this ninth repetition of "surely I have attained the path, attained the fruit," the pattern's danger has become unmistakable through sheer accumulated weight, exactly mirroring how insidious and repeatable this particular error actually is in practice.

Key vocabulary — for Pāli learners
adhimokkha

resolution — here explicitly glossed as saddhā (faith), an intensified clarity of confidence associated with insight; distinct from its more common Abhidhamma sense as the mental factor of "deciding" on an object, showing the term's flexible technical range depending on context.

vipassanupekkhā / āvajjanupekkhā

insight-equanimity / adverting-equanimity — two distinguished forms of the ninth corruption, the first a broad neutrality toward all formations, the second a sharper, more active equanimity specifically at the mind-door as attention turns from one point to the next.

nikanti

attachment — the tenth and, uniquely among the ten, the only corruption that is itself genuinely a defilement rather than merely an occasion for one; described here as so subtle and peaceful-seeming that it "cannot even be recognized as a defilement," making it the most dangerous of the ten precisely because it is the hardest to catch.

Thirty Kinds of Grasping

A final piece of analysis multiplies the ten corruptions into thirty: each one can be grasped in any of three ways — through view ("my illumination has arisen"), through conceit ("how pleasing, my illumination"), or through craving (simply delighting in it) — and a closing verse of "the ancients" names each of the ten by which specific quality of trembling it induces in an unwary meditator's mind.

Pali Source

Ettha ca obhāsādayo upakkilesavatthutāya upakkilesāti vuttā, na akusalattā. Nikanti pana upakkileso ceva upakkilesavatthu ca. Vatthuvaseneva cete dasa. Gāhavasena pana samatiṃsa honti. Kathaṃ? "Mama obhāso uppanno"ti gaṇhato hi diṭṭhigāho hoti, "manāpo vata obhāso uppanno"ti gaṇhato mānagāho, obhāsaṃ assādayato taṇhāgāho, iti obhāse diṭṭhimānataṇhāvasena tayo gāhā. Tathā sesesupīti evaṃ gāhavasena samatiṃsa upakkilesā honti. Tesaṃ vasena akusalo abyatto yogāvacaro obhāsādīsu kampati vikkhipati. Obhāsādīsu ekekaṃ "etaṃ mama, esohamasmi, eso me attā"ti samanupassati. Tenāhu porāṇā –

"Obhāse ceva ñāṇe ca, pītiyā ca vikampati; Passaddhiyā sukhe ceva, yehi cittaṃ pavedhati.

"Adhimokkhe ca paggāhe, upaṭṭhāne ca kampati; Upekkhāvajjanāyañca, upekkhāya nikantiyā"ti.

Ru-Yi Translation

And here, illumination and the rest are called "corruptions" because they are the basis for corruption, not because they are themselves unwholesome. Attachment, however, is both a corruption and a basis for corruption. By way of their basis alone, these are ten; but by way of grasping, they become thirty. How? For one who grasps "my illumination has arisen," there is the grasping of view; for one who grasps "how pleasing, illumination has arisen," there is the grasping of conceit; for one who delights in the illumination, there is the grasping of craving — thus, with respect to illumination, there are three graspings, by way of view, conceit, and craving. Likewise for the rest — thus, by way of grasping, there are thirty corruptions. Under their influence, an unskilled, untrained meditator trembles and becomes distracted with respect to illumination and the rest, regarding each one of them as "this is mine, this I am, this is my self." Hence the ancients said:

"He trembles at illumination and at knowledge, and at rapture — these by which the mind quivers; at tranquility and at bliss as well.

"He trembles at resolution and at exertion, and at establishment; and at the equanimity of adverting, and at the equanimity of attachment."

Reading note — Ru-Yi

The three-way multiplication into view, conceit, and craving is a recurring analytical pattern throughout the Abhidhamma tradition, mapping the ten corruptions onto the exact same threefold root-structure the treatise has used elsewhere for craving-based clinging in general: the grasping of view treats the experience as evidence of a real, substantial self or attainment; the grasping of conceit measures and compares it favorably against some standard; the grasping of craving simply delights in it for its own sake. Distinguishing these three modes matters practically, since they call for slightly different remedies — view calls for correct doctrinal understanding, conceit for humility, and craving for dispassion — even though all three converge on the same underlying corruption.

Key vocabulary — for Pāli learners
upakkilesavatthu

"a basis for corruption" — upakkilesa (corruption) + vatthu (basis, ground, object); the precise technical status of illumination, knowledge, and the eight other non-craving corruptions — not themselves unwholesome, but capable of becoming an object around which the genuinely unwholesome graspings of view, conceit, or craving can form.

diṭṭhigāha / mānagāha / taṇhāgāha

the grasping of view / of conceit / of craving — the three modes by which any of the ten corruptions can be seized upon, multiplying the base list of ten into thirty; a standard threefold analytical scheme recurring throughout the Abhidhamma for how clinging attaches itself to an object.

Seeing Through the Corruptions

The tenth and final subhead resolves the whole teaching: a skilled, wise meditator, when any of the ten corruptions arises, does not savor it but immediately subjects it to exactly the same analysis already applied to every other object in this chapter — impermanent, conditioned, dependently arisen; not-self, since if it were self it could be rightly grasped as self, yet it is not — and thereby regards it, correctly, as "not mine, not I, not my self," never once trembling or becoming distracted.

Pali Source

Kusalo pana paṇḍito byatto buddhisampanno yogāvacaro obhāsādīsu uppannesu "ayaṃ kho me obhāso uppanno, so kho panāyaṃ anicco saṅkhato paṭiccasamuppanno khayadhammo vayadhammo virāgadhammo nirodhadhammo"ti iti vā taṃ paññāya paricchindati upaparikkhati. Atha vā panassa evaṃ hoti, "sace obhāso attā bhaveyya, 'attā'ti gahetuṃ vaṭṭeyya. Anattā ca panāyaṃ 'attā'ti gahito. Tasmā so avasavattanaṭṭhena anattā, hutvā abhāvaṭṭhena anicco, uppādavayapaṭipīḷanaṭṭhena dukkho"ti sabbaṃ arūpasattake vuttanayena vitthāretabbaṃ. Yathā ca obhāse, evaṃ sesesupi.

So evaṃ upaparikkhitvā obhāsaṃ "netaṃ mama, nesohamasmi, na meso attā"ti samanupassati. Ñāṇaṃ…pe… nikantiṃ "netaṃ mama, nesohamasmi, na meso attā"ti samanupassati. Evaṃ samanupassanto obhāsādīsu na kampati na vedhati. Tenāhu porāṇā –

"Imāni dasa ṭhānāni, paññāyassa pariccitā; Dhammuddhaccakusalo hoti, na ca vikkhepaṃ gacchatī"ti.

Ru-Yi Translation

But a skilled, wise, trained meditator, endowed with understanding, when illumination and the rest arise, delimits and examines it with wisdom thus: "this illumination has indeed arisen for me; but this is impermanent, conditioned, dependently arisen, subject to ending, subject to passing away, subject to fading away, subject to cessation" — or else it occurs to him: "if illumination were self, it would be fitting to grasp it as 'self.' But it is not-self, and yet is grasped as 'self.' Therefore it is not-self by reason of not submitting to one's will, impermanent by reason of ceasing to exist after having been, and suffering by reason of being afflicted by arising and passing away" — all this to be elaborated in full by the method already stated in the sevenfold immaterial. And just as with illumination, so too with the rest.

Having examined it in this way, he regards illumination thus: "this is not mine, this I am not, this is not my self." Knowledge … and so on … attachment, too, he regards thus: "this is not mine, this I am not, this is not my self." Regarding it in this way, he does not tremble or quiver at illumination and the rest. Hence the ancients said:

"These ten states have been thoroughly mastered by his wisdom; he becomes skilled regarding agitation about the Dhamma, and does not fall into distraction."

Reading note — Ru-Yi

The remedy this section prescribes is, on inspection, nothing new at all — it is simply the same threefold-characteristic analysis (impermanent, suffering, not-self) this entire chapter has already applied, at ever-finer resolution, to matter, to the immaterial, and to insight-knowledge examining itself. This is the whole point: illumination and the other nine corruptions are not a different kind of thing requiring a different kind of treatment, but simply nine more objects within the same universe of conditioned, dependently arisen phenomena this chapter has been training the meditator to see through from its very first section. The only thing that has changed is the object's unusual attractiveness, which is precisely why explicit warning, rather than trust in the meditator's default reaction, was needed.

Maggāmaggañāṇadassanavisuddhi Complete: The Three Truths Established

Having disentangled all thirty modes of grasping across the ten corruptions, the meditator arrives at last at the chapter's own definition, restated now with full understanding: illumination and the rest are not the path; insight-knowledge itself, freed of corruption and running along its own course, is the path. A brief closing accounting shows that three of the four noble truths — suffering, origination, and now the path — have already been determined by purely mundane knowledge across the last three chapters, with only the truth of cessation, Nibbāna itself, still to come.

Ru-Yi Translation

Not falling into distraction in this way, and having disentangled that thirtyfold tangle of corruption, he determines that illumination and the rest are not the path, but that insight-knowledge, freed from the corruptions and proceeding along its own course, is the path — thus determining both path and not-path. The knowledge that thus stands, having known both path and not-path — "this is the path, this is not the path" — should be understood as purification by knowledge and vision of what is path and what is not path.

And to this extent, three of the truths have been determined by him. How? In purification of view, first, the truth of suffering was determined, by the determining of mind-and-matter. In purification by overcoming doubt, the truth of origination was determined, by the grasping of conditions. In this purification by knowledge and vision of what is path and what is not path, the truth of the path has been determined, by the ascertaining of the right path. Thus, by mundane knowledge alone, so far, three of the truths have been determined.

Reading note — Ru-Yi

The chapter's closing accounting is a small masterstroke of architectural clarity, retroactively revealing a structure that was not obvious while each individual chapter was unfolding on its own terms: purification of view (chapter eighteen) determined the truth of suffering by fixing exactly what mind-and-matter is; purification by overcoming doubt (chapter nineteen) determined the truth of origination by fixing exactly what conditions it; and this chapter determines the truth of the path by fixing exactly what practice actually leads forward, as against its ten most convincing impostors. Three of the four noble truths, first taught in the Buddha's very first sermon, have now each received their own full chapter of dedicated, technical elaboration — with only the truth of cessation, Nibbāna itself, necessarily left for direct realization on the supramundane path rather than mundane analysis, since Nibbāna is, by its very nature, not something mundane knowledge can determine in the way suffering, origination, and the path can be.

That the ten corruptions receive by far this chapter's longest and most vivid treatment — outweighing the comprehension-by-groups material, the sevenfold matter scheme, and knowledge of rise and fall combined — is itself a kind of implicit teaching about where real danger lies in the practice of insight. The earlier subheads are technically demanding but relatively safe: a meditator who fails to master the forty aspects of contemplation, or the exact arithmetic of kamma-born matter, simply has not yet finished the work. The ten corruptions are dangerous precisely because success, not failure, is what produces them — which is exactly why this particular purification bears the name it does: not purification by attaining insight, but purification by knowing, with certainty, the difference between the path and everything that merely resembles it.
