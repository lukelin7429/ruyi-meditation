Taking Up a Meditation Subject

This page carries Ru-Yi's translation of the Visuddhimagga's chapter 3, Kammaṭṭhānaggahaṇaniddesa ("The Description of the Taking-Up of a Meditation Subject") — the opening of the treatise's second major part, on concentration (samādhi), following the same seven/eight-question method used to open the first chapter on virtue. This page opens with why concentration must now be treated, the guiding questions, and concentration's many classifications by way of one kind, two kinds, three kinds, four kinds, and five kinds.

Why Concentration Comes Next

The chapter opens exactly as Sīlaniddesa did — by justifying its own topic from the treatise's founding verse — before laying out the eight guiding questions that will structure everything to come.

Pali Source

Idāni yasmā evaṃ dhutaṅgapariharaṇasampāditehi appicchatādīhi guṇehi pariyodāte imasmiṃ sīle patiṭṭhitena "sīle patiṭṭhāya naro sapañño, cittaṃ paññañca bhāvaya"nti vacanato cittasīsena niddiṭṭho samādhi bhāvetabbo. So ca atisaṅkhepadesitattā viññātumpi tāva na sukaro, pageva bhāvetuṃ, tasmā tassa vitthārañca bhāvanānayañca dassetuṃ idaṃ pañhākammaṃ hoti.

Ko samādhi? Kenaṭṭhena samādhi? Kānassa lakkhaṇarasapaccupaṭṭhānapadaṭṭhānāni? Katividho samādhi? Ko cassa saṃkileso? Kiṃ vodānaṃ? Kathaṃ bhāvetabbo? Samādhibhāvanāya ko ānisaṃsoti?

Ru-Yi Translation

Now, since, for one established in this virtue — cleansed by such qualities as wanting little, accomplished through the maintenance of the ascetic practices — concentration, pointed to by the word "mind" in the saying "a man of wisdom, established in virtue, should develop mind and understanding," should next be developed; and since that teaching, being given there in the utmost brevity, is not even easy to understand, let alone to develop — therefore, in order to show its detail and the method of its development, this series of questions is undertaken.

What is concentration? In what sense is it "concentration"? What are its characteristic, function, manifestation, and proximate cause? Of how many kinds is it? What is its defilement? What is its cleansing? How should it be developed? What is the benefit of developing concentration?

Reading note — Ru-Yi

The chapter's opening move is identical in structure to Sīlaniddesa's, and deliberately so: both chapters justify themselves by pointing back to the very same verse, "established in virtue, a man of wisdom should develop mind and understanding," treating it as a table of contents for the entire treatise in miniature. Sīlaniddesa unpacked "virtue"; this chapter now unpacks "mind" (citta), and the still-unopened third term, "understanding" (paññā), is left standing as the treatise's third and final part, not yet due.

The eight questions are the same seven-question matrix used to open the discussion of virtue, with one difference: "how should it be developed" is added as its own explicit question here, where Sīlaniddesa's treatment of virtue's cultivation was folded into its "kinds" question instead. This is a small but telling sign that concentration, unlike virtue, is defined by this text primarily as a thing to be actively developed through a specific method — which is exactly what the rest of this chapter, and the many chapters after it, will spend most of their length describing.

Key vocabulary — for Pāli learners
samādhi

concentration — from saṃ (together) + ā + dhā (to place); this chapter's entire subject, and the "mind" (citta) half of the treatise's threefold sīla-samādhi-paññā structure.

pañhākamma

a series of questions, a question-and-answer treatment — pañha (question) + kamma (work, act); the same expository method already used throughout Sīlaniddesa, now applied to concentration.

bhāvanā

development, cultivation — from bhāveti, "to cause to become, to develop"; the new question this chapter adds to the standard matrix, reflecting concentration's nature as something actively trained rather than merely maintained.

What Concentration Is, and In What Sense

The first three questions receive brief, technical answers before the chapter turns to its much longer treatment of classification.

Pali Source

Tatridaṃ vissajjanaṃ. Ko samādhīti samādhi bahuvidho nānappakārako. Taṃ sabbaṃ vibhāvayituṃ ārabbhamānaṃ vissajjanaṃ adhippetañceva atthaṃ na sādheyya, uttari ca vikkhepāya saṃvatteyya, tasmā idhādhippetameva sandhāya vadāma, kusalacittekaggatā samādhi.

Kenaṭṭhena samādhīti samādhānaṭṭhena samādhi. Kimidaṃ samādhānaṃ nāma? Ekārammaṇe cittacetasikānaṃ samaṃ sammā ca ādhānaṃ, ṭhapananti vuttaṃ hoti. Tasmā yassa dhammassānubhāvena ekārammaṇe cittacetasikā samaṃ sammā ca avikkhipamānā avippakiṇṇā ca hutvā tiṭṭhanti, idaṃ samādhānanti veditabbaṃ.

Kānassa lakkhaṇarasapaccupaṭṭhānapadaṭṭhānānīti ettha pana avikkhepalakkhaṇo samādhi, vikkhepaviddhaṃsanaraso, avikampanapaccupaṭṭhāno. "Sukhino cittaṃ samādhiyatī"ti vacanato pana sukhamassa padaṭṭhānaṃ.

Ru-Yi Translation

Herein, this is the answer. "What is concentration?": concentration is of many kinds, of various sorts. An answer that undertook to describe all of it would neither accomplish the purpose intended here, and would moreover lead to further distraction; therefore we speak here only with reference to what is intended: concentration is the one-pointedness of a wholesome mind.

"In what sense is it 'concentration'?": it is "concentration" (samādhi) in the sense of "composing" (samādhāna). What is this "composing"? It is said to be the even and proper placing, the settling, of mind and mental factors upon a single object. Therefore, that quality by whose power mind and mental factors remain upon a single object, evenly and properly, undistracted and unscattered, should be understood as "composing."

"What are its characteristic, function, manifestation, and proximate cause?": herein, concentration has non-distraction as its characteristic, the dispelling of distraction as its function, and non-wavering as its manifestation. And, because of the saying "the mind of one who is happy becomes concentrated," happiness is its proximate cause.

Reading note — Ru-Yi

The definition given here — "the one-pointedness of a wholesome mind" — is explicitly narrowed from the outset: the text openly acknowledges that "concentration" in the broadest sense covers unwholesome one-pointedness too (the fixed attention of, say, an angry or greedy mind), but declines to treat that broader sense at all, on the practical ground that doing so would not serve the purpose at hand and would only scatter the exposition. This is a small but clear instance of the commentarial method choosing precision of purpose over completeness of scope.

The proximate cause given — happiness (sukha) — connects concentration directly back to the causal chain the Buddhist path traditionally describes: virtue gives rise to freedom from remorse, which gives rise to gladness, joy, and tranquility, which gives rise to happiness, which in turn gives rise to concentration. Naming happiness as concentration's proximate cause here quietly presupposes that entire chain, without needing to restate it.

Key vocabulary — for Pāli learners
ekaggatā

one-pointedness — eka (one) + agga (point, apex) + tā (the abstract-noun suffix); the core mental factor that is being defined as "concentration" throughout this whole passage.

samādhāna

composing, settling — the abstract noun from which "samādhi" is here derived by the commentary's own etymological method, exactly paralleling how sīla was etymologized in chapter 1.

avikkhepa

non-distraction — a (not) + vikkhepa (distraction, scattering); given here as concentration's defining characteristic, the single quality every classification in this chapter will further subdivide.

padaṭṭhāna

proximate cause — the fourth term of the standard fourfold analytical frame (lakkhaṇa/rasa/paccupaṭṭhāna/padaṭṭhāna) already used throughout Sīlaniddesa and Dhutaṅganiddesa, now applied to concentration itself.

How Many Kinds: The Overview

Before working through each scheme in detail, the chapter previews all of them at once — one single kind by its bare characteristic, then a cascade of two-, three-, four-, and fivefold classifications, each keyed to a different distinguishing feature.

Pali Source

Katividho samādhīti avikkhepalakkhaṇena tāva ekavidho. Upacāraappanāvasena duvidho, tathā lokiyalokuttaravasena sappītikanippītikavasena sukhasahagataupekkhāsahagatavasena ca. Tividho hīnamajjhimapaṇītavasena, tathā savitakkasavicārādivasena pītisahagatādivasena parittamahaggatappamāṇavasena ca. Catubbidho dukkhāpaṭipadādandhābhiññādivasena, tathā parittaparittārammaṇādivasena catujhānaṅgavasena hānabhāgiyādivasena kāmāvacarādivasena adhipativasena ca. Pañcavidho pañcakanaye pañcajhānaṅgavasenāti.

Ru-Yi Translation

"Of how many kinds is it?": first, by way of its characteristic of non-distraction, it is of one kind. It is twofold by way of access and absorption; likewise by way of the mundane and the supramundane; by way of accompanied-by-joy and free-of-joy; and by way of accompanied-by-happiness and accompanied-by-equanimity. It is threefold by way of inferior, medium, and superior; likewise by way of accompanied-by-applied-and-sustained-thought and so on; by way of accompanied-by-joy and so on; and by way of limited, exalted, and immeasurable. It is fourfold by way of difficult-progress-with-sluggish-direct-knowledge and so on; likewise by way of limited-with-limited-object and so on; by way of the four jhāna factors; by way of conducive-to-decline and so on; by way of the sense sphere and so on; and by way of predominance. It is fivefold, in the fivefold method, by way of the five jhāna factors.

Reading note — Ru-Yi

This single dense paragraph functions exactly like the outline verse that opened the treatment of the ascetic practices in the previous chapter — a complete table of contents stated before any of it is explained, so that a reader always knows how many classification schemes remain and under which numerical heading each one belongs. Fourteen distinct classifications are named here in total (one onefold, four twofold, four threefold, six fourfold, one fivefold), and every one of them will be unpacked in turn under the three subheadings that follow.

It is worth noticing that these are not fourteen independent theories about what concentration is, but fourteen different lenses trained on the same single phenomenon defined a moment ago — "the one-pointedness of a wholesome mind." Whether sorted by depth of absorption, by accompanying feeling, by ease of attainment, or by which faculty was made predominant in developing it, every scheme is simply re-describing that same mental factor from a different angle.

Key vocabulary — for Pāli learners
jhāna

absorption, meditative attainment — from jhāyati, "to meditate, to burn [away opposing states]"; the deep states of concentration that several of the classification schemes in this chapter are built around.

upacāra

access — literally "approach, vicinity"; the threshold level of concentration that precedes full absorption, distinguished throughout this chapter from appanā, "absorption" proper.

lokiya / lokuttara

mundane / supramundane — loka (world) + iya (belonging to) vs. uttara (beyond); the distinction between ordinary concentration and the concentration inseparably bound up with the noble path itself.

catukkanaya / pañcakanaye

the fourfold method / in the fivefold method — two standard ways of counting the jhānas, the Sutta style (four) and the Abhidhamma style (five, splitting the first jhāna's transition into two stages), both used interchangeably throughout this chapter's classifications.

The Two-fold Divisions

Following its own preview, the chapter now works through each of the four ways concentration divides into two — by depth, by mundane or supramundane status, and by the two feelings, joy and happiness, that can or cannot accompany it.

Pali Source

Tattha ekavidhakoṭṭhāso uttānatthoyeva. Duvidhakoṭṭhāse channaṃ anussatiṭṭhānānaṃ, maraṇassatiyā, upasamānussatiyā, āhāre paṭikūlasaññāya, catudhātuvavatthānassāti imesaṃ vasena laddhacittekaggatā, yā ca appanāsamādhīnaṃ pubbabhāge ekaggatā, ayaṃ upacārasamādhi. "Paṭhamassa jhānassa parikammaṃ paṭhamassa jhānassa anantarapaccayena paccayo"ti ādivacanato pana yā parikammānantarā ekaggatā, ayaṃ appanāsamādhīti evaṃ upacārappanāvasena duvidho.

Dutiyaduke tīsu bhūmīsu kusalacittekaggatā lokiyo samādhi. Ariyamaggasampayuttā ekaggatā lokuttaro samādhīti evaṃ lokiyalokuttaravasena duvidho.

Tatiyaduke catukkanaye dvīsu pañcakanaye tīsu jhānesu ekaggatā sappītiko samādhi. Avasesesu dvīsu jhānesu ekaggatā nippītiko samādhi. Upacārasamādhi pana siyā sappītiko, siyā nippītikoti evaṃ sappītikanippītikavasena duvidho.

Catutthaduke catukkanaye tīsu pañcakanaye catūsu jhānesu ekaggatā sukhasahagato samādhi. Avasesasmiṃ upekkhāsahagato samādhi. Upacārasamādhi pana siyā sukhasahagato, siyā upekkhāsahagatoti evaṃ sukhasahagataupekkhāsahagatavasena duvidho.

Ru-Yi Translation

Herein, the onefold section is self-evident in meaning. In the twofold section: the one-pointedness of mind obtained by way of the six recollection-subjects, mindfulness of death, the recollection of peace, the perception of the repulsiveness of food, and the defining of the four elements — together with the one-pointedness that occurs in the preliminary stage of the absorption concentrations — this is access concentration. But, because of such sayings as "the preliminary work for the first jhāna is a condition for the first jhāna by the condition of immediate contiguity," the one-pointedness that immediately follows that preliminary work is absorption concentration. Thus it is twofold by way of access and absorption.

In the second pair: the one-pointedness of a wholesome mind on the three mundane planes is mundane concentration. The one-pointedness associated with the noble path is supramundane concentration. Thus it is twofold by way of the mundane and the supramundane.

In the third pair: the one-pointedness in the first two jhānas of the fourfold method, or the first three of the fivefold method, is concentration accompanied by joy. The one-pointedness in the remaining two jhānas is concentration free of joy. Access concentration, however, may be either accompanied by joy or free of joy. Thus it is twofold by way of accompanied-by-joy and free-of-joy.

In the fourth pair: the one-pointedness in the first three jhānas of the fourfold method, or the first four of the fivefold method, is concentration accompanied by happiness. In the remaining jhāna, it is concentration accompanied by equanimity. Access concentration, however, may be either accompanied by happiness or accompanied by equanimity. Thus it is twofold by way of accompanied-by-happiness and accompanied-by-equanimity.

Reading note — Ru-Yi

The list of meditation subjects capable of yielding only access concentration — six recollections, mindfulness of death, the recollection of peace, the perception of repulsiveness in food, and the defining of the four elements — is stated here as settled background knowledge, well before the chapter's own later treatment of all forty meditation subjects. This is characteristic of how the Visuddhimagga's chapters cross-reference each other: a detail belonging properly to a later chapter is used matter-of-factly here because the commentarial tradition already treats it as fixed, shared knowledge, not something this passage needs to argue for.

Notice, too, that access concentration is explicitly said to be able to fall on either side of three of these four pairs (joy or no joy, happiness or equanimity) — only the absorption jhānas are pinned down precisely to one member of each pair. This is a recurring pattern worth watching for through the rest of this chapter: access concentration is consistently treated as the flexible, not-yet-fixed threshold state, while full absorption is exact and classifiable down to the jhāna number.

Key vocabulary — for Pāli learners
parikamma

preliminary work, preparatory practice — the stage of effort immediately preceding absorption, whose "immediately following" moment of one-pointedness the text defines as appanā-samādhi itself.

bhūmi

a plane [of existence or consciousness] — here, "the three planes" (tisu bhūmīsu) refers to the sense-sphere, fine-material-sphere, and immaterial-sphere planes collectively, as opposed to the supramundane.

pīti

joy, rapture — a jhāna factor distinct from sukha (happiness); their separate presence or absence across the jhānas is what the third and fourth pairs in this section are built on.

upekkhā

equanimity — the feeling that replaces happiness in the fourth (or fifth, in the fivefold method) jhāna, marking the most refined level of absorption this section describes.

The Threefold Divisions

The four threefold schemes grade concentration by strength of development, by the presence of applied and sustained thought, by accompanying feeling in finer gradation than the twofold scheme allowed, and by the scope of its object.

Pali Source

Tikesu paṭhamattike paṭiladdhamatto hīno, nātisubhāvito majjhimo, subhāvito vasippatto paṇītoti evaṃ hīnamajjhimapaṇītavasena tividho.

Dutiyattike paṭhamajjhānasamādhi saddhiṃ upacārasamādhinā savitakkasavicāro. Pañcakanaye dutiyajjhānasamādhi avitakkavicāramatto. Yo hi vitakkamatteyeva ādīnavaṃ disvā vicāre adisvā kevalaṃ vitakkappahānamattaṃ ākaṅkhamāno paṭhamajjhānaṃ atikkamati, so avitakkavicāramattaṃ samādhiṃ paṭilabhati. Taṃ sandhāyetaṃ vuttaṃ. Catukkanaye pana dutiyādīsu pañcakanaye tatiyādīsu tīsu jhānesu ekaggatā avitakkāvicāro samādhīti evaṃ savitakkasavicārādivasena tividho.

Tatiyattike catukkanaye ādito dvīsu pañcakanaye ca tīsu jhānesu ekaggatā pītisahagato samādhi. Tesveva tatiye ca catutthe ca jhāne ekaggatā sukhasahagato samādhi. Avasāne upekkhāsahagato. Upacārasamādhi pana pītisukhasahagato vā hoti upekkhāsahagato vāti evaṃ pītisahagatādivasena tividho.

Catutthattike upacārabhūmiyaṃ ekaggatā paritto samādhi. Rūpāvacarārūpāvacarakusale ekaggatā mahaggato samādhi. Ariyamaggasampayuttā ekaggatā appamāṇo samādhīti evaṃ parittamahaggatappamāṇavasena tividho.

Ru-Yi Translation

Among the triads: in the first triad, concentration merely just obtained is inferior; not yet well developed is medium; well developed and having reached mastery is superior. Thus it is threefold by way of inferior, medium, and superior.

In the second triad: the concentration of the first jhāna, together with access concentration, is accompanied by both applied and sustained thought. In the fivefold method, the concentration of the second jhāna is accompanied by sustained thought only, without applied thought. For one who, having seen the danger in applied thought alone, without having seen it in sustained thought, and wishing only to abandon applied thought, transcends the first jhāna, attains a concentration that is merely without applied thought, with sustained thought still remaining; this was said with reference to that. But in the fourfold method, the one-pointedness in the second and following jhānas, or in the fivefold method the third and following jhānas, is concentration without either applied or sustained thought. Thus it is threefold by way of accompanied-by-applied-and-sustained-thought and so on.

In the third triad: the one-pointedness in the first two jhānas of the fourfold method, or the first three of the fivefold method, is concentration accompanied by joy. The one-pointedness in the third and fourth jhāna of that same scheme is concentration accompanied by happiness. In the final jhāna, it is accompanied by equanimity. Access concentration, however, is either accompanied by joy and happiness together, or accompanied by equanimity. Thus it is threefold by way of accompanied-by-joy and so on.

In the fourth triad: the one-pointedness on the plane of access is limited concentration. The one-pointedness in wholesome consciousness of the fine-material and immaterial spheres is exalted concentration. The one-pointedness associated with the noble path is immeasurable concentration. Thus it is threefold by way of limited, exalted, and immeasurable.

Reading note — Ru-Yi

The second triad's explanation of the fivefold method's extra jhāna is a small piece of careful psychological reasoning rather than a bare rule: the text explains that the "applied thought only, no sustained thought" stage exists specifically for a meditator whose attention happened to notice applied thought's coarseness before noticing sustained thought's, and who therefore lets go of the first factor without yet letting go of the second. The fivefold scheme is being presented not as an arbitrary alternative headcount but as a more fine-grained account of how the abandoning of jhāna factors can actually unfold in practice.

The fourth triad's "limited / exalted / immeasurable" grading maps concentration directly onto the traditional three-tiered cosmology this literature assumes throughout — access concentration belongs to the sense-sphere plane, jhāna concentration to the fine-material and immaterial planes, and the concentration bound up with the noble path is set apart from that scale altogether as immeasurable, exactly mirroring how the earlier mundane/supramundane pair worked in the previous section.

Key vocabulary — for Pāli learners
vitakka / vicāra

applied thought / sustained thought — the two jhāna factors whose presence, partial presence, or absence is what distinguishes the first several jhānas from one another across both the fourfold and fivefold counting methods.

ādīnava

danger, drawback — the perception of a jhāna factor's coarseness that motivates a meditator to abandon it and progress to the next, refined jhāna.

vasippatta

having attained mastery — vasī (mastery, control) + patta (attained); the mark of "superior" concentration in the first triad, distinguishing it from concentration merely obtained or not yet well developed.

paritta / mahaggata / appamāṇa

limited / exalted / immeasurable — the three terms of the fourth triad, mapping directly onto the sense-sphere, fine-material-and-immaterial-sphere, and supramundane planes respectively.

The Fourfold and Fivefold Divisions

The chapter's longest classification section works through six different fourfold schemes — by ease and speed of attainment, by scope of concentration and object, by the four jhāna factors themselves, by conduciveness to decline or distinction, by plane, and by which faculty was made predominant in developing it — before closing with the fivefold scheme's own refinement of the second jhāna.

Pali Source

Catukkesu paṭhamacatukke atthi samādhi dukkhāpaṭipado dandhābhiñño, atthi dukkhāpaṭipado khippābhiñño, atthi sukhāpaṭipado dandhābhiñño, atthi sukhāpaṭipado khippābhiññoti.

Tattha paṭhamasamannāhārato paṭṭhāya yāva tassa tassa jhānassa upacāraṃ uppajjati, tāva pavattā samādhibhāvanā paṭipadāti vuccati. Upacārato pana paṭṭhāya yāva appanā, tāva pavattā paññā abhiññāti vuccati. Sā panesā paṭipadā ekaccassa dukkhā hoti, nīvaraṇādipaccanīkadhammasamudācāragahaṇatāya kicchā asukhāsevanāti attho. Ekaccassa tadabhāvena sukhā. Abhiññāpi ekaccassa dandhā hoti mandā asīghappavatti. Ekaccassa khippā amandā sīghappavatti.

Tattha yāni parato sappāyāsappāyāni ca palibodhupacchedādīni pubbakiccāni ca appanākosallāni ca vaṇṇayissāma, tesu yo asappāyasevī hoti, tassa dukkhā paṭipadā dandhā ca abhiññā hoti. Sappāyasevino sukhā paṭipadā khippā ca abhiññā. Yo pana pubbabhāge asappāyaṃ sevitvā aparabhāge sappāyasevī hoti, pubbabhāge vā sappāyaṃ sevitvā aparabhāge asappāyasevī, tassa vomissakatā veditabbā. Tathā palibodhupacchedādikaṃ pubbakiccaṃ asampādetvā bhāvanamanuyuttassa dukkhā paṭipadā hoti. Vipariyāyena sukhā. Appanākosallāni pana asampādentassa dandhā abhiññā hoti. Sampādentassa khippā.

Apica taṇhāavijjāvasena samathavipassanādhikāravasena cāpi etāsaṃ pabhedo veditabbo. Taṇhābhibhūtassa hi dukkhā paṭipadā hoti. Anabhibhūtassa sukhā. Avijjābhibhūtassa ca dandhā abhiññā hoti. Anabhibhūtassa khippā. Yo ca samathe akatādhikāro, tassa dukkhā paṭipadā hoti. Katādhikārassa sukhā. Yo pana vipassanāya akatādhikāro hoti, tassa dandhā abhiññā hoti, katādhikārassa khippā. Kilesindriyavasena cāpi etāsaṃ pabhedo veditabbo. Tibbakilesassa hi mudindriyassa dukkhā paṭipadā hoti dandhā ca abhiññā, tikkhindriyassa pana khippā abhiññā. Mandakilesassa ca mudindriyassa sukhā paṭipadā hoti dandhā ca abhiññā. Tikkhindriyassa pana khippā abhiññāti.

Iti imāsu paṭipadāabhiññāsu yo puggalo dukkhāya paṭipadāya dandhāya ca abhiññāya samādhiṃ pāpuṇāti, tassa so samādhi dukkhāpaṭipado dandhābhiññoti vuccati. Esa nayo sesattayepīti evaṃ dukkhāpaṭipadādandhābhiññādivasena catubbidho.

Dutiyacatukke atthi samādhi paritto parittārammaṇo, atthi paritto appamāṇārammaṇo, atthi appamāṇo parittārammaṇo, atthi appamāṇo appamāṇārammaṇoti. Tattha yo samādhi appaguṇo uparijhānassa paccayo bhavituṃ na sakkoti, ayaṃ paritto. Yo pana avaḍḍhite ārammaṇe pavatto, ayaṃ parittārammaṇo. Yo paguṇo subhāvito, uparijhānassa paccayo bhavituṃ sakkoti, ayaṃ appamāṇo. Yo ca vaḍḍhite ārammaṇe pavatto, ayaṃ appamāṇārammaṇo. Vuttalakkhaṇavomissatāya pana vomissakanayo veditabbo. Evaṃ parittaparittārammaṇādivasena catubbidho.

Tatiyacatukke vikkhambhitanīvaraṇānaṃ vitakkavicārapītisukhasamādhīnaṃ vasena pañcaṅgikaṃ paṭhamaṃ jhānaṃ, tato vūpasantavitakkavicāraṃ tivaṅgikaṃ dutiyaṃ, tato virattapītikaṃ duvaṅgikaṃ tatiyaṃ, tato pahīnasukhaṃ upekkhāvedanāsahitassa samādhino vasena duvaṅgikaṃ catutthaṃ. Iti imesaṃ catunnaṃ jhānānaṃ aṅgabhūtā cattāro samādhī honti. Evaṃ catujhānaṅgavasena catubbidho.

Catutthacatukke atthi samādhi hānabhāgiyo, atthi ṭhitibhāgiyo, atthi visesabhāgiyo, atthi nibbedhabhāgiyo. Tattha paccanīkasamudācāravasena hānabhāgiyatā, tadanudhammatāya satiyā saṇṭhānavasena ṭhitibhāgiyatā, uparivisesādhigamavasena visesabhāgiyatā, nibbidāsahagatasaññāmanasikārasamudācāravasena nibbedhabhāgiyatā ca veditabbā. Yathāha, "paṭhamassa jhānassa lābhiṃ kāmasahagatā saññāmanasikārā samudācaranti hānabhāginī paññā. Tadanudhammatā sati santiṭṭhati ṭhitibhāginī paññā. Avitakkasahagatā saññāmanasikārā samudācaranti visesabhāginī paññā. Nibbidāsahagatā saññāmanasikārā samudācaranti virāgūpasañhitā nibbedhabhāginī paññā"ti (vibha. 799). Tāya pana paññāya sampayuttā samādhīpi cattāro hontīti. Evaṃ hānabhāgiyādivasena catubbidho.

Pañcamacatukke kāmāvacaro samādhi, rūpāvacaro samādhi, arūpāvacaro samādhi, apariyāpanno samādhīti evaṃ cattāro samādhī. Tattha sabbāpi upacārekaggatā kāmāvacaro samādhi. Tathā rūpāvacarādikusalacittekaggatā itare tayoti evaṃ kāmāvacarādivasena catubbidho.

Chaṭṭhacatukke "chandaṃ ce bhikkhu adhipatiṃ karitvā labhati samādhiṃ, labhati cittassekaggataṃ, ayaṃ vuccati chandasamādhi … pe … vīriyaṃ ce bhikkhu … pe … cittaṃ ce bhikkhu … pe … vīmaṃsaṃ ce bhikkhu adhipatiṃ karitvā labhati samādhiṃ, labhati cittassekaggataṃ, ayaṃ vuccati vīmaṃsāsamādhī"ti (vibha. 432; saṃ. ni. 3.825) evaṃ adhipativasena catubbidho.

Pañcake yaṃ catukkabhede vuttaṃ dutiyaṃ jhānaṃ, taṃ vitakkamattātikkamena dutiyaṃ, vitakkavicārātikkamena tatiyanti evaṃ dvidhā bhinditvā pañca jhānāni veditabbāni. Tesaṃ aṅgabhūtā ca pañca samādhīti evaṃ pañcajhānaṅgavasena pañcavidhatā veditabbā.

Ru-Yi Translation

Among the tetrads: in the first tetrad, there is concentration with difficult progress and sluggish direct knowledge; there is concentration with difficult progress and quick direct knowledge; there is concentration with easy progress and sluggish direct knowledge; there is concentration with easy progress and quick direct knowledge.

Herein, the development of concentration that proceeds from the first attention to the meditation subject up until the access of that particular jhāna arises is called "progress." But the understanding that proceeds from access up until absorption is called "direct knowledge." Now, this progress is, for some, difficult — meaning it is troublesome and unpleasant to engage in, owing to the occurrence and taking hold of opposing states such as the hindrances. For others, owing to the absence of these, it is easy. Direct knowledge, too, is for some sluggish — weak, and not swift in its occurrence. For others, it is quick — strong and swift in its occurrence.

Herein, among the suitable and unsuitable things, the preliminary duties such as the cutting off of impediments, and the skills in absorption that we shall describe further on — whoever resorts to what is unsuitable has difficult progress and sluggish direct knowledge. For one who resorts to what is suitable, progress is easy and direct knowledge is quick. But for one who resorts to what is unsuitable in the earlier stage and to what is suitable in the later stage, or the reverse, a mixed condition should be understood. Likewise, for one who devotes himself to development without first accomplishing the preliminary duties such as the cutting off of impediments, progress is difficult; in the reverse case, it is easy. For one who does not accomplish the skills in absorption, direct knowledge is sluggish; for one who does accomplish them, it is quick.

Moreover, the distinction among these should also be understood by way of craving and ignorance, and by way of prior effort in serenity and insight. For one overpowered by craving, progress is difficult; for one not overpowered, it is easy. For one overpowered by ignorance, direct knowledge is sluggish; for one not overpowered, it is quick. For one who has made no prior effort in serenity, progress is difficult; for one who has made such effort, it is easy. For one who has made no prior effort in insight, direct knowledge is sluggish; for one who has made such effort, it is quick. The distinction among these should also be understood by way of defilements and faculties. For one of strong defilements and weak faculties, progress is difficult and direct knowledge is sluggish; but for one of sharp faculties, direct knowledge is quick even so. For one of weak defilements and weak faculties, progress is easy but direct knowledge is sluggish; for one of sharp faculties, however, direct knowledge is quick.

Thus, among these combinations of progress and direct knowledge, whatever person attains concentration by difficult progress and sluggish direct knowledge — for him, that concentration is called "of difficult progress and sluggish direct knowledge." The same method applies to the remaining three combinations as well. Thus it is fourfold by way of difficult-progress-with-sluggish-direct-knowledge and so on.

In the second tetrad: there is concentration that is limited with a limited object; there is limited with an immeasurable object; there is immeasurable with a limited object; there is immeasurable with an immeasurable object. Herein, whatever concentration is not yet skillfully mastered, and is not able to become a condition for a higher jhāna, this is "limited." But whatever occurs with an object that has not been expanded, this has a "limited object." Whatever is mastered, well developed, and able to become a condition for a higher jhāna, this is "immeasurable." And whatever occurs with an object that has been expanded, this has an "immeasurable object." By the mixing of the characteristics just stated, the mixed cases should be understood. Thus it is fourfold by way of limited-with-limited-object and so on.

In the third tetrad: with the hindrances suppressed, the first jhāna is fivefold in its factors — by way of applied thought, sustained thought, joy, happiness, and concentration. From that, with applied and sustained thought calmed, the second is threefold in its factors. From that, with joy having faded away, the third is twofold in its factors. From that, with happiness abandoned, the fourth is twofold in its factors, by way of the concentration accompanied by the feeling of equanimity. Thus these become the four concentrations that are the factors of these four jhānas. Thus it is fourfold by way of the four jhāna factors.

In the fourth tetrad: there is concentration conducive to decline; there is concentration conducive to stability; there is concentration conducive to distinction; there is concentration conducive to penetration. Herein, conduciveness to decline should be understood by way of the occurrence of opposing states; conduciveness to stability, by way of the persistence of mindfulness in conformity with that attainment; conduciveness to distinction, by way of the attainment of a higher distinction; and conduciveness to penetration, by way of the occurrence of perception and attention accompanied by disenchantment. As it is said: "In one who has obtained the first jhāna, perceptions and attention accompanied by sensual desire occur — this is understanding conducive to decline. Mindfulness in conformity with that attainment persists — this is understanding conducive to stability. Perceptions and attention accompanied by freedom from applied thought occur — this is understanding conducive to distinction. Perceptions and attention accompanied by disenchantment, connected with dispassion, occur — this is understanding conducive to penetration" (Vibhaṅga 799). And the concentrations associated with that understanding are likewise four. Thus it is fourfold by way of conducive-to-decline and so on.

In the fifth tetrad: sense-sphere concentration, fine-material-sphere concentration, immaterial-sphere concentration, and unincluded concentration — these are the four kinds of concentration. Herein, all one-pointedness of access is sense-sphere concentration. Likewise, the one-pointedness of wholesome consciousness of the fine-material sphere and the rest are the other three. Thus it is fourfold by way of the sense sphere and so on.

In the sixth tetrad: "if a monk gains concentration, gains one-pointedness of mind, by making zeal predominant, this is called concentration due to zeal ... if a monk [gains it] by making energy [predominant] ... by making mind [predominant] ... if a monk gains concentration, gains one-pointedness of mind, by making investigation predominant, this is called concentration due to investigation" (Vibhaṅga 432; S.N. 3.825). Thus it is fourfold by way of predominance.

In the fivefold scheme: the second jhāna spoken of in the fourfold division should be split in two — as a second jhāna by the transcending of applied thought alone, and as a third by the transcending of both applied and sustained thought — and the five jhānas should be understood in this way. And the five concentrations that are the factors of these should likewise be understood as fivefold by way of the five jhāna factors.

Reading note — Ru-Yi

The first tetrad's four-way cross of "progress" and "direct knowledge" is then re-derived from three separate angles in succession — suitability of conditions and preliminary duties, the presence of craving and ignorance, and the strength of one's defilements against one's faculties — without the text ever claiming these three explanations compete with one another. This is a recognizable feature of the commentarial method already seen throughout Sīlaniddesa and Dhutaṅganiddesa: multiple valid causal accounts of the same phenomenon are simply laid side by side, each illuminating the same distinction from a different angle, with no need to rank or choose among them.

The sixth tetrad's quotation on zeal, energy, mind, and investigation as four possible "predominant" routes into concentration is a direct citation of the four bases of spiritual success (iddhipāda) recast specifically in terms of concentration — the same four faculties that, in their more familiar canonical role, are said to be the direct cause of a Buddha's or arahant's ability to extend the lifespan. Here they are treated far more modestly, simply as four different psychological levers by which an ordinary meditator's one-pointedness of mind can first be established.

Key vocabulary — for Pāli learners
paṭipadā / abhiññā

progress / direct knowledge — two technical terms given special, narrow definitions in this section alone: the effort leading up to access, and the understanding from access up to absorption, respectively — not abhiññā's more familiar sense of "superknowledge."

sappāya / asappāya

suitable / unsuitable — describing conditions (food, climate, company, dwelling, and the like) that either favor or hinder a given meditator's practice, a theme the text says it will treat at length later in the chapter.

indriya

a faculty — here, "sharp" (tikkha) versus "weak" (muda) faculties, one of several lenses (alongside defilement-strength, craving, and ignorance) used to explain why concentration comes easily to some and with difficulty to others.

hānabhāgiya / visesabhāgiya

conducive to decline / conducive to distinction — the pair of terms the chapter's later "defilement and cleansing" question is answered directly in terms of, drawn from the fourth tetrad developed here.

adhipati

predominance, a dominant condition — the technical basis for the sixth tetrad, naming whichever of zeal, energy, mind, or investigation was given the leading role in a given occasion of attaining concentration.

Defilement and Cleansing

Rather than developing new terminology, the chapter answers its fifth and sixth questions by simply pointing back to the fourth tetrad just completed, drawing directly on the Vibhaṅga's own definitions.

Pali Source

Ko cassa saṃkileso, kiṃ vodānanti ettha pana vissajjanaṃ vibhaṅge vuttameva. Vuttañhi tattha "saṃkilesanti hānabhāgiyo dhammo. Vodānanti visesabhāgiyo dhammo"ti (vibha. 828). Tattha "paṭhamassa jhānassa lābhiṃ kāmasahagatā saññāmanasikārā samudācaranti hānabhāginī paññā"ti (vibha. 799) iminā nayena hānabhāgiyadhammo veditabbo. "Avitakkasahagatā saññāmanasikārā samudācaranti visesabhāginī paññā"ti (vibha. 799) iminā nayena visesabhāgiyadhammo veditabbo.

Ru-Yi Translation

"What is its defilement, and what is its cleansing?": herein, the answer is simply what is stated in the Vibhaṅga. For it is said there: "Defilement is the state conducive to decline. Cleansing is the state conducive to distinction" (Vibhaṅga 828). Herein, the state conducive to decline should be understood by this same passage: "in one who has obtained the first jhāna, perceptions and attention accompanied by sensual desire occur — this is understanding conducive to decline" (Vibhaṅga 799). And the state conducive to distinction should be understood by this passage: "perceptions and attention accompanied by freedom from applied thought occur — this is understanding conducive to distinction" (Vibhaṅga 799).

Reading note — Ru-Yi

The brevity here is itself informative: rather than developing a fresh account of defilement and cleansing specific to concentration, the text simply re-uses the fourth tetrad's own hānabhāgiya/visesabhāgiya pair, already fully explained a few paragraphs earlier, and backs the identification with a direct Vibhaṅga citation. This is the same economy of exposition seen throughout this literature — once a distinction has been properly established, later questions are answered by pointing back to it rather than restating it in new language.

It is also worth noting what falls outside this pairing: the fourth tetrad named four grades (decline, stability, distinction, penetration), but this question only draws on two of them, decline and distinction, leaving stability and penetration unused here. Nothing is made of that omission — it simply reflects that "defilement" and "cleansing" are a natural pair, while "stability" (staying where one already is) and "penetration" (a further, different kind of breakthrough) belong to a different concern than these two questions are asking about.

Key vocabulary — for Pāli learners
saṃkilesa

defilement, corruption — saṃ (thoroughly) + kilesa (defilement); the fifth question in this chapter's opening matrix, answered here by direct identification with "the state conducive to decline."

vodāna

cleansing, purification — the sixth question's answer, identified with "the state conducive to distinction"; the same term used throughout Sīlaniddesa for the cleansing of virtue.

Vibhaṅga

the "Analysis," the second book of the Abhidhamma Piṭaka — cited twice by section number in this short passage alone, exemplifying how directly the Visuddhimagga draws on canonical Abhidhamma material for its technical definitions.

How Concentration Should Be Developed: Overview

The chapter's seventh question — by far its largest — opens by disposing of supramundane concentration in a single sentence, since its development is inseparable from the development of understanding itself, before turning to the far longer treatment mundane concentration requires.

Pali Source

Kathaṃ bhāvetabboti ettha pana yo tāva ayaṃ lokiyalokuttaravasena duvidhotiādīsu ariyamaggasampayutto samādhi vutto, tassa bhāvanānayo paññābhāvanānayeneva saṅgahito. Paññāya hi bhāvitāya so bhāvito hoti. Tasmā taṃ sandhāya evaṃ bhāvetabboti na kiñci visuṃ vadāma.

Yo panāyaṃ lokiyo, so vuttanayena sīlāni visodhetvā suparisuddhe sīle patiṭṭhitena yvāssa dasasu palibodhesu palibodho atthi, taṃ upacchinditvā kammaṭṭhānadāyakaṃ kalyāṇamittaṃ upasaṅkamitvā attano cariyānukūlaṃ cattālīsāya kammaṭṭhānesu aññataraṃ kammaṭṭhānaṃ gahetvā samādhibhāvanāya ananurūpaṃ vihāraṃ pahāya anurūpe vihāre viharantena khuddakapalibodhupacchedaṃ katvā sabbaṃ bhāvanāvidhānaṃ aparihāpentena bhāvetabboti ayamettha saṅkhepo.

Ru-Yi Translation

"How should it be developed?": herein, as for that concentration already spoken of as associated with the noble path, in the passage beginning "twofold by way of the mundane and the supramundane" — its method of development is included simply within the method of developing understanding itself. For when understanding is developed, that concentration is thereby developed too. Therefore, with reference to it, we say nothing separately as to how it should be developed.

But as for mundane concentration — it should be developed, in brief, by one who, having purified his virtue in the way already described, and being established in virtue thus thoroughly purified, has cut off whichever of the ten impediments applies to him; who has approached a good friend able to give a meditation subject; who has taken up, in accordance with his own temperament, one of the forty meditation subjects; who, having abandoned a dwelling unsuited to the development of concentration, dwells in a suitable one; who has cut off minor impediments; and who does not let the whole procedure of development lapse. This, herein, is the summary.

Reading note — Ru-Yi

The single-sentence dismissal of supramundane concentration's development is a striking structural choice: rather than treating it as a separate, parallel topic, the text simply folds it entirely into the future treatment of understanding (paññā), the treatise's still-unopened third part. This quietly confirms something implicit throughout this chapter so far — that mundane concentration (built through the forty meditation subjects, serenity practice, and the jhānas) and supramundane concentration (inseparable from insight and the noble path) are different enough in how they are developed that only one of them belongs to this chapter at all.

The brief summary here previews the entire remaining shape of the chapter: cutting off the ten impediments, finding a teacher, choosing a meditation subject suited to temperament, and finding a suitable dwelling — each of these becomes its own extended treatment in turn, starting with the impediments immediately below.

Key vocabulary — for Pāli learners
palibodha

an impediment, an obstruction — pari (around) + rudh (to obstruct); the subject of the entire section that follows, naming anything that ties a meditator's mind down before he can even begin.

kalyāṇamitta

a good friend, a spiritual friend — kalyāṇa (good, beautiful) + mitta (friend); here specifically the teacher able to give a meditation subject, treated in its own section later in this chapter.

cariyā

temperament, conduct, character — the basis for matching a meditator to one of the forty meditation subjects, elaborated at length in this chapter's later section on the six types of temperament.

The Ten Impediments

Before explaining each impediment individually, the chapter names all ten at once, in a single verse — and immediately clarifies that none of them is an impediment for everyone: what matters is not the thing itself but whether the mind has become attached to it.

Pali Source

Ayaṃ pana vitthāro, yaṃ tāva vuttaṃ "yvāssa dasasu palibodhesu palibodho atthi, taṃ upacchinditvā"ti, ettha —

Āvāso ca kulaṃ lābho, gaṇo kammañca pañcamaṃ;
Addhānaṃ ñāti ābādho, gantho iddhīti te dasāti. —

Ime dasa palibodhā nāma. Tattha āvāsoyeva āvāsapalibodho. Esa nayo kulādīsu.

Tattha āvāsoti ekopi ovarako vuccati ekampi pariveṇaṃ sakalopi saṅghārāmo. Svāyaṃ na sabbasseva palibodho hoti. Yo panettha navakammādīsu ussukkaṃ vā āpajjati, bahubhaṇḍasannicayo vā hoti, yena kenaci vā kāraṇena apekkhavā paṭibaddhacitto, tasseva palibodho hoti, na itarassa.

Ru-Yi Translation

But this is the detailed treatment. As for what was just said, "having cut off whichever of the ten impediments applies to him" — herein —

A dwelling, a family, gain, a following, and work as the fifth; a journey, relatives, sickness, books, and psychic power — these are the ten.

These are called the ten impediments. Herein, the dwelling itself is the impediment of dwelling. The same method applies to family and the rest.

Herein, "a dwelling" means even a single cell, even a single lodging-plot, or even an entire monastery. This is not an impediment for everyone. But for whoever, in connection with it, becomes engrossed in construction work and the like, or has accumulated many belongings there, or for whatever reason has an expectant, attached mind toward it — for him alone it is an impediment, not for another.

Reading note — Ru-Yi

The single opening line — "this is not an impediment for everyone" — governs the whole list that follows, and is worth holding onto through every story this section tells: the text is never arguing that dwellings, families, gain, or the rest are inherently dangerous. It is arguing that attachment to any of them is dangerous, and that the same object can be a complete non-issue for one person and a total obstruction for another, depending entirely on the state of that person's mind toward it.

Key vocabulary — for Pāli learners
āvāsa

a dwelling, residence — the first of the ten impediments, ranging in scope from a single cell to an entire monastery.

navakamma

new work, construction — nava (new) + kamma (work); named here as one of the specific activities that can turn a dwelling into a genuine impediment.

paṭibaddhacitta

a mind that is bound, attached — paṭibaddha (bound, tied) + citta (mind); the precise condition, rather than the object itself, that this whole section identifies as the real impediment.

A Story: The Dwelling That Was Not an Impediment

The chapter illustrates the impediment of dwelling with a story of two monks of equal ordination-standing — one who has spent years in a remote forest retreat, and one who has remained at the well-appointed Thūpārāma — and lets a single small exchange between them reveal which of the two has actually let go of attachment.

Pali Source

Tatridaṃ vatthu — dve kira kulaputtā anurādhapurā nikkhamitvā anupubbena thūpārāme pabbajiṃsu. Tesu eko dve mātikā paguṇā katvā pañcavassiko hutvā pavāretvā pācinakhaṇḍarājiṃ nāma gato. Eko tattheva vasati. Pācinakhaṇḍarājigato tattha ciraṃ vasitvā thero hutvā cintesi "paṭisallānasāruppamidaṃ ṭhānaṃ, handa naṃ sahāyakassāpi ārocemī"ti. Tato nikkhamitvā anupubbena thūpārāmaṃ pāvisi. Ru-Yi Translation

Here is the story. It is said that two young men of good family, having left Anurādhapura, went forth in due course at Thūpārāma. Of these, one, having mastered the two recitation-summaries, and having become a monk of five years' standing and completed the rains-invitation, went to a place called Pācīnakhaṇḍarājī. The other continued to dwell right there. The one who had gone to Pācīnakhaṇḍarājī, having dwelt there long and become an elder, thought: "This is a place well suited to solitary meditation — come, let me tell my companion about it too." So, setting out from there, he in due course entered Thūpārāma. And the elder of equal standing, seeing him just as he was entering, went out to meet him, took his bowl and robe, and performed the customary duties. The visiting elder, having entered his lodging, thought: "Now my companion will send me ghee, or molasses, or a drink — for he has long resided in this city." Not receiving anything that night, in the morning he thought: "Now he will send rice-gruel and refreshments obtained from his supporters." Not seeing that either, he thought, "there is no one to send it — he must mean to give it once I've gone in for alms," and so, early in the morning, he went with him into the village. The two of them, having walked along a single street, obtained only a ladleful of rice-gruel, and sat down in the resting-hall and drank it. Then the visitor thought: "there must be no regular rice-gruel here — but at meal-time, people will now give fine food." But then, at meal-time too, they walked for alms and ate only what was obtained; and the other said: "Venerable sir, do you always get by like this?"

"Yes, friend," he replied. "Venerable sir, Pācīnakhaṇḍarājī is comfortable — let us go there." The elder, leaving the city by the southern gate, took the road to the potters' village. The other said: "But, venerable sir, why have you taken this road?" "Was it not you, friend, who spoke in praise of Pācīnakhaṇḍarājī?" "But, venerable sir, is there nothing extra belonging to you at the place you've lived so long?" "Indeed, friend — the bed and chair belong to the Sangha, and they have already been put away; there is nothing else of mine." "But mine, venerable sir — my walking-staff, my oil-tube, and my sandal-bag — are still right there." "Have you, friend, after staying just one day, already set aside this much?" "Yes, venerable sir." He, with a mind full of confidence, having paid respects to the elder, said: "For one such as you, venerable sir, dwelling in the forest is proper anywhere at all. But Thūpārāma is the resting-place of the relics of four Buddhas; at the Bronze Palace, favorable Dhamma-teaching, the sight of the Great Shrine, and the sight of the elders can all be had — it goes on as if it were still the Buddha's own time. Do dwell right here." And on the next day, taking his own bowl and robe, he himself departed. For one such as this, a dwelling is not an impediment.

Reading note — Ru-Yi

The story's turn hinges on a single small confession: after years in a remote forest dwelling, the visiting elder owns nothing beyond communal furniture already returned to the Sangha, while his friend, after merely a single day's preparation for the journey, has already set aside a walking-staff, an oil-tube, and a sandal-bag. The point is not that these three small items are themselves faults — a traveling monk plainly needs such things — but that the ease and speed with which they accumulate, even in one day, shows how readily attachment can take hold, in sharp contrast to years of apparently austere forest life that left no residue of ownership at all.

The story's resolution inverts the expected direction of travel: rather than the settled monk following his friend out to the celebrated forest retreat, it is the friend who insists the elder remain at Thūpārāma — a famously well-resourced, relic-rich site — precisely because the elder has already demonstrated, through owning nothing, that no dwelling could ever become an impediment for him. The story's closing line makes the point explicit: it was never about which place was better, only about which mind was already free.

A Story: The Family That Was Not an Impediment

The second impediment gets its own extended story — of a young monk who spends an entire rains-retreat, unrecognized, in the very house of his own parents, and whose mother's reaction on learning the truth becomes an occasion for canonical teaching in its own right.

Pali Source

Kulanti ñātikulaṃ vā upaṭṭhākakulaṃ vā. Ekaccassa hi upaṭṭhākakulampi "sukhitesu sukhito"tiādinā (vibha. 888; saṃ. ni. 4.241) nayena saṃsaṭṭhassa viharato palibodho hoti, so kulamānusakehi vinā dhammassavanāya sāmantavihārampi na gacchati. Ekaccassa mātāpitaropi palibodhā na honti, koraṇḍakavihāravāsittherassa bhāgineyyadaharabhikkhuno viya.

Theropi vihāraṃ āgantvā dutiyadivase koraṇḍakagāmaṃ pāvisi.

Sā taṃ ekakameva āgacchantaṃ disvā "mato me maññe putto, ayaṃ thero ekakova āgacchatī"ti therassa pādamūle nipatitvā paridevamānā rodi. Thero "nūna daharo appicchatāya attānaṃ ajānāpetvāva gato"ti taṃ samassāsetvā sabbaṃ pavattiṃ ārocetvā pattatthavikato taṃ sāṭakaṃ nīharitvā dasseti.

Ru-Yi Translation

"A family" means either a family of relatives or a family of supporters. For some, even a family of supporters, if he dwells closely attached to them in the manner described as "happy when they are happy" and so on (Vibhaṅga 888; S.N. 4.241), becomes an impediment — such a one will not even go to a nearby monastery to hear the Dhamma without the family's own people. For some, even his own parents are not impediments — as in the case of the young nephew-monk of the elder who dwelt at Koraṇḍaka Monastery.

It is said that he went to Rohaṇa for the purpose of scriptural recitation-study. And the elder's own sister, a laywoman, would always ask the elder for news of him. One day the elder, thinking "I shall bring the young one back," set out facing toward Rohaṇa. The young monk too, thinking "I have dwelt here long; now, having seen my preceptor and learned news of the laywoman, I shall come back," set out from Rohaṇa. The two of them met on the bank of the river. He, having performed the customary duty for the elder at the foot of a certain tree, when asked "where are you going?", told him the reason. The elder said: "Well done — the laywoman, too, always asks after you; I myself have come for this very purpose. You go on; I, however, shall spend this rains right here" — and so sent him on his way. He arrived at that monastery on the very day appointed for entering the rains-retreat. And the lodging he received was, as it happened, the very one built by his own father.

Then his father, coming the next day and asking, "Venerable sir, to whom has our lodging fallen?", and hearing "to a visiting young monk," approached him, paid respects, and said: "Venerable sir, there is a customary duty for one who has entered the rains in our lodging." "What is it, layman?" "For the three months, you should take almsfood at our house alone, and, having completed the rains, ask leave of us at the time of your departure." He consented by remaining silent. The layman, having gone home, said: "A visiting monk has come to our dwelling; he should be attended to with care." The laywoman, agreeing, "Very well," prepared fine food, both hard and soft. The young monk, too, at meal-time, went to the house of his relatives. No one recognized him.

He, having taken almsfood there for the full three months, and having completed the rains, asked leave, saying, "I am going." His relatives said, "Tomorrow, venerable sir, you may go" — and the next day, having fed him right there at the house, and having filled an oil-tube, given a lump of molasses and a nine-cubit cloth, said: "Go now, venerable sir." He, having given the blessing, set out facing toward Rohaṇa.

His preceptor, too, having completed the rains and coming back along the same road, saw him at that very place they had met before. He performed the customary duty for the elder at the foot of a certain tree. Then the elder asked him: "Well, good sir, did you see the laywoman?" "Yes, venerable sir," he said, and, having told him the whole matter, anointed the elder's feet with that oil, made a drink with the molasses, and gave that cloth, too, to the elder himself; then, having paid respects to the elder, said, "Rohaṇa alone is suitable for me, venerable sir," and went on. The elder, too, having come to the monastery, on the next day entered Koraṇḍaka village.

And the laywoman, thinking "my brother, having brought my son, is now coming," stood always watching the road. Seeing him coming alone, she thought, "my son must be dead — this elder comes alone," and, falling at the elder's feet, wept, lamenting. The elder, thinking "surely, out of wanting little, the young one went without making himself known," comforted her, told her the whole matter, and, taking that cloth out of his bowl-bag, showed it to her.

The laywoman, filled with faith, lying face down toward the direction her son had gone, paying homage, said: "Surely it must have been with a monk just like my son as living witness that the Blessed One taught the practice of the relay of chariots (M.N. 1.252 and following), the practice taught to Nālaka (Sn. 684 and following), the practice taught to Tuvaṭaka (Sn. 921 and following), and the practice of the great noble lineage illustrating contentment and delight in development regarding the four requisites (A.N. 4.28; D.N. 3.309). Even eating for three months in the very house of the mother who bore him, he would not say, 'I am your son, you are my mother' — oh, what an astonishing person!" For one such as this, not even his own parents are impediments — how much less another family of supporters.

Reading note — Ru-Yi

The story's structure depends entirely on withheld recognition: neither the father, the mother, nor any of the relatives know the visiting monk is their own son and nephew, and he never once corrects them. The narrative's quiet power comes from how ordinary the whole three months appear from the family's side — a routine act of hospitality to an anonymous guest — while only the reader (and, in the end, the mother) learns what it actually was. Even the small detail that his assigned lodging happened to be the one his own father had built is left unremarked upon within the story itself; it is simply the coincidence that sets the entire episode in motion.

The mother's response, once she learns the truth, is worth noting for how it reasons: rather than expressing regret at not having recognized her son, she reinterprets the entire experience as confirmation of specific canonical teachings — the relay-of-chariots simile, the instructions given to Nālaka and to Tuvaṭaka, and the teaching on the great noble lineage of contentment with the four requisites — treating her own son's conduct as living proof that these teachings describe something real and attainable, not merely words. This is exactly the kind of internal, text-citing reasoning this commentarial genre relies on: praise is grounded in scripture the text itself names, not in the narrator's own independent judgment.

The Remaining Impediments: Gain, a Following, Work, and a Journey

The chapter now moves faster through the remaining eight impediments, giving each a brief practical explanation of how it arises and exactly how it should be cut off — without further extended stories, apart from the anecdotes reserved for the impediment of books.

Pali Source

Lābhoti cattāro paccayā. Te kathaṃ palibodhā honti? Puññavantassa hi bhikkhuno gatagataṭṭhāne manussā mahāparivāre paccaye denti. So tesaṃ anumodento dhammaṃ desento samaṇadhammaṃ kātuṃ na okāsaṃ labhati. Aruṇuggamanato yāva paṭhamayāmo, tāva manussasaṃsaggo na upacchijjati. Puna balavapaccūseyeva bāhullikapiṇḍapātikā āgantvā "bhante, asuko upāsako upāsikā amacco amaccadhītā tumhākaṃ dassanakāmā"ti vadanti, so "gaṇhāvuso, pattacīvaran"ti gamanasajjova hotīti niccabyāvaṭo, tasseva te paccayā palibodhā honti. Tena gaṇaṃ pahāya yattha naṃ na jānanti, tattha ekakena caritabbaṃ. Evaṃ so palibodho upacchijjatīti.

Gaṇoti suttantikagaṇo vā ābhidhammikagaṇo vā, yo tassa uddesaṃ vā paripucchaṃ vā dento samaṇadhammassa okāsaṃ na labhati, tasseva gaṇo palibodho hoti, tena so evaṃ upacchinditabbo. Evaṃ alabhamānena "mayhamāvuso, ekaṃ kiccaṃ atthi, tumhe yathāphāsukaṭṭhānāni gacchathā"ti gaṇaṃ pahāya attano kammaṃ kattabbanti.

Kammanti navakammaṃ. Taṃ karontena vaḍḍhakīādīhi laddhāladdhaṃ jānitabbaṃ, katākate ussukkaṃ āpajjitabbanti sabbadā palibodho hoti. Sopi evaṃ upacchinditabbo, sace appaṃ avasiṭṭhaṃ hoti niṭṭhapetabbaṃ. Sace bahu, saṅghikañce navakammaṃ, saṅghassa vā saṅghabhārahārakabhikkhūnaṃ vā niyyādetabbaṃ. Attano santakañce, attano bhārahārakānaṃ niyyādetabbaṃ. Tādise alabhantena saṅghassa pariccajitvā gantabbanti.

Addhānanti maggagamanaṃ. Yassa hi katthaci pabbajjāpekkho vā hoti, paccayajātaṃ vā kiñci laddhabbaṃ hoti. Sace taṃ alabhanto na sakkoti adhivāsetuṃ, araññaṃ pavisitvā samaṇadhammaṃ karontassapi gamikacittaṃ nāma duppaṭivinodanīyaṃ hoti, tasmā gantvā taṃ kiccaṃ tīretvāva samaṇadhamme ussukkaṃ kātabbanti.

Ru-Yi Translation

"Gain" means the four requisites. How do these become impediments? For a meritorious monk, wherever he goes, people give him requisites with great retinues. He, giving them the blessing and teaching them the Dhamma, finds no opportunity to do a recluse's work. From the rising of dawn until the first watch, his contact with people is not cut off. And then, in the very depth of the pre-dawn hour, monks accustomed to abundant almsfood come and say, "Venerable sir, such-and-such a layman, laywoman, minister, or minister's daughter wishes to see you" — and he, saying "take my bowl and robe, friend," is always ready to set out, constantly occupied; for him alone, these requisites are impediments. By this, he should give up the crowd and wander alone, somewhere he is not known. Thus that impediment is cut off.

"A following" means a group of students in the discourses or in the Abhidhamma — whoever, giving them recitation-instruction or answering their questions, finds no opportunity for a recluse's work, for him alone a following becomes an impediment; by him it should be cut off in this way. If those monks have taken up much and little remains, he should finish that and then enter the forest. If little has been taken up and much remains, without going beyond a league from them, within the boundary of a league he should approach another teacher of a group and say: "Let the venerable one take these students in hand with recitation and the rest." If, proceeding thus, he cannot obtain such a teacher, he should say: "Friends, I have a certain task; go wherever is comfortable for you," and, giving up the group, attend to his own work.

"Work" means construction work. For one engaged in it, what has and has not been obtained from carpenters and the like must be kept track of, and diligence must be applied to what is done and undone — it is thus, at all times, an impediment. This too should be cut off in this way: if little remains, it should be finished. If much remains, then, if the construction work belongs to the Sangha, it should be handed over to the Sangha or to monks who bear the Sangha's burdens; if it is his own, it should be handed over to those who bear his own burdens. If unable to find such a person, he should relinquish it to the Sangha and go.

"A journey" means travel by road. For whoever has someone somewhere expecting to go forth under him, or has some requisite yet to be obtained — if, not obtaining it, he is unable to bear the wait, then even for one who has entered the forest and is doing a recluse's work, the thought of the journey still to be made is hard to dispel; therefore he should go, settle that business, and only then apply diligence to a recluse's work.

Reading note — Ru-Yi

Each of these four impediments follows the same underlying shape already set by the dwelling and family stories: the thing itself (requisites, a group of pupils, a construction project, an unfinished errand) is morally neutral, but a monk's own success or entanglement in it can quietly consume the entire day meant for practice. The remedy prescribed for each is notably concrete and practical rather than merely attitudinal — leaving for an unfamiliar place, handing pupils to another teacher, formally transferring an unfinished building project, or simply finishing the errand first — reflecting this literature's consistent preference for a clear, actionable cutting-off over a vague resolution to "let go" internally.

The impediment of gain is also a quiet acknowledgment that a monk's own spiritual reputation can become an obstacle to him: it is precisely because he is "meritorious" (puññavantassa) — evidently respected and sought after — that people's generosity becomes a trap for his own practice. Success in the eyes of the lay community and progress in one's own meditation are treated here as two entirely separate things, capable of working directly against each other.

The Remaining Impediments: Relatives, Sickness, Books, and Psychic Power

The final four impediments close out the list of ten — relatives receive the chapter's most detailed practical guidance of any impediment beside dwelling and family, sickness turns on a striking piece of self-address, and books close with three anecdotes of elders whose scriptural mastery survived decades of complete neglect.

Pali Source

Ñātīti vihāre ācariyupajjhāyasaddhivihārikaantevāsikasamānupajjhāyakasamānācariyakā, ghare mātā pitā bhātāti evamādikā. Te gilānā imassa palibodhā honti, tasmā so palibodho upaṭṭhahitvā tesaṃ pākatikakaraṇena upacchinditabbo.

Ru-Yi Translation

"Relatives" means, in the monastery, one's teachers, preceptors, pupils, students, fellow pupils under the same preceptor, and fellow students under the same teacher; at home, one's mother, father, brother, and so on. When these are sick, they become an impediment for him; therefore that impediment should be cut off by attending upon them and restoring them to their normal state.

Herein, first, a sick preceptor, if he does not quickly recover, should be cared for even for the rest of one's life. Likewise the going-forth teacher, the higher-ordination teacher, one's own pupil, a pupil one has ordained or given the going-forth to, and fellow pupils under the same preceptor. But a dependence-teacher, a recitation-teacher, a pupil under one's dependence, a pupil receiving recitation-instruction, and fellow students under the same teacher should be cared for only for as long as the relation of dependence or instruction has not been ended — though if one is able, they should indeed be cared for even beyond that. Toward one's mother and father, one should act just as toward a preceptor: even if they hold royal power, and expect attendance from their son, it should indeed be given. If they have no medicine, one's own should be given; if none is at hand, it should indeed be sought by walking for alms and given. But for brothers and sisters, only what belongs to them should be procured and given. If they have none, one's own should be given temporarily, to be taken back later when something else is obtained; if nothing is obtained, they should not be pressed to repay it. For a sister's husband who is not a blood relative, it is not proper either to prepare or to give medicine directly — but, having said to the sister, "give this to your husband," it may be given to her to pass on. The same method applies to a brother's wife. But their children are indeed this one's own relatives, so it is proper to act for them directly.

"Sickness" means any illness. Afflicting the mind, it becomes an impediment; therefore it should be cut off by treating it with medicine. But if, even after treating it with medicine for some days, it does not subside, then, having reproached this body with the thought, "I am not your slave, nor your servant — it is precisely by nourishing you that I have met with suffering in the beginningless round of saṃsāra," a recluse's work should be done regardless.

"Books" means the carrying of the scriptures in mind. This is an impediment for one constantly occupied with recitation and the like, not for another. Here are the accounts —

It is said that the Elder Deva, a reciter of the Majjhima Nikāya, went to the presence of another Elder Deva who dwelt in Malaya, and asked for a meditation subject. The elder asked: "What is your standing, friend, in the scriptures?" "The Majjhima is well mastered by me, venerable sir." "Friend, this Majjhima is hard to maintain — while reciting the first fifty suttas, the middle fifty comes to mind and fades; while reciting that, the last fifty fades in turn. From where, then, would you get a meditation subject?" "Venerable sir, having obtained a meditation subject from you, I shall not look at the scriptures again" — and, taking up the meditation subject, without reciting for nineteen years, in the twentieth year he attained arahantship. And to monks who came for the purpose of recitation-study, he said: "For twenty years, friends, I have not looked at the scriptures — nevertheless, I am still practiced in this; begin." And from the beginning right to the end, he had no doubt even about a single syllable.

The Elder Nāga, too, who dwelt at Karuḷiyagiri, having set aside the scriptures for eighteen years, then gave recitation-instruction to monks on the Dhātukathā. When they compared it with the village-dwelling elders' own versions, not a single question came out in the wrong order.

At the Mahāvihāra, too, an elder named Tipiṭaka-Cūḷābhaya, thinking "without even having received the commentary, I shall recite through the three Piṭakas within the circle of the five Nikāyas," had the golden drum sounded to announce it. The community of monks said: "Whose teachers' transmission is this? Let him speak only his own teachers' transmission; otherwise we do not permit him to speak." His preceptor, too, when he came to attend upon him, asked: "Was it you, friend, who had the drum sounded?" "Yes, venerable sir." "For what reason?" "I shall recite the scriptures, venerable sir." "Friend Abhaya, how do the teachers explain this passage?" "They explain it thus, venerable sir." The elder rejected it with "no." Again he said, by one method after another, "they explain it thus, venerable sir" — three times. The elder rejected every one with "no," and said: "Friend, what you said first was indeed the teachers' own way — but because you have not received it from a teacher's own mouth, you were unable to stand firm in saying 'thus the teachers explain it.' Go and listen in the presence of your own teachers." "Where shall I go, venerable sir?" "Beyond the river, in the Rohaṇa district, at Tulādhārapabbata Monastery, there dwells an elder named Mahādhammarakkhita, master of the whole of the scriptures; go to his presence." "Very well, venerable sir," he said, and, having paid respects to the elder, went with five hundred monks to that elder's presence, paid respects, and sat down. The elder asked: "Why have you come?" "To hear the Dhamma, venerable sir." "Friend Abhaya, in the Dīgha and Majjhima, they question me from time to time. But the remainder I have not looked at for as much as thirty years. Nevertheless, you recite it to me at night; I shall explain it to you by day." He, saying "very well, venerable sir," did so. Having had a great pavilion built at the door of the dwelling, the village-dwellers came day after day to hear the Dhamma. The elder recited at night; explaining it by day, he in due course finished the whole exposition, and then, sitting on a mat before the Elder Abhaya, said: "Friend, tell me a meditation subject." "Venerable sir, what are you saying? Was it not from your own presence that I heard it? What could I explain that you do not already know?" Then the elder said to him: "Friend, that is a different matter — this is simply the way of one who has already traveled it." The Elder Abhaya was, it is said, a stream-enterer at that time. Having then given him a meditation subject, the elder Mahādhammarakkhita — Abhaya Thera later heard, while reciting the Dhamma at the Bronze Palace, that he had attained final Nibbāna. Hearing this, saying "bring my robe, friend," he put on his robe and said: "Fitting indeed, friends, is our teacher's path to arahantship. Our teacher, friends, was truly a thoroughbred. He, sitting on a mat before his own pupil in the Dhamma, said, 'Tell me a meditation subject.' Fitting indeed, friends, is the elder's path to arahantship." For those such as these, books are not an impediment.

"Psychic power" means the ordinary worldling's psychic power. For this is as hard to maintain as an infant lying on its back, or as a young crop; it breaks with the merest trifle. But this is an impediment to insight, not to concentration, since it is attained only after attaining concentration. Therefore, one who seeks insight should cut off the impediment of psychic power; another may leave it as it is. This, for now, is the detailed treatment of the discourse on the impediments.

Reading note — Ru-Yi

The treatment of sick relatives is the most finely graded piece of practical ethics in this whole section: care is calibrated exactly to the closeness and permanence of the relationship — lifelong for a preceptor, only for the duration of a formal dependence for a lesser teacher (though extended further if one is able), unconditional for parents even if they are royalty, and carefully bounded for in-laws, who may receive medicine only relayed through a blood relative, never handed to them directly. Nothing here reads as arbitrary; each distinction tracks a real difference in the kind of bond and obligation involved, worked out with the same precision the chapter earlier applied to jhāna factors.

The three scriptural anecdotes together make one cumulative point: mastery of the texts, once genuinely established, survives decades of total neglect — nineteen years, eighteen years, thirty years — because it was never really about moment-to-moment familiarity but about something more deeply set. The Tipiṭaka-Cūḷābhaya story adds a further layer: even vast learning is worthless without a traceable line of transmission from a living teacher, and the story's real payoff is the final scene, where the master eventually asks his own former pupil, now further along the path, for a meditation subject — a quiet demonstration that scriptural authority and spiritual attainment, while often found together, are not actually the same thing.

Two Kinds of Meditation Subject

Before turning to how a teacher should be found, the chapter first distinguishes two fundamentally different roles a meditation subject can play — one universal and preparatory, the other individually matched and sustained throughout the whole of practice.

Ru-Yi Translation

"Having approached a good friend able to give a meditation subject": herein, a meditation subject is twofold — the universal meditation subject and the individual meditation subject. Herein, the universal meditation subject is loving-kindness toward the community of monks and the rest, and mindfulness of death. Some also say the perception of foulness.

For a monk devoted to a meditation subject should, first of all, having delimited the scope, develop loving-kindness toward the community of monks within the boundary: "may they be happy, free from affliction." Then toward the deities within the boundary. Then toward the ruling people in the alms-resort village. Then, starting from the people there, toward all beings. For, by loving-kindness toward the community of monks, he produces gentleness of mind in those who dwell with him; then his life together with them becomes happy. By loving-kindness toward the deities within the boundary, he becomes one whose protection is well arranged, through the righteous protection of deities whose minds have been made gentle. By loving-kindness toward the ruling people of the alms-resort village, his requisites become well protected through the righteous protection of rulers whose minds have been made gentle. By loving-kindness toward the people there, he goes about unmolested by them, their minds having been made confident. By loving-kindness toward all beings, his going about is everywhere unobstructed. But by mindfulness of death, reflecting "I must certainly die," he gives up improper livelihood, has his sense of urgency growing ever further, and becomes one whose conduct does not sink into laxity. And for one whose mind is habituated by the perception of foulness, even heavenly objects do not overpower his mind by way of greed.

Because it is thus of great help, and because it should be desired and wished for everywhere, and because it is the ground of the meditator's intended work of continued application to practice, it is called the "universal meditation subject."

But among the forty meditation subjects, whichever suits a given person's temperament — because it is to be constantly carried by him, and because it is the proximate cause for each successively higher work of development — is called the "individual meditation subject." Thus, whoever gives both of these kinds of meditation subject is called "one who gives a meditation subject." That is "the giver of a meditation subject."

Reading note — Ru-Yi

The universal meditation subject's benefits are laid out as a widening series of concentric circles — fellow monks, then local deities, then the village's rulers, then its ordinary people, then all beings without limit — and each ring is paired with a very specific, practical payoff: harmony with those one lives among, protection by local deities, secure requisites, freedom from harassment, and finally unobstructed movement everywhere. Loving-kindness here is not presented as an abstract virtue but as something with concrete, almost logistical consequences for a meditator's ability to actually practice undisturbed — a very down-to-earth justification for what might otherwise look like a purely devotional exercise.

The distinction between "universal" and "individual" meditation subjects also quietly explains why this whole chapter needed the ten-impediments discussion before it could even reach the forty meditation subjects proper: the universal subjects (loving-kindness and mindfulness of death, certainly) are meant to be established first, as protective and motivating groundwork, before a meditator ever takes up the one subject — out of the forty still to come — that will become his sustained, individually-suited practice.

The Qualities of a Good Friend, and Whom to Approach

Having explained what a meditation subject is, the chapter turns to who is qualified to give one — opening with a canonical verse on friendship, then working down a careful hierarchy from the Buddha himself to the humblest conscientious custodian of even a single portion of the tradition.

Ru-Yi Translation

"A good friend": —

Dear and respected, worthy of esteem, a speaker of good counsel and one who patiently endures being spoken to, a maker of profound talk, and one who does not urge another toward what is improper. (A.N. 7.37)

One endowed with such qualities as these, wholly seeking one's welfare, standing on the side of growth — this is "a good friend."

But because of such sayings as "for, Ānanda, it is in dependence on me as a good friend that beings subject to birth become freed from birth" (S.N. 1.129; 5.2), the Fully Enlightened One himself alone is the good friend complete in every respect. Therefore, while he is present, a meditation subject taken in the presence of that very Blessed One is well taken. But when he has attained final Nibbāna, it is proper to take it in the presence of whichever of the eighty great disciples is still living. When none of these is available, it should be taken in the presence of one who has reached the destruction of the taints — an arahant — who has produced the jhānas of the fourfold or fivefold scheme by means of that very meditation subject one wishes to take up, and has developed insight with that jhāna as its proximate cause.

But does an arahant declare himself, saying "I am an arahant"? What should be said is this: knowing the pupil's capacity to accomplish the practice, he does reveal himself. Did not the Elder Assagutta, knowing of a monk who had taken up a meditation subject, "this one is capable of accomplishing the meditation subject," spread out a piece of hide in the air and, sitting there cross-legged, explain the meditation subject to him?

Therefore, if one obtains an arahant, that is good; if not, then among a non-returner, a once-returner, a stream-enterer, a worldling who has attained jhāna, a holder of the three Piṭakas, a holder of two Piṭakas, and a holder of one Piṭaka — in the presence of each earlier one in preference to each later. And if even a holder of one Piṭaka is not available, it should be taken in the presence of whoever has mastered even just a single portion of the communal recitation together with its commentary, and who is conscientious. For such a teacher, a holder of the textual tradition, a guardian of the lineage, a preserver of the transmitted custom, holds only his teachers' view, not his own private opinion. For this very reason, the ancient elders said three times: "a conscientious one will guard it, a conscientious one will guard it."

And here, the arahants and the rest already mentioned explain only the path they themselves have attained. But one of great learning, having approached this or that teacher and had his learning and questioning thoroughly purified, considering the discourse and the reasoning from here and there, applying what is suitable and unsuitable, going through difficult ground like a great elephant showing the main road, will explain the meditation subject properly. Therefore, having approached such a good friend able to give a meditation subject, and having performed the customary duties toward him, one should take up the meditation subject.

Reading note — Ru-Yi

The hierarchy running from the Buddha down through the great disciples, the arahants, the three lower noble persons, a mere jhāna-attaining worldling, and finally down to holders of one, two, or three Piṭakas, ending at "whoever has mastered even a single portion together with its commentary, provided he is conscientious," makes an important structural point: attainment is always preferred over mere learning, but learning combined with conscientiousness is treated as a genuinely sufficient fallback, not a poor substitute to be avoided. The three-times-repeated line "a conscientious one will guard it" reads almost like a proverb being quoted for reassurance — trustworthiness of transmission, not personal spiritual attainment, is what ultimately guarantees the teaching stays intact.

The final paragraph's contrast between an arahant (who "explains only the path he himself has attained") and one of great learning (bahussuta, who can draw on many teachers and match instruction to what actually suits the pupil) is a striking, almost counterintuitive point: direct personal attainment is not automatically the more versatile teaching qualification. An arahant knows his own path with certainty but only his own; a widely learned, well-trained teacher, precisely because he has cross-checked many sources against each other, may be better equipped to adapt his teaching to a given pupil's specific situation — the "great elephant clearing a path through difficult ground" image captures exactly this kind of practical, adaptive skill.

Reading note — Ru-Yi

The insistence on going straight to the teacher's dwelling, without first stopping to rest or clean up, is explained by a specifically social danger rather than any point of doctrine: monks unfavorably disposed toward that teacher might use the delay as an opening to talk the newcomer out of studying with him at all. The instruction is less about physical discipline than about protecting a fragile, still-forming decision from being undermined by gossip before it can even be acted on.

The oil-tube episode is a small masterclass in reading unstated social cues correctly: refusing the oil outright would offend, but using it immediately on the feet risks a different offense if it turns out to be the teacher's personal body-oil rather than a shared, all-purpose one — so the pupil is instructed to apply it first to the head and shoulders, the safer default, and only extend to the feet once the teacher has explicitly said the oil is for general use. Nothing here is stated as a fixed rule so much as a demonstration of the kind of attentiveness a pupil is expected to bring on his own, reading each new situation rather than reciting a memorized script.

The final instruction — that one should never let a meditation subject be explained at a time when one is physically unwell, and should say so honestly rather than push through — closes the whole section on a practical, almost medical note: attention itself is treated as a limited resource that illness genuinely depletes, and pretending otherwise would simply waste the teaching being offered.

The Six Temperaments

The chapter now turns to "in accordance with his own temperament" — first fixing the number of temperaments at six, then showing how three further temperaments proposed by some are best understood as reducing to greed and delusion, and how each of the three unwholesome temperaments has a natural wholesome counterpart that shares its underlying texture.

Now, as to "in accordance with his own temperament" — herein, "temperament" means six temperaments: the temperament of greed, the temperament of hatred, the temperament of delusion, the temperament of faith, the temperament of intelligence, and the temperament of discursive thought. Just as greed seeks out objects of sensual desire, so faith seeks out such qualities as virtue. Just as greed does not let go of what is harmful, so faith does not let go of what is beneficial; therefore, the temperament of faith is akin to the temperament of greed.

But because, at the time wholesome states occur in one of hating temperament, understanding becomes strong in him — since hatred has qualities close to those of understanding. For just as, on the unwholesome side, hatred is without affection and does not cling to its object, so too, on the wholesome side, is understanding. And just as hatred seeks out faults even where they do not exist, so understanding seeks out only real faults. Just as hatred proceeds by way of avoiding beings, so understanding proceeds by way of avoiding attachment to formations; therefore, the temperament of intelligence is akin to the temperament of hatred.

But because, for one of deluded temperament striving for the arising of wholesome states not yet arisen, obstructive thoughts mostly arise — since delusion has a characteristic close to that of discursive thought. For just as delusion is unsteady through confusion, so is discursive thought, through thinking in many different ways. And just as delusion is fickle through not penetrating its object, so is discursive thought, through its light, quick conceiving; therefore, the temperament of discursive thought is akin to the temperament of delusion.

Others speak of three further temperaments by way of craving, conceit, and views. Reading note — Ru-Yi

The pairing of each unwholesome temperament with a structurally similar wholesome one is a genuinely elegant piece of analysis: faith is not simply "the opposite of greed" but shares greed's own texture — its smoothness, its tendency to seek out and fasten onto something, its reluctance to let go — redirected toward a beneficial object instead of a harmful one. The same logic holds for understanding's kinship with hatred (both look critically for faults, both keep a certain distance from their object) and discursive thought's kinship with delusion (both are restless, quick-shifting, and lack settled penetration). Wholesome and unwholesome states are being shown here as sharing an underlying "shape," differing only in what they are aimed at.

The dismissal of the three further temperaments — craving, conceit, and views — is a tidy piece of Abhidhamma reduction: rather than treating these as genuinely new categories, the text simply reclassifies craving as greed itself, conceit as something that always accompanies greed, and views as always rooted in delusion, so the count stays at a manageable six rather than expanding indefinitely with every possible combination.

Ru-Yi Translation

But what is the source of these temperaments? And how should it be known, "this person is of greedy temperament, this person is of one or another of the temperaments of hatred and the rest"? What is suitable for a person of which temperament?

Herein, first, some say that the first three temperaments have their source in past habitual conduct, and in a source of elemental disorder. It is said that one who, in the past, was much given to pleasant undertakings and beautiful deeds becomes of greedy temperament, or one who, having passed away from a heaven, has arisen here. One who, in the past, was much given to deeds of cutting, killing, binding, and enmity becomes of hating temperament, or one who, having passed away from hell or from the nāga realm, has arisen here. One who, in the past, was much given to drinking liquor, and devoid of learning and inquiry, becomes of deluded temperament, or one who, having passed away from the animal realm, has arisen here — thus they speak of a source in past habitual conduct. But, owing to the predominance of two elements, the earth element and the water element, a person becomes of deluded temperament. Owing to the predominance of the other two, he becomes of hating temperament. But owing to all four being equal, he becomes of greedy temperament. And, among the bodily humors, one with predominant phlegm becomes of greedy temperament; one with predominant wind becomes of deluded temperament. Or: one with predominant phlegm becomes of deluded temperament; one with predominant wind becomes of greedy temperament — thus they speak of a source in elemental disorder.

Herein, since not all who, in the past, were much given to pleasant undertakings and beautiful deeds, and who, having passed away from a heaven, have arisen here, are of greedy temperament alone — nor are the others necessarily of hating or deluded temperament. And likewise, for the elements, there is, by the very method just stated, no fixed rule of predominance. And in the rule concerning the bodily humors, only the pair of greed and delusion was stated, and even that is self-contradictory from one statement to the next. And for the temperaments of faith and the rest, not even a single source was stated at all. Therefore all this is an unsettled, imprecise account.

But here is the determination in accordance with the view of the commentary-teachers; for this was said in the "Account of Predominance" (Dhammasaṅgaṇī-aṭṭhakathā 498): "These beings, by the fixed order of their past causes, are ones with predominant greed, predominant hatred, predominant delusion, predominant non-greed, predominant non-hatred, and predominant non-delusion.

For one in whom, at the moment of accumulating that kamma, greed was strong and non-greed weak, while non-hatred and non-delusion were strong and hatred and delusion weak — for him, the weak non-greed is unable to overpower the greed, but the strong non-hatred and non-delusion are able to overpower the hatred and delusion. Therefore he, born by way of the rebirth-linking given by that kamma, is greedy, yet of a happy disposition, free from anger, wise, with knowledge like a diamond.

But for one in whom, at that moment, greed and hatred were strong and non-greed and non-hatred weak, while non-delusion was strong and delusion weak — he, by the same method as before, is both greedy and hating; but he is wise, with knowledge like a diamond, like the Elder Dattābhaya.

For one in whom, at that moment, greed, non-hatred, and delusion were strong and the others weak — he, by the same method as before, is both greedy and dull-witted, but virtuous and free from anger, like the Elder Bākula.

Likewise, for one in whom, at that moment, all three of greed, hatred, and delusion were strong and non-greed and the rest weak — he, by the same method as before, is greedy, hating, and deluded, all three.

But for one in whom, at that moment, non-greed, hatred, and delusion were strong and the others weak — he, by the same method as before, is not greedy and has few defilements in that regard, unmoved even on seeing a heavenly object; but he is hating and dull-witted.

But for one in whom, at that moment, non-greed, non-hatred, and delusion were strong and the others weak — he, by the same method as before, is not greedy and not hating, and virtuous; but he is dull-witted.

Likewise, for one in whom, at that moment, non-greed, hatred, and non-delusion were strong and the others weak — he, by the same method as before, is not greedy and is wise; but he is hating and prone to anger.

But for one in whom, at that moment, all three of non-greed, non-hatred, and non-delusion were strong and greed and the rest weak — he, by the same method as before, is, like the Elder Mahāsaṅgharakkhita, not greedy, not hating, and wise."

And herein, whoever is said to be "greedy" is of greedy temperament. The hating and the dull-witted are of hating and deluded temperament respectively. The wise is of intelligent temperament. The not-greedy-and-not-hating, by nature of being trusting, are of faithful temperament. Or: just as one born by kamma accompanied by non-delusion is of intelligent temperament, so one born by kamma accompanied by strong faith is of faithful temperament. One born by kamma accompanied by thoughts of sensual desire and the like is of discursive-thought temperament. One born by kamma accompanied by a mixture of greed and the rest is of mixed temperament. Thus it should be understood that the source of the temperaments is the rebirth-producing kamma accompanied by one or another of greed and the rest.

Reading note — Ru-Yi

The text's treatment of the two popular theories is worth pausing on for its method, not just its conclusion: it does not simply reject "past habits" and "bodily humors" as wrong on principle, but tests each against its own claimed predictions and shows the predictions fail — not everyone who was generous in a past life or reborn from a heaven is greedy-tempered, the elemental rule cannot even keep its own predominance-count straight, and the humor theory contradicts itself between its two stated versions. This is a genuinely empirical style of argument within a pre-modern commentarial text: a theory is judged by whether the cases it is supposed to explain actually bear it out.

The eight-combination analysis that follows is a small masterpiece of systematic enumeration: with three unwholesome roots (greed, hatred, delusion) each either strong or weak at the moment a person's rebirth-producing kamma was formed, every possible combination is worked through in turn, from "greed alone strong" through "all three strong" to the ideal "none of the three strong" — Elder Mahāsaṅgharakkhita's case. The three named elders (Dattābhaya, Bākula, Mahāsaṅgharakkhita) are not chosen at random: each illustrates a specific, real combination of qualities — wise yet still hating, virtuous yet dull, or the ideal free of all three defilements — grounding an abstract combinatorial scheme in remembered, individual people.

Key vocabulary — for Pāli learners
nidāna

a source, origin, cause — the term this whole section investigates, testing and then rejecting two popular candidates before settling on the commentarial tradition's own account.

ussada

predominance, excess — ud (up) + sad (to sit, settle); the key term borrowed directly from the cited "Account of Predominance" (Ussadakittana) in the Dhammasaṅgaṇī commentary.

kammāyūhana

the accumulating of kamma — kamma (action) + āyūhana (heaping up, accumulation); the precise moment, prior to rebirth, whose exact configuration of strong and weak roots this whole analysis is built around.

paṭisandhi

rebirth-linking — the moment of conception-consciousness that inherits the specific balance of roots established by past kamma, directly producing the new being's temperament.

vajirūpamañāṇa

knowledge like a diamond — vajira (diamond, thunderbolt) + upama (simile) + ñāṇa (knowledge); the recurring description here for sharp, penetrating wisdom, applied to several of the eight combinations.
