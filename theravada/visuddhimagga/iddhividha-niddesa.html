The Kinds of Supernormal Power

This page carries Ru-Yi's translation of the Visuddhimagga's chapter 12, Iddhividhaniddesa ("The Discussion of the Kinds of Supernormal Power") — the opening chapter of the treatise's second part, and the first of two chapters treating the direct knowledges available to one who has mastered concentration. It works through all ten kinds of iddhi in the Paṭisambhidāmagga's classical list, devotes the bulk of its length to the power of resolve in full technical and narrative detail, and closes with the more compact powers of transformation and the mind-made body — before the treatise turns, in the next chapter, to the remaining five direct knowledges.

Five Mundane Direct Knowledges, and Fourteen Ways to Tame the Mind

Having completed the entire treatment of concentration proper, the treatise now turns to direct knowledge — the fruit that makes concentration's development worthwhile beyond its own immediate benefits, and the necessary foundation for the wisdom section still to come. The Buddha himself named five mundane direct knowledges reachable through the fourth jhāna: supernormal power, the divine ear, penetration of others' minds, recollection of past lives, and knowledge of beings' passing away and rebirth. Before any of these can be attempted, the meditator who has already reached the fourth jhāna in each of the eight kasiṇas must first tame his mind through fourteen distinct exercises — moving through the kasiṇas and jhānas forward, backward, and in combination — the same kind of rigorous preparatory drilling an apprentice undergoes before being trusted with delicate, high-stakes work.

Ru-Yi Translation

Now, in order to accomplish those mundane direct knowledges by virtue of which this development of concentration was said to have the benefit of direct knowledge, the yogi who has already attained the fourth jhāna in the earth kasiṇa and the rest must apply himself to practice. For thus his development of concentration will both have its benefit actually attained and become more firmly established, and, endowed with such a development of concentration whose benefit has been attained and which has become more firmly established, he will accomplish the development of wisdom with ease. Therefore we shall now begin the discussion of direct knowledge.

For the Blessed One, both in order to show the benefit of the development of concentration to those sons of good family who had attained the concentration of the fourth jhāna, and in order to teach an ever more sublime and refined Dhamma, spoke of the five mundane direct knowledges by the method beginning, "with mind thus concentrated, purified, bright, without blemish, free from defilement, grown soft and workable, steady, and attained to imperturbability, he directs and inclines his mind toward the various kinds of supernormal power. He experiences the various kinds of supernormal power: having been one, he becomes many" (D.N. 1.238) — namely, supernormal power, the knowledge of the element of the divine ear, the knowledge of penetrating others' minds, the knowledge of recollecting past lives, and the knowledge of the passing away and rebirth of beings.

Herein, a beginning yogi wishing to perform the transformation of supernormal power beginning with "having been one, he becomes many" should, having produced the eight attainments in each of the eight kasiṇas ending with the white kasiṇa, then proceed —

by way of the kasiṇas in forward order, by way of the kasiṇas in reverse order, by way of the kasiṇas in forward and reverse order; by way of the jhānas in forward order, by way of the jhānas in reverse order, by way of the jhānas in forward and reverse order; by way of skipping jhānas, by way of skipping kasiṇas, by way of skipping both jhānas and kasiṇas; by way of transposing factors, by way of transposing objects, by way of transposing both factors and objects; by way of defining factors, and by way of defining objects.

By these fourteen methods the mind should be tamed.

Reading note — Ru-Yi

The requirement that a meditator have already mastered the fourth jhāna in all eight kasiṇas — not merely one — before attempting any of these fourteen exercises is a significant escalation beyond anything demanded so far in this treatise. Every earlier chapter treated the kasiṇas somewhat independently, as alternative doors to the same basic attainment; here, for the first time, genuine mastery is defined as fluency across all eight simultaneously, since the fourteen taming exercises depend on being able to move freely between them.

The fourteen categories themselves are best understood as three families of drill rather than fourteen unrelated techniques: moving through the kasiṇas alone, moving through the jhānas alone, and moving through both together — each family then further varied by direction (forward, reverse, both) and by whether progress is continuous or deliberately skips steps. The goal in every case is the same: a mind so flexible it can shift its concentrated state on command, in any direction, without hesitation.

Key vocabulary — for Pāli learners
abhiññā

direct knowledge, supernormal knowledge — abhi- (toward, intensively) + jñā (to know); the five mundane attainments this chapter and the next treat, all reached by refining the fourth jhāna already mastered in the concentration section.

paridameti

to tame, to fully train — pari- (thoroughly) + dam- (to tame, subdue); the verb describing what the fourteen exercises accomplish, treating the concentrated mind as something requiring active, disciplined conditioning before it can perform supernormal feats.

ukkantika

skipping, moving by intervals — describing three of the fourteen methods in which the meditator deliberately jumps over intervening jhānas or kasiṇas rather than progressing through every one in sequence, a harder variant of the basic forward/reverse drill.

The Fourteen Exercises Defined, One by One

Each of the fourteen naming categories from the previous section is now defined precisely. Moving through the eight kasiṇas from earth to white in strict sequence is "forward"; reversing that order is "backward"; alternating between the two is "forward-and-backward." The same three variations apply to the four jhānas and the four immaterial attainments taken together as a single eight-step ladder. "Skipping" methods deliberately jump over an intervening jhāna or kasiṇa rather than passing through it. "Transposing" methods hold either the jhāna or the object fixed while varying the other, or vary both together. And the two "defining" methods simply catalogue, without any actual re-attainment, how many factors each jhāna has, or which kasiṇa is which.

Ru-Yi Translation

Herein, what is "the kasiṇas in forward order," and so on up to what is "defining objects"? Here, a monk attains jhāna in the earth kasiṇa, then in the water kasiṇa, and so on, attaining in the eight kasiṇas in this order a hundred times or even a thousand times — this is called "the kasiṇas in forward order."

Attaining in that same order, but starting from the white kasiṇa and proceeding in reverse, is called "the kasiṇas in reverse order."

Attaining again and again, from the earth kasiṇa up to the white kasiṇa, and then from the white kasiṇa back down to the earth kasiṇa, by way of forward and reverse order, is called "the kasiṇas in forward and reverse order."

Attaining again and again, in order, from the first jhāna up to the base of neither-perception-nor-non-perception, is called "the jhānas in forward order."

Attaining again and again, from the base of neither-perception-nor-non-perception back down to the first jhāna, is called "the jhānas in reverse order."

Attaining again and again, from the first jhāna up to the base of neither-perception-nor-non-perception, and then from the base of neither-perception-nor-non-perception back down to the first jhāna, by way of forward and reverse order, is called "the jhānas in forward and reverse order."

Having attained the first jhāna in the earth kasiṇa, then attaining the third jhāna in that same kasiṇa, then, having removed that very kasiṇa, the base of infinite space, then the base of nothingness — thus skipping over the kasiṇa while advancing the jhāna by alternating steps, is called "skipping jhānas." The same application should be made starting from the water kasiṇa and the rest.

Having attained the first jhāna in the earth kasiṇa, then that same first jhāna in the fire kasiṇa, then in the blue kasiṇa, then in the red kasiṇa — thus, by this method, skipping over the jhāna while advancing the kasiṇa by alternating steps, is called "skipping kasiṇas."

Having attained the first jhāna in the earth kasiṇa, then the third jhāna in the fire kasiṇa, then, having removed the blue kasiṇa, the base of infinite space, then, from the red kasiṇa, the base of nothingness — by this method, skipping over both the jhāna and the kasiṇa, is called "skipping both jhānas and kasiṇas."

Having attained the first jhāna in the earth kasiṇa, then attaining the other jhānas in that very same kasiṇa, is called "transposing factors."

Having attained the first jhāna in the earth kasiṇa, then that very same first jhāna in the water kasiṇa, and so on, that very same first jhāna in the white kasiṇa — thus attaining one and the same jhāna across all the kasiṇas, is called "transposing objects."

Having attained the first jhāna in the earth kasiṇa, the second in the water kasiṇa, the third in the fire kasiṇa, the fourth in the wind kasiṇa, then, having removed the blue kasiṇa, the base of infinite space; from the yellow kasiṇa, the base of infinite consciousness; from the red kasiṇa, the base of nothingness; from the white kasiṇa, the base of neither-perception-nor-non-perception — thus transposing both the factors and the objects by alternating steps, is called "transposing both factors and objects."

Having determined that the first jhāna has five factors, the second three factors, and the third two factors, and likewise that the fourth jhāna, the base of infinite space, and so on up to the base of neither-perception-nor-non-perception each have their own number of factors — this mere determination of the jhāna-factors is called "defining factors."

Likewise, having determined, "this is the earth kasiṇa," "this is the water kasiṇa," and so on, "this is the white kasiṇa" — this mere determination of the objects is called "defining objects." Some also wish to include "defining both factors and objects," but since this does not occur in the commentaries, it is certainly not an actual approach to development.

Reading note — Ru-Yi

The distinction between "skipping jhānas" and "skipping kasiṇas" is a fine but important one: in the first, the meditator stays with a single kasiṇa but leaps across jhāna levels (first, then third, then straight into the formless attainments); in the second, the meditator stays within a single jhāna level but leaps across different kasiṇa objects. Only the third variant, skipping both together, actually combines the two kinds of leap at once — a genuinely demanding test of a mind's flexibility.

The closing note about "defining both factors and objects" is a small but telling piece of scholarly restraint: even though some unnamed teachers apparently wanted to round the list out to a symmetrical fifteen, the commentarial tradition itself never records this combined method as an actual practice, and the text declines to invent one merely for the sake of completeness — a reminder that this treatise generally prefers attested practice over theoretical tidiness.

Key vocabulary — for Pāli learners
anuloma / paṭiloma

forward order / reverse order — anu- (following) + loma (hair, i.e. "with the grain") versus paṭi- (against) + loma ("against the grain"); the basic directional pair applied to both the kasiṇa sequence and the jhāna sequence throughout this section.

saṅkantika

transposing, transferring — saṃ- (together) + kram- (to step, to go); describing the exercises that hold one variable (a jhāna factor or a kasiṇa object) fixed while systematically varying the other.

vavatthāpana

defining, determining — the two purely analytical exercises (of factors, and of objects) that require no actual re-entry into any attainment, simply a clear mental cataloguing of what has already been mastered.

One in a Hundred Thousand: How Rare Real Mastery Actually Is

The chapter pauses to state, with unusual bluntness, exactly how difficult this whole path actually is: at every single stage — the kasiṇa's preliminary work, producing its sign, developing that sign to absorption, taming the mind by the fourteen methods, performing the transformation itself, and finally becoming quick enough at it to be reliable — only one meditator in a hundred, or a thousand, succeeds. Two stories illustrate the stakes of that final, rarest quality, quick-wittedness: thirty thousand monks, shamed into urgency by watching a single eight-year monk single-handedly protect their ailing teacher from a threatening nāga, and an elder who conjured solid ground in mid-air to shield a crowd from a rain of burning embers sent by Māra himself. The section closes with an important exception: Buddhas, Paccekabuddhas, and chief disciples need none of this graduated training at all, since their supernormal powers arise automatically the moment they attain arahantship — the long apprenticeship described here is for everyone else.

Ru-Yi Translation

But there is simply no possibility whatsoever that a beginning meditator, whose development is not yet complete, could accomplish the transformation of supernormal power without first having tamed his mind by these fourteen methods. For the beginner, even the preliminary work on the kasiṇa is a burden — only one in a hundred, or in a thousand, is capable of it. For one who has done the preliminary work on the kasiṇa, producing the sign is a burden — only one in a hundred, or in a thousand, is capable of it. For one in whom the sign has arisen, developing it and reaching absorption is a burden — only one in a hundred, or in a thousand, is capable of it. For one who has reached absorption, taming the mind by the fourteen methods is a burden — only one in a hundred, or in a thousand, is capable of it. And for one whose mind has been tamed by the fourteen methods, this transformation of supernormal power is itself a burden — only one in a hundred, or in a thousand, is capable of it. And even for one who has reached that transformation, being quick-witted at it is itself a burden — only one in a hundred, or in a thousand, becomes quick-witted. It was just so with Elder Rakkhita, only eight years since his higher ordination, at Therambatthala, when about thirty thousand monks possessed of supernormal power had come to tend the ailing Elder Mahārohaṇagutta. His power has already been told in the exposition of the earth kasiṇa (Vism. 1.78 and following). Having seen that power of his, the elder said: "Friends, had Rakkhita not been present, we would all have deserved blame, people saying, 'they were unable to protect the nāga-king.' Therefore, a weapon one carries and wields oneself should only be carried and wielded after its rust has first been cleaned away." Standing by the elder's admonition, all thirty thousand monks became quick-witted.

And even when quick-wittedness is present, being a support for others is itself a burden — only one in a hundred, or in a thousand, is capable of it — as with the elder who, when Māra caused a rain of embers to fall during the Giribhaṇḍavāhana festival, conjured earth in the sky and thereby protected the people from that rain of embers.

But for those of powerful prior practice — Buddhas, Paccekabuddhas, chief disciples, and the like — this transformation of supernormal power, and other distinct qualities such as the discriminating knowledges, are accomplished by the mere attainment of arahantship alone, without this stated sequence of development at all. Therefore, just as a goldsmith wishing to make an ornament first makes the gold soft and workable by blowing on the fire and the like, and just as a potter wishing to make a vessel first makes the clay soft by thoroughly kneading it — just so, a beginner should first tame his mind by these fourteen methods, and make it soft and workable by way of entering jhāna with desire, mind, energy, or investigation as the predominant factor, and by way of mastery in adverting and the rest, before carrying out the practice for supernormal power. But one endowed with sufficient prior cause may do so merely with mastery of the fourth jhāna alone in the kasiṇas. And to show the manner in which this practice should be carried out, the Blessed One spoke thus, beginning, "with mind thus concentrated..."

Reading note — Ru-Yi

The cascading "one in a hundred, or in a thousand" refrain, repeated six times in strict succession, is a deliberately sobering piece of rhetoric: even granting the most generous reading — one in a hundred at each stage — the compounded probability of clearing all six hurdles in succession is vanishingly small, and the text clearly intends the reader to register just how exceptional a fully accomplished master of supernormal power actually is, rather than treating these powers as a routine reward for ordinary diligence.

The goldsmith and potter similes, closing the section, make a specific point about sequencing rather than difficulty: neither craftsman can shape valuable material until it has first been made soft and workable, and the fourteen taming exercises play exactly this preparatory role for the mind — not the supernormal feat itself, but the necessary conditioning that makes the mind receptive to being shaped into it at all.

Eight Qualities of a Mind Fit for Direct Knowledge

The chapter now glosses, word by word and then by a second complete method, the canonical formula describing the exact quality of mind needed before any direct knowledge can be realized. Purified, bright, without blemish, free from defilement, grown soft, workable, steady, and attained to imperturbability — eight terms are shown to build on one another in careful sequence, purity yielding brightness, blemish-freedom yielding freedom from defilement, softness yielding workability like well-refined gold, and a final steadiness so complete it is guarded on six fronts at once — by faith against faithlessness, by energy against laziness, by mindfulness against negligence, by concentration against restlessness, by wisdom against ignorance, and by its own luminosity against the darkness of the defilements.

Here is the explanatory discussion, following the canonical wording itself. Herein, "he" is that yogi who has attained the fourth jhāna. "Thus" points to the sequence leading to the fourth jhāna — the meaning is, having attained the fourth jhāna by this sequence beginning with the attainment of the first jhāna. "Concentrated" means concentrated by this concentration of the fourth jhāna. "Mind" means fine-material-sphere consciousness. As for "purified" and the rest: it is purified by the purity of equanimity and mindfulness. Precisely because it is purified, it is "bright" — the meaning is, radiant. Because, through the destruction of the conditions for pleasure and the rest, it is free of the blemish of lust and the rest, it is "without blemish." Precisely because it is without blemish, it is "free from defilement" — for it is by blemish that a mind becomes defiled. Because it has been well developed, it is "grown soft" — the meaning is, having attained mastery, for a mind that operates under its own control is called "soft." And precisely because it is soft, it is "workable" — the meaning is, capable of work, fit for work. For a soft mind is workable, like well-refined gold, and both of these qualities come about precisely through good development. As it was said: "Monks, I do not see even a single other thing that, when thus developed and made much of, becomes as soft and as workable as the mind" (A.N. 1.22).

Because it is established in these states of purity and the rest, it is "steady." Precisely because it is steady, it is "attained to imperturbability" — the meaning is, immovable, unshaken. Or: because it is steady by way of a soft, workable state under its own control, it is "steady." Because it is grasped and protected by faith and the rest, it is "attained to imperturbability." For a mind grasped by faith is not shaken by lack of faith. One grasped by energy is not shaken by laziness. One grasped by mindfulness is not shaken by negligence. One grasped by concentration is not shaken by restlessness. One grasped by wisdom is not shaken by ignorance. One that has gone to luminosity is not shaken by the darkness of the defilements. Grasped by these six qualities, it becomes "attained to imperturbability." Thus, a mind endowed with these eight factors is capable of being directed toward the direct realization, through direct knowledge, of those things that are to be realized through direct knowledge.

Here is another method: "concentrated" by the concentration of the fourth jhāna. "Purified" by being far removed from the hindrances. "Bright" by having transcended applied thought and the rest. "Without blemish" by the absence of the desire-based occasions that are conditions for the attainment of jhāna. "Free from defilement" by the removal of the mind's defilements such as covetousness and the rest. Both of these should be understood in accordance with the Anaṅgaṇa Sutta and the Vatthūpama Sutta (M.N. 1.57 and following). "Grown soft" by the attainment of mastery. "Workable" by arriving at the state of a basis for supernormal power. "Steady" and "attained to imperturbability" by arriving at a sublime state through the fulfillment of development — the meaning is, it is "steady" in such a way that it becomes "attained to imperturbability." Thus too, a mind endowed with these eight factors is capable of being directed toward the direct realization, through direct knowledge, of those things to be realized through direct knowledge, becoming the basis, the proximate cause, for that realization.

Reading note — Ru-Yi

The two explanations offered here are not competing interpretations but complementary lenses on the same eight terms: the first reads them as a chain of mutual causation (purity causing brightness, blemish-freedom causing freedom from defilement, softness causing workability), while the second maps each term directly onto specific technical categories already established elsewhere in the canon (the hindrances, applied thought, the desire-based occasions named in the Anaṅgaṇa Sutta) — offering the same eight-fold structure once as an internal psychological process and once as a checklist against named obstacles.

The sixfold guarding of "imperturbability" — faith against faithlessness, energy against laziness, mindfulness against negligence, concentration against restlessness, wisdom against ignorance, and luminosity against the darkness of defilement — quietly reintroduces the five spiritual faculties (saddhā, vīriya, sati, samādhi, paññā) from far earlier in the Buddhist path, showing that even this specialized, advanced preparation for supernormal power ultimately rests on exactly the same basic qualities cultivated from the very beginning of practice.

What "Power" Means, and Ten Kinds of It

The second and much longer part of the chapter turns from the mind's preparation to the ten kinds of "power" (iddhi) itself, opening with a careful etymological survey of the word's range of meaning: succeeding in the sense of accomplishment and attainment (as when a desire "succeeds" for one who pursues it), succeeding as the fruit of a properly accomplished means (as when a virtuous person's sincere resolve bears fruit precisely because of its own purity), and succeeding as the specific quality by which beings "become powerful, prosperous, arrived at excellence." This last sense yields the canonical list of ten distinct kinds of power the rest of the chapter will work through one by one: resolve, transformation, the mind-made body, the pervasive influence of knowledge, the pervasive influence of concentration, the power of the noble ones, kamma-born power, the power of merit, power consisting in magical knowledge, and simple success through proper application.

Reading note — Ru-Yi

The three-fold etymological survey establishes something important before any of the ten specific powers is even named: "iddhi" is not, at its root, a magical or occult term at all, but the ordinary word for something simply working, succeeding, or bearing fruit — the same word used of an ordinary person's sincere wish coming true. The word's later, more exotic sense (flying, walking through walls, and so on) is only one particular application of this much broader, thoroughly mundane meaning of successful accomplishment.

The ten-item list is deliberately heterogeneous in a way worth flagging early: it ranges from an ascetic's deliberately cultivated meditative attainments (resolve, transformation) to a bird's ordinary capacity for flight (kamma-born power) to a wheel-turning monarch's wealth (the power of merit) — a genuine reminder that this treatise's ten "powers" span everything from rigorous jhāna-based cultivation to the simple, unremarkable consequences of having been born with a particular kind of body or accumulated a particular kind of merit.

Resolve, Transformation, and the Mind-Made Body

The first three of the ten powers, all directly cultivated through meditation, are defined in quick succession: the power of resolve, by which one adverts to being a hundred, a thousand, or a hundred thousand and then simply determines it so by knowledge; the power of transformation, by which one abandons one's natural appearance and displays instead the form of a boy, a nāga, or even an entire army in battle array; and the power of the mind-made body, by which a second body, complete with form, is created from within the meditator's own physical body. These three will occupy the bulk of the chapter's remaining technical discussion once the other seven, more varied kinds of power have been surveyed.

Reading note — Ru-Yi

The distinction between transformation and the mind-made body is easy to blur but structurally important: transformation replaces the meditator's own appearance entirely — his natural form is abandoned, and a boy or a nāga is seen where he used to be — while the mind-made body is created in addition to the original body, a genuinely second, independently functioning form produced from within the first. One is a substitution; the other is a multiplication.

These three powers are singled out for extended treatment later in the chapter precisely because they alone depend on the elaborate jhāna-based apparatus — the fourteen taming exercises, the four bases, the sixteen roots — built up over the preceding sections. The remaining seven kinds of power, taken up next, arise through entirely different mechanisms: direct knowledge itself, concentration's own pervasive effect, noble attainment, kamma, merit, esoteric knowledge, and simple skillful effort.

The Power of Knowledge: Three Children Protected by an Arahantship Not Yet Attained

The fourth power, arising from the pervasive influence of knowledge, is illustrated not by any deliberate meditative feat but by three extraordinary childhood survival stories, each attributed retroactively to the future arahant-path knowledge each child would one day attain in that very lifetime. Bākula, swallowed whole by a fish as an infant after falling into a river, emerged unharmed when a merchant's wife cut the fish open expecting only its flesh. Saṃkicca survived being pierced by a stake and burned on his own mother's funeral pyre while still in her womb, crying out at the very moment of the blow and so revealing that he still lived. And the boy Bhūtapāla, accidentally left outside a locked city gate overnight in a place haunted by fierce spirits, came through three full watches of darkness entirely unharmed. In each case, the text's claim is not that the child somehow already possessed a power he could consciously use, but that the future attainment yet to come somehow already exerted a protective influence backward across the very lifetime that would eventually reach it.

Thus, his being unharmed within the fish's belly was, for Venerable Bākula — who was in his final existence — produced by the power of the arahant-path knowledge yet to be attained by him within that very existence; therefore it is called "the power arising from the pervasive influence of knowledge." The story should be told in full detail.

As for Elder Saṃkicca: while he was still in the womb, his mother died. Having placed her body on the funeral pyre and pierced it through with stakes, while it was being burned, the child, receiving a blow to the eye-socket from the tip of a stake, cried out. Then, realizing, "the child is alive," they took her down, split open her belly, and gave the child to his grandmother. Raised by her, and having grown up, he went forth, and, together with the discriminating knowledges, attained arahantship. Thus, by the very same method, his being unharmed upon the wooden funeral pyre is called, for Venerable Saṃkicca, "the power arising from the pervasive influence of knowledge."

As for the boy Bhūtapāla: his father was a poor man in Rājagaha. He went by cart to the forest for wood, and, having made up a load of it, reached the vicinity of the city gate in the evening. Then his oxen, having thrown off the yoke, entered the city. He, having seated his little son at the foot of the cart, went following after the oxen, and entered the city himself. Before he could come back out, the gate was shut. That boy's being unharmed for the three watches of the night outside the city, in a place haunted by fierce spirits, is, by the very same method, called "the power arising from the pervasive influence of knowledge." The story should be told in detail.

Reading note — Ru-Yi

What makes this power genuinely distinct from the other nine is its direction of causation: in every other kind of power, some already-attained state (a jhāna, a resolve, an accumulated store of merit) produces an effect going forward. Here, the causal arrow runs backward — a knowledge these three children would each attain only later in the very same lifetime is said to have already been protecting them before it existed as an actual attainment, purely by virtue of the fact that it was destined to arise in that specific life-continuum.

The choice of three ordinary, powerless children — an infant, an unborn fetus, and a toddler abandoned by accident — rather than three already-accomplished meditators is a deliberate way of isolating what this power actually is: none of the three did anything, practiced anything, or even possessed the capacity for volition sufficient to invoke a power in any conventional sense. The protection is attributed entirely to the pull of a future they had not yet reached, not to any present skill of their own.

The Power of Concentration: A Blow That Landed, a Pyre That Burned Nothing

The fifth power, arising from the pervasive influence of concentration, works by the same backward and sideways logic as the fourth, but now attached to serenity rather than knowledge. Three of its five canonical examples are given here. Venerable Sāriputta, struck on the head hard enough to sound like thunder by a malicious spirit, took no harm at all because he happened to be absorbed in an attainment at the exact moment of the blow. Elder Sañjīva, mistaken for dead while deep in the attainment of cessation, was set alight by cowherds performing what they took to be a cremation — and not even a thread of his robe burned. And Elder Khāṇukoṇḍañña, absorbed so still and so long in the forest at night that five hundred passing bandits mistook him for a tree stump and piled their entire stolen haul on top of him, rose up unharmed exactly when his own predetermined attainment ended, so startling the bandits that all of them went forth and attained arahantship on the spot.

Ru-Yi Translation

The special distinction produced by the power of serenity, whether before, after, or at the very moment of concentration, is "the power arising from the pervasive influence of concentration." For this was said: "the abandonment of the hindrances through the first jhāna succeeds — thus it is the power arising from the pervasive influence of concentration … the abandonment of the perception of the base of nothingness through the attainment of the base of neither-perception-nor-non-perception succeeds — thus it is the power arising from the pervasive influence of concentration. Venerable Sāriputta's is the power arising from the pervasive influence of concentration; Venerable Sañjīva's; Venerable Khāṇukoṇḍañña's; the laywoman Uttarā's; the laywoman Sāmāvatī's is the power arising from the pervasive influence of concentration" (Paṭis. iii.16).

Herein, when Venerable Sāriputta, dwelling together with Elder Mahāmoggallāna at Kapotakandarā, was sitting in the open air on a moonlit night with his hair newly shaved, a certain malicious spirit, although restrained by a companion spirit, struck him a blow on the head — a blow whose sound was like thunder from a roaring cloud. At that very time, the elder had entered an attainment at the moment of the blow. And from that blow, no harm whatsoever befell him. This is that venerable one's power arising from the pervasive influence of concentration. The story has already come down in the Udāna (Ud. 34).

As for Elder Sañjīva: cowherds and others, observing him, while he had entered the attainment of cessation, as having died, gathered grass, sticks, and cow-dung and set fire to it. Not even a single thread of the elder's robe was burned. This is his power arising from the pervasive influence of concentration, produced by the power of serenity proceeding by way of the successive attainments. The story has already come down in the sutta (M.N. 1.507).

Elder Khāṇukoṇḍañña, for his part, was by nature one who dwelt much in attainment. Once, in a certain forest, having entered an attainment at night, he sat down. Five hundred bandits, having stolen goods and being on their way, wishing to rest, and thinking, "now there is no one following on our path," while setting down their goods, thinking, "this is a stump," placed all their goods on top of the elder himself. Then, when those who had rested were about to leave and were taking up the goods that had been set down first, the elder, at the predetermined ending of his own attainment, rose up. Seeing the elder's movement, they cried out in fear. The elder said, "Do not be afraid, lay followers — I am a monk." They came, paid homage, and, through the confidence inspired by the elder's conduct, went forth, and, together with the discriminating knowledges, attained arahantship (Dhp. Comm. 1.1). Here, the elder's freedom from harm, though buried beneath five hundred loads of goods, is "the power arising from the pervasive influence of concentration."

Reading note — Ru-Yi

Each of these three stories isolates a different temporal relationship named in the definition itself: Sāriputta's protection occurs precisely "at the very moment" of concentration, since he happened to be absorbed exactly when struck; Sañjīva's occurs while he remains in an attainment that has already begun and continues through the danger; and Khāṇukoṇḍañña's ends at a moment predetermined in advance, his own rising serving as the very thing that reveals the danger and resolves it. None of the three deliberately used concentration as a shield — the protection was simply a side-effect of a state they had entered for its own sake.

The bandits' conversion is a small but pointed narrative choice: the story could easily have ended with their fear and flight, but instead it turns their terror at witnessing an apparent stump come to life into the direct cause of their own eventual arahantship — the very same event that demonstrates concentration's protective power also becomes the occasion for genuine spiritual transformation in those who witness it.

The Power of Concentration, Continued: Boiling Oil and a Frozen Arrow

Two further examples of the power of concentration, both involving laywomen rather than monastics, close out this fifth kind of power. Uttarā, doused with a pan of boiling oil by a jealous rival, entered loving-kindness in the very instant of the attack, and the scalding oil simply rolled off her body like a drop of water off a lotus leaf. And Sāmāvatī, falsely accused before her own husband, King Udena, of hiding a venomous snake in her lute to assassinate him, pervaded the enraged king with loving-kindness the moment he drew a poison-tipped arrow against her — leaving him standing there, trembling, physically unable either to release the arrow or to lower his bow, until she herself calmly told him to stand down.

Ru-Yi Translation

The laywoman Uttarā was the daughter of the merchant Puṇṇa. A courtesan named Sirimā, overcome by jealousy, poured a pan of boiling oil over her head. Uttarā, in that very instant, entered upon loving-kindness. The oil rolled off her, turning aside like a drop of water off a lotus leaf. This is her power arising from the pervasive influence of concentration. The story should be told in detail.

Sāmāvatī was the chief queen of King Udena. The brahmin Māgaṇḍiya, wishing to secure the position of chief queen for his own daughter, had a venomous snake placed inside her lute, and said to the king, "Great king, Sāmāvatī, wishing to kill you, keeps a venomous snake concealed in her lute." The king, seeing this and enraged, thinking, "I shall kill Sāmāvatī," drew his bow and fitted a poison-tipped arrow. Sāmāvatī, together with her retinue, pervaded the king with loving-kindness. The king, able neither to release the arrow nor to lower it, stood there trembling. Then the queen said to him, "Are you tired, great king?" "Yes, I am tired." "Then lower your bow." The arrow fell right at the king's own feet. Then the queen admonished him: "Great king, one who is not at fault should not be wronged." Thus, the king's inability to release the arrow is the laywoman Sāmāvatī's power arising from the pervasive influence of concentration.

Reading note — Ru-Yi

Both stories share a specific structural feature worth noting: in neither case does the woman act against her attacker at all — Uttarā does not deflect the oil, and Sāmāvatī does not disarm the king. Each simply enters loving-kindness toward the very person threatening her, and the protective effect follows as a byproduct of that state rather than as any targeted counter-measure. The power operates entirely through the quality of the practitioner's own mind, never through any action directed outward.

Sāmāvatī's brief, almost gentle exchange with the king afterward — "are you tired?" followed by a simple instruction to lower the bow — is a striking tonal choice for a scene of attempted murder: rather than triumph, reproach, or fear, the text gives her a composure so complete that she can address her would-be killer's physical exhaustion before addressing the injustice done to her, closing with a single line of moral instruction rather than any accusation.

The Power of the Noble Ones: Seeing Freely, In Any Direction

The sixth power belongs only to arahants who have attained complete mastery over their own minds: the ability to perceive the non-repulsive in what is genuinely repulsive, the repulsive in what is genuinely pleasant, either perception freely in objects that are a mixture of both, and, beyond all of that, a perfect six-factored equanimity that sets both perceptions aside entirely and simply looks on, mindful and fully aware, unmoved in either direction. Where an ordinary meditator can only cultivate loving-kindness toward an unpleasant object or foulness-perception toward an attractive one as a deliberate corrective technique, the arahant endowed with this power can shift between these perceptions — or dispense with the shift altogether — completely at will.

Ru-Yi Translation

The dwelling with the perception of the non-repulsive amid repulsive things, and so on, is called "the power of the noble ones." As it is said: "what is the power of the noble ones? Here, if a monk wishes, 'may I dwell perceiving the non-repulsive in the repulsive,' he dwells perceiving the non-repulsive there … he dwells looking on with equanimity, mindful and fully aware" (Paṭis. iii.17). This is called "the power of the noble ones" precisely because it occurs only among the noble ones who have attained mastery over mind.

For an arahant with taints destroyed, endowed with this power, dwells perceiving the non-repulsive when, toward a repulsive, undesirable object, he practices the pervasion of loving-kindness or attention to the elements. He dwells perceiving the repulsive when, toward a non-repulsive, desirable object, he practices the pervasion of foulness or the attention, "impermanent." Likewise, toward objects that are both repulsive and non-repulsive together, practicing that same pervasion of loving-kindness or attention to the elements, he dwells perceiving the non-repulsive. And toward objects that are both non-repulsive and repulsive together, practicing that same pervasion of foulness or the attention, "impermanent," he dwells perceiving the repulsive. But, carrying out the six-factored equanimity described by the method beginning, "having seen a form with the eye, he is neither glad nor sad" — he dwells, having set aside both the repulsive and the non-repulsive together, looking on with equanimity, mindful and fully aware. For in the Paṭisambhidāmagga this very meaning is analyzed by the method beginning: "how does one dwell perceiving the non-repulsive in the repulsive? Toward an undesirable object, one either pervades it with loving-kindness, or brings it to mind by way of the elements" (Paṭis. iii.17). This is called "the power of the noble ones" precisely because it occurs only among the noble ones who have attained mastery over mind.

Reading note — Ru-Yi

What separates this from an ordinary meditator practicing loving-kindness or foulness-contemplation as a corrective exercise is speed and freedom of direction: the arahant possessing this power does not need to work up to the corrective perception gradually as a beginner would, and, crucially, can move in any of four directions at will — non-repulsive amid the repulsive, repulsive amid the non-repulsive, either freely amid a mixture of both, or beyond both perceptions entirely into six-factored equanimity — rather than being limited to a single fixed technique for a single fixed situation.

Naming this "the power of the noble ones" specifically, rather than simply "an advanced meditative skill," makes an important point about what actually earns the label "power" in this system: it is not raw technical ability so much as the complete freedom from being pushed around by whatever object happens to present itself — the noble one's mind responds to the repulsive and the pleasant exactly as it chooses, never as either object dictates.

Kamma's Free Gift, and Merit's Extravagant Rewards

The seventh and eighth powers require no cultivation whatsoever — they are simply the automatic consequence of what a being already is. Every bird, every deva, the first humans of the aeon, and certain unhappy spirits fly through the air with no jhāna or insight at all: this is the power born of the result of kamma, present from birth. The power of merit belongs instead to those whose accumulated good deeds have ripened to an extraordinary degree — a wheel-turning monarch traveling through the sky with his entire army, and, most memorably, the five members of a single household: the merchant Meṇḍaka, whose upward glance alone fills thousands of storehouses with rain-falling rice; his wife, whose single measure of cooked rice feeds the whole of India without ever running out; his son, whose bag of a thousand coins never empties no matter how much he gives away; his daughter-in-law, whose single basket of grain likewise never diminishes; and his slave, whose single plow cuts fourteen furrows in the time it takes to cut seven.

Ru-Yi Translation

The flying through the air and the like, characteristic of birds and the rest, is called "the power born of the result of kamma." As it is said: "what is the power born of the result of kamma? This is the power born of the result of kamma belonging to all birds, all devas, certain humans, and certain of those in states of loss" (Paṭis. iii.18). For here, all birds fly through the air without any jhāna or insight at all. Likewise all devas, the humans of the first aeon, and certain other humans. Likewise, the flying through the air of the yakkhinī Piyaṅkaramātā (S.N. 1.240), and of Uttaramātā, Phussamittā, Dhammaguttā, and others among certain of those in states of loss, is "the power born of the result of kamma."

The flying through the air and the like, characteristic of wheel-turning monarchs and the like, is called "the power of one possessed of merit." As it is said: "what is the power of one possessed of merit? A wheel-turning king travels through the air together with his four-fold army, down to the very grooms and cowherds. The householder Jotika's is the power of one possessed of merit. The householder Jaṭilaka's is the power of one possessed of merit. The householder Ghosita's is the power of one possessed of merit. The householder Meṇḍaka's is the power of one possessed of merit. The power of the five great meritorious ones is the power of one possessed of merit." In brief, the special success that occurs when a store of merit has come to full ripeness is "the power of one possessed of merit."

Herein, for the householder Jotika, breaking through the earth, a jeweled palace arose, together with sixty-four wishing-trees — this was his power of one possessed of merit. For Jaṭilaka, a golden mountain eighty cubits high arose. For Ghosita, remaining unharmed even when attempts were made to kill him in seven different ways, is his power of one possessed of merit. For Meṇḍaka, the appearance, within an area of a single karīsa, of goats made of the seven kinds of treasure, is his power of one possessed of merit. The five great meritorious ones are: the merchant Meṇḍaka; his wife, Candapadumasirī; his son, the merchant Dhanañcaya; his daughter-in-law, Sumanadevī; and his slave, named Puṇṇa. Among them: when the merchant, having washed his head, looks up at the sky, twelve thousand five hundred storehouses' worth of red rice grains fill up from the sky. When his wife takes merely a single measure of cooked rice and serves the inhabitants of the whole of Jambudīpa, the food is never exhausted. When his son takes a bag of a thousand coins and gives to the inhabitants of the whole of Jambudīpa, the coins are never exhausted. When his daughter-in-law takes a single basket of grain and distributes it to the inhabitants of the whole of Jambudīpa, the grain is never exhausted. When his slave plows with a single plow, "seven furrows this way, seven furrows that way" become fourteen furrows at once. This is their power of one possessed of merit.

Reading note — Ru-Yi

The explicit inclusion of "certain of those in states of loss" among the beings possessing kamma-born flight is a small but notable point of doctrinal even-handedness: this power is not reserved for fortunate rebirths, since even certain suffering spirits, by the specific nature of the kamma that produced their particular unhappy existence, can fly through the air exactly as birds and devas do — a capacity entirely independent of whether the rebirth itself was a good or bad one.

The five-member household is a deliberately egalitarian illustration of merit's power: the same extraordinary abundance extends equally to the wealthy merchant, his wife, his son, his daughter-in-law, and — pointedly — his slave, whose plow accomplishes double the normal work. Merit, unlike social status, is presented here as something any member of a household can independently accumulate and enjoy the fruits of, regardless of rank.

Magical Knowledge, and Success Through Simple Effort — The Ten Complete

The last two of the ten powers close out the list. Holders of spells fly through the air and conjure visions of elephants and marching armies purely through recited magical knowledge — a power distinct from all the others in requiring neither jhāna, nor merit, nor kamma, but only the correct esoteric formula. And the tenth and broadest power is simply success through proper effort in any undertaking at all: the canonical text applies it narrowly to spiritual practice itself, culminating in the abandonment of every defilement through the arahant path, but the commentarial tradition extends it generously to any skillfully executed task whatsoever — a craft, a medical treatment, the mastery of a body of scriptural learning, or even ordinary plowing and sowing done well. With all ten now named, the chapter clarifies precisely which of them the canonical phrase "the various kinds of supernormal power" was actually pointing to at the very start of this whole discussion: strictly, only the first, the power of resolve — though the powers of transformation and the mind-made body are meant to be understood as included as well.

The flying through the air and the like, characteristic of holders of spells and the like, is "the power consisting in magical knowledge." As it is said: "what is the power consisting in magical knowledge? Holders of spells, having recited a spell, go through the air. But the succeeding, through this or that proper application, of this or that task, is "the power that succeeds, in the sense of succeeding, through proper application in this or that particular matter." As it is said: "the abandonment of sensual desire through renunciation succeeds — thus it is the power that succeeds through proper application in this or that particular matter … the abandonment of all defilements through the path of arahantship succeeds — thus it is the power that succeeds through proper application in this or that particular matter" (Paṭis. iii.18). And here, the canonical text has come down showing only proper application reckoned as spiritual practice, in a manner similar to the preceding passage. But in the commentary, it has come down more broadly, such that the special distinction produced by performing this or that task — whether arranging a cart in formation and the like, any craft-work whatsoever, any medical work whatsoever, the learning of the three Vedas, the learning of the three Piṭakas, or even, at the very least, such things as plowing and sowing — is likewise "the power that succeeds, in the sense of succeeding, through proper application in this or that particular matter."

Thus, among these ten kinds of power, in the phrase "the various kinds of supernormal power," it is only the power consisting in resolve that is actually intended. But in the present context, the powers of transformation and of the mind-made body should certainly be included as well.

Reading note — Ru-Yi

The commentarial expansion of the tenth power — from the canonical text's narrow focus on spiritual practice alone to the broader inclusion of any skillfully performed craft, medical treatment, or scriptural mastery — is a significant interpretive move: it effectively declares that genuine competence in any field of human endeavor, achieved through correct and sustained effort, belongs to the very same conceptual category as a monk's progress along the path to arahantship. Both are simply instances of proper application succeeding at its intended task.

The closing clarification — that the canonical phrase which opened this entire discussion technically names only the first of the ten powers, with transformation and the mind-made body included only by extension — is a useful piece of housekeeping before the chapter turns, in its remaining sections, to explaining exactly how these three specific powers are performed in practice. The other seven, having now been fully surveyed, will not be revisited.

Directing the Mind Toward Power: The Framework That Makes Multiplication Possible

Having surveyed all ten kinds of power in the abstract, the chapter now turns to explaining, mechanically, how the power of resolve actually produces its most famous feat — a single monk, wishing to walk about, recite, or answer questions before a large gathering, becoming a hundred or even a thousand. The canonical formula's own verbs are unpacked first: "directs" describes the preparatory mind being pulled away from its kasiṇa-object and pointed toward the goal of supernormal power, while "inclines" describes that mind being made to lean fully toward the specific power about to be attained. The chapter then names the four supporting structures that make this transformation possible at all: four grounds, four bases, eight footings, and sixteen roots — each to be explained individually in the sections that follow, before determination by knowledge completes the process.

Ru-Yi Translation

"The various kinds of supernormal power": "power" here means by way of a category of power, or a division of power. "He directs and inclines his mind": that monk, in the manner already stated, when that mind has become the basis for direct knowledge, directs the preparatory mind toward the accomplishment of supernormal power — removing it from the kasiṇa-object and sending it toward supernormal power. "Inclines" means he makes it lean, makes it slope, toward the power to be attained. "He" is that monk who has thus directed his mind. "Various" means manifold, of different kinds. "Kind of power" means a category of power. "He experiences" means he undergoes, touches, realizes, attains — this is the meaning. Now, showing his manifold state, it is said, beginning, "having been one." Herein, "having been one" means having naturally been one before performing the power. "He becomes many": wishing to walk about, or to recite, or to ask a question in the presence of many people, he becomes even a hundred, even a thousand. How, then, does this come about? By accomplishing the four grounds, the four bases, the eight footings, and the sixteen roots of power, and then determining by knowledge.

Reading note — Ru-Yi

The word-by-word gloss of "directs" and "inclines" is careful to preserve a real distinction between two separate mental movements: the first is essentially a redirection, pulling attention away from the kasiṇa-disk that has served as the object throughout every prior stage of practice, and the second is a positive orientation, actively leaning the freed attention toward the specific power now being sought. Neither step alone would suffice — a mind merely detached from its old object, without also being actively inclined toward the new goal, would simply drift rather than accomplish anything.

The four-part framework about to be explained — grounds, bases, footings, and roots — should not be read as four alternative or competing accounts of the same thing, but as four different analytical lenses on a single underlying process, each drawn from a different canonical source and each emphasizing a different aspect: the grounds locate power within the jhānas themselves, the bases identify its immediate causal factors, the footings distinguish those factors from concentration proper, and the roots specify the qualities that make the resulting concentration genuinely unshakeable.

The Four Grounds: Why the Fourth Jhāna Alone Is Power's True Home

The first of the four frameworks, the "grounds" of power, simply identifies each of the four jhānas by its own dominant quality — seclusion for the first, joy and pleasure for the second, equanimity and pleasure for the third, neither-pain-nor-pleasure for the fourth. But the chapter draws a sharp distinction among these four: only the fourth jhāna is power's genuine, direct ground. The first three merely prepare the way, since their joy and pleasure leave the meditator's body pervaded by a perception of lightness that makes it workable — but the actual accomplishment of power itself belongs to the fourth jhāna alone.

And here, since, by the pervasion of joy and the pervasion of pleasure, the first three jhānas cause the meditator to enter upon a perception of pleasure and a perception of lightness, so that his body becomes light and workable and thereby attains power, these three should be understood, by this manner of speaking, as "grounds of the requisite conditions," inasmuch as they lead to the obtaining of power only in this indirect sense. Reading note — Ru-Yi

The distinction between the first three jhānas as merely "grounds of the requisite conditions" and the fourth as the "true ground" mirrors a pattern already familiar from this treatise's very first chapters on jhāna: joy and pleasure are never treated as ends in themselves, but as intermediate stages whose real function is to leave a residue — here, a perception of bodily lightness — that becomes useful only once it has been carried forward into the calmer, more refined fourth jhāna where power actually arises.

It is worth noting that "lightness" here is not merely metaphorical: the text is quite specific that the actual physical sensation of the body becoming light and workable is the direct causal mechanism by which the earlier jhānas contribute to supernormal power, connecting an abstract meditative attainment to a concrete, felt bodily quality that the meditator can presumably notice directly.

The Four Bases: Desire, Energy, Mind, and Investigation, Each Paired with Striving

The second framework, the four bases of power, names the four possible predominant factors behind the concentration that leads to power — desire, energy, mind, or investigation — each one paired with the same four-fold "volitional formations of striving" (the effort to prevent, abandon, develop, and maintain, familiar from the four right efforts elsewhere in the canon). The chapter offers two complementary etymologies for "base" itself: in one sense, it is the foundation supporting the concentration and its associated mental factors; in another, more literal sense, it is simply "that by which one advances" — the specific means by which the goal of power is actually reached, exactly as a foot carries a person forward step by step.

"The four bases" should be understood as the four bases of power. For this was said: "what are the four bases of power? Here, a monk develops the base of power endowed with concentration due to desire and volitional formations of striving. And here, "concentration due to desire" is concentration whose cause is desire, or in which desire predominates — a designation for the concentration obtained by making the desire to act one's predominant factor. "Volitional formations of striving" are formations of a striving nature — a designation for the right effort of striving that accomplishes the four functions. "Endowed with" means possessed of both concentration due to desire and the volitional formations of striving. "Base of power": whether by the manner of speaking that emphasizes accomplishment, or by the manner that emphasizes succeeding — "by this, beings succeed, become powerful, prosperous, arrived at excellence" — the meaning, by either manner, is: the remaining mass of mind and mental factors, associated with the direct-knowledge consciousness reckoned as "power," is their "base," in the sense of being the foundation for the concentration due to desire and the volitional formations of striving. For this was said: "'base of power' means, for one in that state, the aggregate of feeling … the aggregate of consciousness" (Vibh. 434).

Or alternatively: "that by which one advances" is a "base" — the meaning is, "that by which one arrives." The base of power is "the base of power" — a designation for desire and the rest. As it is said: "monks, if a monk, in dependence on desire, obtains concentration, obtains one-pointedness of mind, this is called 'concentration due to desire.' He strives ... for the sake of the non-arising of unarisen evil states ... these are called 'volitional formations of striving.' Thus this desire, this concentration due to desire, and these volitional formations of striving — this, monks, is called 'the base of power endowed with concentration due to desire and volitional formations of striving'" (S.N. 5.825). The meaning for the remaining bases of power should be understood in the same way.

Reading note — Ru-Yi

The careful distinction between "desire" (chanda) itself and "concentration due to desire" (chandasamādhi) is worth not glossing over: desire alone is not concentration, and concentration alone is not desire — the base of power requires the specific concentration that arises when desire has been made the predominant, organizing factor behind it, a subtly different thing from either desire or concentration considered separately.

The four bases quietly cover the full range of human motivational styles: someone driven primarily by wanting something (desire), by sheer effort and persistence (energy), by focused attention itself (mind), or by analytical curiosity (investigation) can each reach the very same destination, provided that whichever quality dominates is genuinely paired with the same fourfold striving. The path to supernormal power, in other words, does not require any single personality type — only that whatever naturally drives a given individual be properly harnessed.

The Eight Footings: Why Desire Is Never Concentration, Even Though It Produces It

The third framework simply doubles the four bases into eight footings by insisting, for each one in turn, on a precise and easily blurred distinction: desire is not concentration, and concentration is not desire, even though desire, properly relied upon, genuinely produces concentration. The same careful separation is repeated for energy, mind, and investigation. The point is not pedantry for its own sake — it establishes that each of the four qualities functions as a distinct cause working together with, rather than being identical to, the concentration that actually leads to power.

Ru-Yi Translation

"The eight footings" should be understood as desire and the rest, eight in number. For this was said: "what are the eight footings of power? If a monk, in dependence on desire, obtains concentration, obtains one-pointedness of mind — desire is not concentration, concentration is not desire; desire is one thing, concentration another. If a monk, in dependence on energy ... in dependence on mind ... if a monk, in dependence on investigation, obtains concentration, obtains one-pointedness of mind — investigation is not concentration, concentration is not investigation; investigation is one thing, concentration another. These eight footings of power lead to the obtaining of power ... to confidence in power" (Paṭis. iii.9). For here, the desire to produce power, conjoined together with concentration, leads to the obtaining of power; likewise energy and the rest. Reading note — Ru-Yi

The insistence that "desire is one thing, concentration another" prevents a subtle but real conceptual error: it would be easy to assume that sufficiently strong desire simply is concentration, or that concentration is nothing more than desire intensified to a fine point. The text instead treats them as two genuinely distinct phenomena that happen to arise together and function jointly — desire causing concentration to arise without ever becoming concentration itself, exactly as a spark causes a fire without being the fire.

Counting the same four qualities twice — once as "bases" paired with striving, once as "footings" paired with, and distinguished from, concentration — reflects two different analytical purposes rather than genuine redundancy: the bases frame desire and the rest as productive causes of a compound state, while the footings frame them as components requiring careful conceptual separation from the state they help produce.

Sixteen Roots of an Unshakeable Mind

The fourth and final framework, the sixteen roots, catalogues every specific way a mind can be described as genuinely "imperturbable" — ten roots describing a mind free from various structural distortions (not bent down into laziness, not bent up into restlessness, not inclined toward lust, not inclined away into ill will, unattached to views, unbound by desire, liberated from sensual lust, disjoined from the defilements, past the boundary of the defilements, and arrived at unity beyond their manifold variety), and six further roots describing a mind actively guarded by the same six qualities named earlier in the chapter's discussion of the eight-factored fitness for direct knowledge — faith, energy, mindfulness, concentration, wisdom, and luminosity, each specifically shielding against its own particular opposite. The chapter closes this long structural analysis by explaining why the same underlying content appears twice, once in the canonical sutta formula and once here in the Paṭisambhidāmagga's own more detailed breakdown: repetition here serves clarity, not redundancy.

Ru-Yi Translation

"The sixteen roots": the imperturbability of mind should be understood by sixteen aspects. For this was said: "how many roots does power have? Sixteen roots. A mind not bent down does not shake with laziness — this is imperturbability. A mind not bent up does not shake with restlessness — this is imperturbability. A mind not inclined toward does not shake with lust — this is imperturbability. A mind not inclined away does not shake with ill will — this is imperturbability. An unattached mind does not shake with wrong view — this is imperturbability. An unbound mind does not shake with desire and lust — this is imperturbability. A liberated mind does not shake with sensual lust — this is imperturbability. A disjoined mind does not shake with the defilements — this is imperturbability. A mind that has removed its own boundary does not shake at the boundary of the defilements — this is imperturbability. A mind that has arrived at unity does not shake with the manifold defilements — this is imperturbability. A mind grasped by faith does not shake with faithlessness — this is imperturbability. A mind grasped by energy does not shake with laziness — this is imperturbability. A mind grasped by mindfulness does not shake with negligence — this is imperturbability. A mind grasped by concentration does not shake with restlessness — this is imperturbability. A mind grasped by wisdom does not shake with ignorance — this is imperturbability. A mind that has gone to luminosity does not shake with the darkness of ignorance — this is imperturbability. These sixteen roots of power lead to the obtaining of power ... to confidence in power" (Paṭis. iii.9).

Admittedly, this same meaning is already established by the passage beginning, "with mind thus concentrated," and so on; but it is stated again here in order to show that the first jhāna and the rest are the grounds, bases, footings, and roots of power. The former is the method that has come down in the suttas; this present one is that of the Paṭisambhidāmagga. Thus it is stated again in both places for the sake of freedom from confusion.

Reading note — Ru-Yi

The first ten roots and the last six roots operate by two distinct logics worth separating clearly: the first ten describe a mind whose own internal structure has been corrected — no longer bent, inclined, attached, bound, or fragmented by defilement in any of ten specific ways — while the last six describe a mind actively defended from outside, held fast by six positive qualities each standing guard against one specific corrupting counterpart. Structural correction and active defense together, not either alone, constitute genuine imperturbability.

The chapter's own explanation for repeating this same eight-quality content here as "roots," having already given it earlier as the eight-factor description of a mind fit for direct knowledge, is a small but genuinely useful piece of intellectual honesty: rather than pretending the overlap is accidental or hoping the reader won't notice, the text names the two different sources (the suttas and the Paṭisambhidāmagga) explicitly and states that the repetition exists purely to prevent confusion about how the terminology maps across both traditions.

Key vocabulary — for Pāli learners
āneñja

imperturbability, unshakeability — a- (not) + iñjati (to shake, waver); the single quality all sixteen roots are said to establish, each one naming a specific way the mind becomes immune to a particular corrupting influence.

vimariyādikata

having removed its own boundary — vi- (away) + mariyādā (a boundary, limit) + kata (made); one of the ten structural roots, describing a mind that has dissolved the very boundary-line the defilements would otherwise use to demarcate their own territory.

ekattagata

arrived at unity — eka (one) + atta (state, nature) + gata (arrived, gone); describing a mind that has moved beyond the fragmenting multiplicity of the various defilements into a single, unified condition no longer susceptible to their variety.

The Actual Mechanics: Preliminary Work, Re-Entry, Then a Single Determining Moment

With all four supporting frameworks now explained, the chapter finally describes the precise sequence of mental events that actually produces multiplication. Having emerged from the basis-jhāna, the meditator performs preliminary work — simply repeating, "may I become a hundred" — then re-enters that same jhāna, emerges once more, and determines it; and it is at the very moment of that determining-consciousness, not before, that he genuinely becomes a hundred. If this does not succeed at first, the whole cycle may be repeated once or twice more. The three consciousnesses involved each take a different object: the basic jhāna-consciousness rests on the kasiṇa-sign as always, but the preliminary-work and determining consciousnesses both take the actual desired number — a hundred, a thousand — as their object, and take it as a genuine visible form, never as a mere abstract concept.

Ru-Yi Translation

"Determining by knowledge": this monk, having accomplished these states constituting the grounds, bases, and footings of power, having entered the jhāna that serves as the basis for direct knowledge and emerged from it, if he wishes to become a hundred, performs the preliminary work, "may I become a hundred, may I become a hundred" — and then, having entered that same basis-jhāna once more and emerged from it, he determines it; and together with that very determining-consciousness, he becomes a hundred. The preliminary-work consciousnesses have either a hundred or a thousand as their object — and this by way of actual visible form, not by way of mere abstract concept. The determining-consciousness likewise has either a hundred or a thousand as its object in just the same way. That determining-consciousness, like the absorption-consciousness already described, arises as a single moment immediately following the change-of-lineage consciousness, belonging to the fine-material fourth jhāna.

Reading note — Ru-Yi

The specification that the desired multitude is taken "by way of visible form, not by way of mere concept" is a precise and important technical point: the meditator is not merely thinking abstractly about the number a hundred, the way one might silently count, but genuinely holding a hundred actual, visible copies of himself as the direct object of consciousness — the multiplication is a perceptual and meditative event, not an act of arithmetic imagination.

The permission to repeat the whole cycle "once or twice more" if the first attempt does not succeed is a small but humanizing detail in an otherwise highly technical description: even at this most advanced stage of the entire treatise's meditative program, failure on a first attempt is treated as entirely ordinary and expected, warranting simple repetition rather than any deeper crisis of practice.

Cūḷapanthaka, Part One: A Cloth That Would Not Stay Clean

The Paṭisambhidāmagga's formula for becoming many names a specific witness — Venerable Cūḷapanthaka — and the chapter now tells his story in full. Younger brother to an already-arahant elder, Cūḷapanthaka proved unable to memorize even a single verse in four months, and was expelled from the monastery by his own frustrated brother as simply incapable of the teaching. The Buddha found him weeping at the gate, took him by the arm, and gave him something far simpler than verse memorization: a plain piece of cloth to hold and wipe while repeating, "a cloth for wiping, a cloth for wiping." As his hands dirtied the once-clean cloth, Cūḷapanthaka suddenly grasped, on his own, that the fault lay not in the cloth but in his own embodied existence — and from that single, humble insight, developed all the way to full arahantship, complete with every discriminating knowledge and every direct knowledge, in a single sitting.

Ru-Yi Translation

As for what was said, "just as Venerable Cūḷapanthaka did" — this was said in order to point to a living witness to the state of becoming manifold. This should be illustrated by the story itself. It is told that two brothers, having been born on a road, received the name "Panthaka" [Road-born]. The elder of the two, Mahāpanthaka, went forth and attained arahantship together with the discriminating knowledges. Having become an arahant, he had Cūḷapanthaka ordained as well, and gave him this verse —

"Just as a fragrant red lotus might be in bloom at dawn, its scent undiminished — behold the Radiant One, shining forth, blazing like the sun in the sky." (A.N. 5.195)

He was unable to master this verse even in four months. Then the elder, thinking, "you are incapable in this teaching," expelled him from the monastery. At that time, the elder happened also to be the one responsible for assigning meal invitations. Jīvaka, approaching the elder, said, "Venerable sir, tomorrow, please bring the Blessed One together with five hundred monks to receive alms at our house." The Blessed One said, "One who is unable to master a recitation is not, on that account, incapable in my teaching — do not grieve, monk" — and, taking him by the arm, brought him into the monastery, and, by supernormal power, created a piece of cloth and gave it to him, saying, "Come now, monk — wiping this, recite again and again, 'a cloth for wiping, a cloth for wiping.'"

As he did so, the cloth turned black. He gained the perception, "this cloth was pure — there is no fault in it; rather, this is the fault of my own embodied existence" — and, bringing his knowledge down onto the five aggregates, developing insight, he brought it, advancing steadily, right up to the threshold of the change-of-lineage moment. Then the Blessed One spoke to him these verses of radiant light —

"Lust is called 'dust' — yet it is not the dust of the ground; this is a name given to lust itself, 'dust.' Having abandoned this dust, the wise dwell in the teaching of one free of dust.
"Hatred ... [likewise] ....
"Delusion is called 'dust' — yet it is not the dust of the ground; this is a name given to delusion itself, 'dust.' Having abandoned this dust, the wise dwell in the teaching of one free of dust." (Mahāniddesa 209)

At the conclusion of that verse, the nine supramundane states, together with the four discriminating knowledges and the six direct knowledges, came, as it were, right into his hand.

Reading note — Ru-Yi

The Buddha's diagnosis — "one who is unable to master a recitation is not, on that account, incapable in my teaching" — quietly separates two things this story could easily have conflated: verbal memorization and genuine spiritual capacity. Cūḷapanthaka's failure was specific to one particular skill, not a verdict on his fitness for liberation, and the entire remainder of the story exists to demonstrate that a method matched to his actual capacity — a simple physical repetition rather than abstract verse — could reach exactly the same destination his brother's more conventional training had already reached.

The wordplay running through the cloth exercise and the closing verses is worth making explicit: "rajo" means both ordinary physical dust and, by extension, mental defilement. Cūḷapanthaka's concrete observation of dirt genuinely accumulating on cloth becomes, through the Buddha's verses, the exact vehicle for understanding an entirely abstract truth about lust, hatred, and delusion as "dust" of a different kind — a method perfectly calibrated to a mind that had struggled with pure verbal abstraction from the outset.

Cūḷapanthaka, Part Two: A Thousand Identical Monks, and How to Become One Again

The very next day, now a fully accomplished arahant, Cūḷapanthaka demonstrated his new power on a grand scale: while the Buddha withheld his own alms bowl at Jīvaka's house, waiting for the one remaining monk still back at the monastery, Cūḷapanthaka multiplied himself a thousand times over in a nearby mango grove, so that the messenger sent to fetch him found an entire monastery blazing with identical saffron robes and could not tell which figure was the real one — until, following the Buddha's own instruction, he simply grabbed the hem of whichever robe he reached first, at which every duplicate vanished at once. The chapter closes this story with the precise technical mechanics behind it: created duplicates are, by default, exact and unindividuated copies of the original, doing whatever he does and nothing else, unless the meditator deliberately wills variety into them — different ages, different hairstyles, different tasks — and the same basic procedure of preliminary work, re-entry, and determination that produced the multitude also, in reverse, collapses it back into one.

Reading note — Ru-Yi

The default identity of created duplicates — indistinguishable copies that do exactly what the original does, in perfect unison — is a technical detail that repays attention: it means the baseline power of multiplication is, in a sense, the easier case, since deliberate variety (different ages, hairstyles, activities) requires an additional, separate act of preliminary work and determination layered on top of the basic multiplication itself. Sameness is the default; difference must be specifically willed.

Cūḷapanthaka's own motive for wanting to return to being one — a modest, almost bureaucratic concern about drawing unwanted attention to his teacher's reputation rather than his own — is a quietly telling character note appended to what could otherwise read as a purely technical passage: even at the peak of demonstrating a spectacular new power, the story's final beat is about restraint and humility, not display.

Becoming Visible: Splitting Open the Earth Between Two Cities

Having finished with the power of resolve's multiplication feats, the chapter turns to two related marvels: making something visible, and making it invisible. Becoming visible means turning darkness into light, the covered into the open, or the out-of-range into what lies within range — and its first-ever performer, characteristically, was the Buddha himself. Invited to the distant city of Sāketa by the laywoman Cūḷasubhaddā, he arranged for the entire journey there in five hundred created pavilions so that the people of Sāketa and Sāvatthī, seven leagues apart, could see each other directly — and, arriving in the city, he split the earth open down to the Avīci hell and parted the sky up to the Brahmā-world, making the full extent of the cosmos itself visible to the assembled crowd.

Reading note — Ru-Yi

The three paired opposites defining visibility — darkness and light, covered and open, out-of-range and within range — cover three genuinely distinct ways something can fail to be seen: an absence of illumination, a physical obstruction, and simple distance. The Sāketa episode's earth-splitting and sky-parting go well beyond the narrower definition just given — this is not merely removing an obstruction to sight but making visible things that were never meant to be seen at all under ordinary conditions, the very floor of hell and the furthest reach of the Brahmā-world. The Buddha's own use of the power, characteristically, exceeds the baseline case it is used to illustrate.

Three Months Teaching the Gods, and a Journey Bored Straight Through a Mountain

A second, even more elaborate demonstration of the same power unfolds around the Buddha's famous sojourn among the gods. After performing the twin marvel and freeing eighty-four thousand beings from bondage, the Buddha climbed, in two strides, from the earth's surface to the summit of Mount Sineru itself, and spent three uninterrupted months there teaching the Abhidhamma to the assembled deities of ten thousand world-systems — leaving a duplicate Buddha behind to continue the teaching whenever he stepped away for his own daily almsround, gathering food from the Northern Kuru continent and briefing Elder Sāriputta each day on exactly how much Dhamma he had covered. Below on earth, an entire encampment of pilgrims waited twelve leagues wide simply to see him again — sustained the whole time by a single generous young merchant — until Elder Anuruddha located him with the divine eye, and Elder Mahāmoggallāna, diving straight through the solid earth and boring clean through Mount Sineru itself, surfaced at the Buddha's own feet to deliver the pilgrims' request and arrange his eventual return.

The Day the World Opened, and a Fan That Revealed Heaven and Hell

The Buddha's actual descent from Tāvatiṃsa, arranged for the very next day on three staircases of gold, silver, and jewels built to order by the divine architect Vissakamma, produced what tradition calls simply "the opening of the world": standing atop Mount Sineru, the Buddha made every world-system in all directions, and every plane from the lowest hell to the highest heaven, visible at once, so that humans and gods could see each other face to face without so much as tilting their heads. He himself descended the central, jeweled staircase, flanked by the sense-sphere gods on one side and the Brahmās on the other, escorted by Sakka carrying his bowl and robe, a Great Brahmā holding a three-league parasol, and a gandhabba playing music in his honor — a spectacle so overwhelming that not a single witnessing being failed to conceive the wish for future Buddhahood themselves. A much smaller but equally striking echo of the same power appears centuries later on the island of Sri Lanka, where Elder Dhammadinna, mid-sermon, simply turned his hand-fan face down to reveal all the way to Avīci hell, then face up to reveal all the way to the Brahmā-world, terrifying and enticing his listeners by turns until several attained the noble paths on the spot.

Reading note — Ru-Yi

The detail that neither humans nor devas needed to look up or down to see each other — "all saw one another directly, face to face" — is a carefully chosen image of genuine, symmetrical encounter rather than a hierarchical display: on this one extraordinary day, the ordinary cosmic ordering that places heavens above and the human world below is temporarily suspended, and beings from radically different planes of existence simply meet each other as equals in a shared field of vision.

Placing Dhammadinna's fan trick immediately after the Buddha's own cosmic display is a deliberate scaling exercise on the compiler's part: the same fundamental power — making the invisible visible — that once opened the entire universe to view for a single unrepeatable day can also be exercised, centuries later, by an ordinary accomplished elder, using nothing more than a hand-fan, to make an equally vivid and equally effective point to an audience of ordinary listeners.

Becoming Invisible: A Son Hidden From His Own Father

The mirror-image power, becoming invisible, works by exactly the reverse operations: light into darkness, the uncovered into the covered, within-range into out-of-range. Its first performer was again the Buddha, who made the young Yasa invisible to his own searching father though the young man sat right beside him — the very scene that led to Yasa's own eventual awakening. The same technique later let the Buddha hide King Udena's chief minister Mahākappina, together with his thousand ministers, from Queen Anojā and her retinue of a thousand women even as they sat directly nearby, redirecting the queen's attention from "finding the king" toward "finding herself" instead, with the whole assembly reaching the noble paths as a result. And centuries later, Elder Mahinda performed the identical feat on his own arrival in Sri Lanka, concealing his traveling companions from the king who greeted him.

The Buddha's reply to Queen Anojā — "which would be better for you to seek, the king, or yourself?" — turns a straightforward act of concealment into a piece of genuine spiritual instruction: the invisibility is not simply a trick that happens to precede the teaching, but the very device that redirects the queen's attention from an external search (finding her husband) to an internal one (finding herself), setting up exactly the frame of mind needed for the Dhamma that follows.

Placing Mahinda's arrival in Sri Lanka alongside these canonical Indian episodes performs the same function this treatise's stories have performed throughout: connecting the power described in abstract technical terms to a concrete, historically significant moment the reader would already know — in this case, the very founding event of Theravāda Buddhism's establishment on the island where this treatise itself was likely composed.

Two Kinds of Marvel: When the Power Shows Itself, and When Only Its Effect Does

The chapter now draws a further distinction cutting across everything just discussed: an "openly manifest" marvel reveals both the power and the person performing it at once, while a "non-manifest" marvel reveals only the power's effect, leaving its source hidden. The twin marvel — fire from the upper body, water from the lower, visible to everyone at once as the Buddha's own doing — exemplifies the first kind. The second kind is illustrated by two quieter, more mysterious episodes: Elder Mahaka, asked by the householder Citta for a demonstration, retreated indoors and simply caused a flame to burn a bundle of grass placed on the porch, through a keyhole and door-crack, without ever singeing the outer robe placed beside it — visibly a marvel, but with no visible agent performing it. And the Buddha himself once made his own voice audible to an assembly of Brahmās while making his body invisible to them, so that they heard him but never saw who was speaking.

Reading note — Ru-Yi

The distinction between these two categories of marvel is not merely a classification exercise — it reflects a genuine difference in what an observer can actually learn. Watching the twin marvel, Citta's household, and the assembled crowds at Sāketa all knew immediately who was responsible for what they saw; but the grass burning behind a closed door, or a disembodied voice addressing an assembly of Brahmās, leaves the witness with proof that something extraordinary has happened while withholding proof of exactly who or what caused it.

The Buddha's choice to remain invisible while addressing the Brahmās in the Brahmanimantanika Sutta is worth reading in light of that sutta's larger point: the passage occurs in the context of correcting a Brahmā's mistaken belief in his own supreme, unsurpassed status, and the Buddha's decision to be heard but not seen arguably reinforces the lesson itself — the truth he speaks does not depend on, and is not validated by, any visible display of his own superior form.

Walking Through Walls: A Debate Over Why Space, Specifically

The chapter now turns to a sequence of vivid physical feats, beginning with passing unobstructed through solid walls, ramparts, and even mountains as easily as through open air. The method requires the space kasiṇa specifically: the meditator adverts to the obstruction, determines "let this become space," and it genuinely becomes space — hollow for one wishing to go below or above, an opening for one wishing to pass straight through. This prompts a genuine scholarly disagreement recorded in the text itself: the learned Elder Tipiṭaka Cūḷābhaya argued that the space kasiṇa is unnecessary — since an elephant is not conjured by entering an "elephant kasiṇa," mastery of any of the eight kasiṇa attainments alone should suffice for anything one wishes. His fellow monks overruled him only on textual grounds: the canonical formula itself specifically names the space kasiṇa, and so that wording must be followed regardless of the logical point.

"He goes unobstructed through walls, through ramparts, through mountains, just as though through space": herein, "through a wall" means through another's wall, that is, through the far side of a wall. "Mountain" is either a mountain of earth or a mountain of rock. "Unobstructed" means without catching, without sticking. "Just as though through space" means as though through open space. One wishing to travel in this way should, having attained the space kasiṇa and emerged from it, advert to a wall, a rampart, or a mountain — even Sineru or the mountains ringing the world-system — and, having performed the preliminary work, determine, "let this become space." It becomes space itself. For one wishing to descend below, or to ascend above, it becomes hollow; for one wishing to pass straight through, it becomes an opening. He goes there unobstructed.

But the Elder Tipiṭaka Cūḷābhaya said on this point: "friends, what is the purpose of entering upon the space kasiṇa? Does one wishing to create an elephant or a horse and the like enter upon an elephant-kasiṇa or a horse-kasiṇa? Is it not simply this: having done the preliminary work in whatever kasiṇa one likes, the only actual requirement is mastery over the eight attainments — and then, whatever one wishes for, that very thing comes about?" The monks replied: "venerable sir, it is specifically the space kasiṇa that has come down in the canonical text; therefore this must certainly be stated just as it is." Here is that canonical text —

"Being naturally a gainer of the space-kasiṇa attainment, he adverts to a wall, a rampart, a mountain. Having adverted, he determines by knowledge, 'let this become space.' It becomes space. He goes unobstructed through the wall, the rampart, the mountain. Just as ordinary people, without power, go unobstructed through what is open and unenclosed by anything — just so, this possessor of power, who has attained mastery of mind, goes unobstructed through the wall, the rampart, the mountain, as though through space." (Paṭis. iii.11)

If, for that monk, while going after having so determined, another mountain or tree should arise in between, should he enter the attainment and determine it again? There is no fault in doing so. For entering again and determining anew is comparable to taking dependence anew in the presence of one's preceptor. And, because this monk has determined, "let this become space," it does indeed become space. And, by the very power of his earlier determination, it is simply not possible that another mountain or tree should naturally arise in between. But in the case of something created by another possessor of power, the first creation is the stronger; the other must then go around it, either above or below.

Reading note — Ru-Yi

The exchange between Cūḷābhaya and his fellow monks is a rare, explicit moment of methodological disagreement preserved rather than smoothed over: Cūḷābhaya's logical objection is never actually refuted on its own terms — nobody explains why the space kasiṇa should be specially necessary if mastery alone suffices elsewhere — and the matter is settled purely by appeal to the canonical text's own specific wording. The treatise records the disagreement honestly rather than manufacturing a tidy resolution.

The rule about competing determinations — a naturally arising obstacle cannot appear within an already-determined space, but another meditator's independently created obstacle can, with the earlier creation simply taking precedence — quietly establishes something significant about how this power interacts with other agents' own exercise of the same power: one master's determination is absolute against ordinary reality, but not against a rival master's equally valid determination, which must instead be navigated around like any other real obstacle.

Diving Through Solid Earth as Though It Were Water

The next feat reverses the usual relationship between earth and water: using the water kasiṇa this time, the meditator delimits a specific area of ground, determines "let this be water," and genuinely dives in and out of the earth there exactly as an ordinary swimmer would in a pool. The power extends well beyond mere diving — bathing, drinking, washing clothes, even drawing out ghee, oil, honey, or molasses that remain genuinely ghee, oil, honey, or molasses once removed into a container. And the transformation is entirely private to the meditator unless he deliberately extends it: for everyone else, the ground remains solid ground the whole time, people continuing to walk, ride, and even farm across the very same delimited spot — unless he specifically wills that it become water for them as well, and it reverts to ordinary earth automatically once the determined time period expires.

The insistence that ghee stays ghee, oil stays oil, and water stays water once drawn out into a container is a small but philosophically important safeguard: it confirms that this power transforms genuine substance, not mere appearance — the meditator is not producing an illusion that would dissolve on contact, but actually altering the earth's underlying nature within the delimited area, in a way that persists and behaves exactly as the substance it has become.

The default privacy of the effect — the same ground being solid earth for everyone else even while it is genuine water for the meditator alone — reveals something important about how these powers are meant to interact with the ordinary world: nothing here is imposed on unwitting bystanders by default. Extending the transformation to others requires a separate, deliberate act of will, treating other people's experience of reality as something the meditator must specifically choose to affect, not something automatically swept along by his own attainment.

Walking on Water: The Mirror Image, Using the Earth Kasiṇa

The feat now runs in the opposite direction from the previous one: using the earth kasiṇa this time, the meditator delimits an area of water, determines "let this be earth," and walks — or stands, sits, or lies down — upon it exactly as on solid ground, the water simply refusing to break beneath him. The same private, reversible, and extendable qualities apply as before: fish and water-birds continue swimming through the very same patch as ordinary water, the meditator alone experiencing it as ground, unless he specifically wills the transformation to be shared, and the effect lapses back into ordinary water once the fixed time period runs out. The power is not limited to producing earth either — a jeweled mountain, a golden mountain, or a tree can be conjured in its place by exactly the same procedure.

Flying Cross-Legged Like a Bird — And Why the Divine Eye Is Not Optional

Flight itself, the chapter now explains, is simply another application of the earth kasiṇa: rather than transforming an already-solid surface, the meditator delimits a patch of open space exactly the size of a cross-legged seat, a bed, or a walking path — whichever posture he intends to travel in — and determines it to be earth, producing solid support wherever he wills it through the sky. But an important safety requirement is added that the previous feats never mentioned: anyone traveling by air must also possess the divine eye, since unseen mountains, trees, or jealous nāgas and garuḍas creating obstacles in mid-flight pose a real danger that only advanced perception can spot in time. Another elder is recorded objecting that a mind already this concentrated should not need to formally re-enter jhāna at all before each determination — but the text insists on following the same careful, step-by-step procedure regardless. The divine eye also serves a second, more modest purpose: scouting a suitably discreet landing spot, so the meditator does not descend conspicuously at a public bathing-place or village gate and draw unwanted attention.

Touching the Sun and Moon — No Kasiṇa Required, and a Debate About Growing Hands

The last of this cluster of physical feats requires no kasiṇa attainment at all — only the basis-jhāna for direct knowledge itself is needed, since the sun and moon, despite orbiting forty-two thousand leagues above and illuminating three continents at once, are simply brought within hand's reach by determination alone, exactly like any nearby object. The meditator can either travel to them directly or, remaining seated or lying down, simply will them to approach — at which point they come, "like a palm-fruit released from its stalk," or else his own hand stretches out to meet them. This last detail sparks a technical debate recorded in the text: when a limb stretches to reach something distant, does the meditator's actual living tissue elongate, or only the non-living material dependent on it? One elder pointedly objects, citing Mahāmoggallāna's own well-attested ability to shrink small enough to pass through a keyhole and grow large again at will, that living tissue itself evidently can do both.

The Taming of Nandopananda, Part One: A Nāga King's Wrath

The chapter now sets aside its technical explanations for a single, extended narrative: the taming of the nāga king Nandopananda. Having accepted an invitation to alms at Anāthapiṇḍika's house, the Buddha spent the night surveying the ten-thousand-fold world-system with his knowledge, and saw a wrong-viewed, faithless nāga king named Nandopananda come into range — along with the one person capable of weaning him off that wrong view, Elder Mahāmoggallāna. The next morning, rather than going directly to the meal, the Buddha announced a "excursion among the gods" and led five hundred monks flying directly over the top of Nandopananda's own lavish drinking-party canopy, deliberately making sure the nāga king would see them passing overhead. Enraged at what he took as an insult — ascetics scattering the dust of their feet over his head on their way to and from the heaven of the gods — Nandopananda abandoned his human-like form, coiled seven times around Mount Sineru itself, and spread his hood over the summit, plunging the entire Tāvatiṃsa heaven into darkness and rendering it invisible to the monks below.

Reading note — Ru-Yi

The Buddha's careful two-step reflection — first noticing that Nandopananda has come within the range of his knowledge at all, then specifically checking whether he has the "supporting condition" for conversion — models a discipline this treatise has shown before in other stories: even omniscient perception is not treated as automatically actionable; the Buddha deliberately investigates whether intervention is warranted before choosing to act, and only then identifies the specific person, Moggallāna, suited to the specific task.

Nandopananda's grievance is worth taking seriously on its own terms rather than dismissing as mere arrogance: from his perspective, the monks really were flying directly over his home and, by the logic of ritual purity that governs so much of this cosmology, genuinely defiling him and his court by passing dust-covered feet above their heads. The story does not deny that a real breach of decorum occurred — it simply shows that his violent response to it was disproportionate, and that his underlying wrong view made him unable to receive the Triple Gem's protection that might otherwise have tempered his reaction.

The Taming of Nandopananda, Part Two: Smoke, Fire, and a Walk Through a Belly

Only when every other monk had volunteered and been refused did Mahāmoggallāna offer himself — and the Buddha immediately granted permission. What followed was an escalating contest fought entirely through transformation: Moggallāna matched the nāga's own enormous coiled form, pressed him against Sineru until both were smoking and then both blazing, entered his body through first one ear and out the other, then one nostril and out the other, and finally walked back and forth inside his belly after the nāga opened his mouth in a failed attempt to trap him between his fangs. The Buddha himself, watching from outside, warned Moggallāna that this was an unusually powerful nāga — to which the elder calmly replied that his four bases of power were so thoroughly perfected he could tame a hundred thousand such nāgas. When the defeated Nandopananda finally released a blast of poisonous breath in one last attempt to harm him, Moggallāna simply entered the fourth jhāna, and the wind could not so much as stir a single pore of his skin — at which point, taking the form of a garuḍa, the nāga's own natural predator, he pursued the terrified serpent to complete surrender.

The escalating duel — matching form for form, smoke for smoke, fire for fire, before Moggallāna finally simply enters the nāga's own body — traces a deliberate progression from confrontation to something closer to complete, almost intimate mastery: by the time Moggallāna is walking calmly back and forth inside the nāga's belly, the contest has already effectively ended; everything that follows is merely the nāga's own failing attempts to salvage some measure of the fight.

The Buddha's decision to withhold permission from every other volunteer until Moggallāna alone stepped forward directly illustrates this same chapter's earlier claim that "quick-wittedness" — the ability to enter the necessary attainment fast enough, under real pressure, to survive an actual crisis — is the rarest of all the qualities this whole path requires; the text states plainly that the other monks might have managed every step up to the poison-breath attack, but could not have reacted in time at the one moment that actually mattered.

Exercising Mastery With the Body, All the Way to the Brahmā-World

Returning from the Nandopananda narrative to systematic explanation, the chapter now addresses the final and most far-reaching application of the power of resolve: reaching the Brahmā-world itself and exercising bodily mastery there. The canonical formula lays out a complete toolkit of reciprocal transformations — far made near, near made far, much made little, little made much — before turning to the two distinct ways of actually traveling there: with a visible body, more slowly, by first bringing the mind under the body's influence, or with an invisible body, more swiftly, by instead bringing the body under the mind's influence. Both methods pass through the same threshold experience, entering a perception of pleasure and of lightness that leaves the traveling body as buoyant as a wisp of cotton-down caught on the wind. Only once there, creating a mind-made duplicate before the Brahmā to walk, stand, speak, and even hold conversation on the meditator's behalf, does the formula consider bodily mastery to be genuinely achieved.

Reading note — Ru-Yi

The four reciprocal transformations opening this passage — far into near, near into far, much into little, little into much — establish a general principle before the specific case of Brahmā-world travel is even addressed: this power operates on the very categories of distance and quantity themselves, treating them as malleable relationships rather than fixed facts, and everything that follows (the journey itself, the mind-made duplicate) is simply a specific application of this same underlying flexibility.

The distinction between "bringing mind under body's influence" for visible travel and "bringing body under mind's influence" for invisible travel captures something genuinely subtle: visible travel is deliberately the slower, more physically grounded mode, since a body that remains visible to onlookers must still behave in a manner recognizably continuous with an ordinary body moving through space, while invisible travel, unconstrained by any need to look physically plausible to a witness, can simply be as fast as thought itself.

Making the Far Near: A Shortened Road, and a City Brought Close

The chapter now traces who first performed each of the four reciprocal transformations, grounding the abstract formula in specific, named precedent. The Buddha himself made the far near when he stepped from earth's surface directly onto the summit of Mount Sineru, treating the intervening Mount Yugandhara as a single stepping-stone. Mahāmoggallāna did the same when he compressed an entire thirty-league road so that a twelve-league procession of pilgrims arrived instantly. Centuries later in Sri Lanka, Elder Cūḷasamudda, needing to find almsfood for seven hundred monks during a famine, shortened the very earth itself to walk his community from Sri Lanka to distant Pāṭaliputta in a single stride — casually correcting his startled companions' sense of both distance and scale along the way — while Elder Tissadatta performed the identical feat merely to pay an evening visit to the Bodhi Tree. And the reverse transformation, making the near far, belongs to the Buddha alone: he deliberately lengthened the short distance between himself and the murderous Aṅgulimāla, keeping the bandit forever just out of reach as he pursued him on foot.

Reading note — Ru-Yi

Cūḷasamudda's brief, almost deadpan exchange with his startled monks — "senior elders bring even what is far and make it near," and, moments later, "senior elders make even what is huge into something small" — treats an event of genuinely cosmic scale (crossing to a distant city, dismissing the ocean's vastness) with a striking matter-of-factness. The comedy of the scene depends entirely on the monks' ordinary astonishment being met with a teacher's calm, almost bureaucratic explanation, exactly the same tone found throughout this chapter's other stories of masters performing extraordinary feats as routine business.

The single, spare sentence given to the Buddha's use of the opposite transformation — lengthening, rather than shortening, the distance to Aṅgulimāla — carries an entire well-known story in miniature: the murderer, despite running at full speed, could never catch up to the walking Buddha, precisely because the Buddha kept the gap between them artificially wide, a detail whose deeper point (about the futility of pursuing salvation through force rather than sincere request) the text leaves entirely unstated, trusting the reader to already know it.

Making Much Into Little, Little Into Much — and Sour Into Sweet

The commentary now supplies precedent for the remaining pair of reciprocal transformations, much made little and little made much, before noting that the same power extends further still, to any quality a meditator wishes to alter. Elder Mahākassapa compressed five hundred girls' entire offering of moon-cakes into the volume of a single bowl. Elder Mahāmoggallāna, in the well-known story of the miser Illisa, performed the opposite feat, expanding a bare handful of food to satisfy an entire assembly. The Buddha himself did the same for the destitute Kākavaliya, multiplying one bowl of thin, sourish gruel to feed the whole monks' order and thereby setting in motion the very merit that would make Kākavaliya a wealthy man's heir within a week. And Elder Mahā-Anuḷa went further yet, showing that the power is not limited to quantity at all: he simply willed plain river water to become sweet ghee-cream for a group of monks who had managed to beg only dry, unappetizing rice.

Cotton-Down on the Wind: What "Bodily Mastery" Actually Requires

Having supplied precedent for every clause of the canonical formula, the commentary now works through it phrase by phrase — clarifying that seeing, hearing, and reading a distant Brahmā's mind require no travel at all, simply an expansion of one's own divine faculties from where one already sits. It then distinguishes the two travel methods with unusual technical precision: bringing mind under body's influence yields the slower, "body-following" gait of ordinary walking or flight along a self-created path; bringing body under mind's influence yields a swiftness limited only by thought itself, arriving in the very instant of the determining mind, whether at its arising, its persisting, or its passing away. Both methods pass through the same doorway, a perception of pleasure and lightness that leaves the body genuinely as buoyant as cotton-down flung on the wind. The passage closes with the commentary's own final, precise ruling on what actually counts as "bodily mastery" in the Brahmā-world: not the divine-eye seeing, not the divine-ear hearing, not even standing and conversing with the Brahmā from where one already sits — only the actual creation of the mind-made duplicate, standing before the Brahmā and acting in every particular exactly as the meditator himself acts, satisfies the formula's full demand.

Reading note — Ru-Yi

The commentary's closing ruling is a striking act of definitional narrowing: after cataloguing an entire sequence of extraordinary feats — seeing across worlds, hearing across worlds, reading minds at a distance, even conversing with a Brahmā without leaving one's seat — it declares that none of this actually counts as "bodily mastery." Only the single, final act of manifesting a mind-made duplicate that walks, stands, and speaks exactly as the meditator does satisfies the formula. Everything else, however impressive, is filed under "preliminary portion" (pubbabhāga) — a reminder that this text consistently defines a power by its most demanding, most precisely specified instance, not by any of its more easily achieved approximations.

The three-moment analysis of when exactly the invisible-body traveler arrives — at the arising, persisting, or dissolving of the determining thought-moment — pushes Abhidhamma's already fine-grained analysis of consciousness to a genuinely strange edge case, and the elder's answer ("all three") refuses to resolve the strangeness by picking just one; travel by mind, it turns out, cannot be pinned to a single instant of ordinary temporal experience at all.

The Power of Transformation: Becoming Child, Nāga, Lion, or Army

Paragraph 398 turns to the second of the ten kinds of iddhi listed earlier in the chapter, the power of transformation, and immediately distinguishes it from the mind-made-body power still to come: transformation means actually setting aside one's own ordinary appearance and displaying another form entirely — a child, a nāga, a garuḍa, an asura, a deva, a Brahmā, the ocean, a mountain, a lion, a tiger, a leopard, or the outward show of elephants, horses, chariots, foot-soldiers, and whole battle-formations. The method mirrors the power of resolve exactly: emerging from the kasiṇa-based basis-jhāna, adverting to the desired form, performing the preliminary work, then re-entering and determining, "let me be such a one" — whereupon the transformation occurs together with the determining thought itself. A single, understated reference names Devadatta as an example of this very power turned to harmful ends, a story the reader is assumed already to know in full from the Cūḷavagga.

Reading note — Ru-Yi

The Devadatta reference is deliberately terse, a single subordinate clause ("just as Devadatta did") pointing to a story every reader of the Cūḷavagga would already know in full: Devadatta's use of this very power to transform himself into a child seated on Ajātasattu's lap, terrifying the prince into loyalty, as the opening move in his campaign against the Buddha. The commentary's restraint here is notable — it cites the case purely as a technical illustration of the mechanism, without pausing to moralize on its famously disastrous outcome, trusting the reader to supply that context unprompted.

The distinction drawn between transforming oneself ("let me be a child") and manifesting something external to oneself ("let there be an elephant") is a small but important clarification: the same underlying power branches into two grammatically and functionally distinct determinations, one reflexive and one not, depending on whether the meditator's own identity or a wholly separate object is being called into being.

The Mind-Made Body: Reed From Muñja-Grass, Sword From Scabbard

The chapter's final technical topic, paragraph 399, explains the power of the mind-made body through three linked similes drawn directly from the Paṭisambhidāmagga. The meditator first determines his own body to become hollow, then, within that hollow body, determines a second body to arise — and draws it out exactly as one draws a reed from a stalk of muñja-grass, a sword from its scabbard, or a snake from its slough: two things that appeared identical and inseparable, revealed to have been genuinely distinct all along. The commentary's own closing note on these similes is precise about their purpose — they are meant to show only that the mind-made form resembles the meditator himself, not that a literal husk is discarded in producing it. With this power explained, the treatise reaches its formal close: the traditional colophon marking the twelfth chapter, "Discussion of the Kinds of Supernormal Power," complete.

Ru-Yi Translation

But one wishing to make a mind-made body, having emerged from the basis-jhāna, first adverts to his body and determines, in the way already stated, "let it become hollow" — and it becomes hollow. Then, having adverted to another body within it, and having done the preliminary work, he determines in the way already stated, "let there be, within that one, another body." He draws that one out just as one draws a reed from muñja-grass, a sword from its scabbard, or a snake from its slough. Hence it was said: "Here, a monk creates, from this body, another body, possessing form, mind-made, complete with all its major and minor limbs, its faculties not deficient. It is just as if a man were to draw out a reed from muñja-grass; it would occur to him: 'this is the muñja-grass, this is the reed — the muñja-grass is one thing, the reed is another thing, but the reed was drawn out from the muñja-grass'" and so on (Paṭis. iii.14). And here, just as the reed and the other examples are similar in kind to the muñja-grass and the rest, so too the mind-made form is simply similar in kind to the possessor of power himself — these similes are stated for the purpose of showing just this. This is the power of the mind-made body.

Reading note — Ru-Yi

All three similes — reed from muñja-grass, sword from scabbard, snake from slough — share a single precise logical structure worth noticing: in each case, two things that appeared to be one continuous thing are shown, by the act of separation itself, to have always been genuinely two things wearing the shape of one. The commentary's closing clarification is important precisely because it heads off a natural misreading: nothing is being discarded or shed the way a scabbard is set aside once the sword is drawn. The point of the simile is resemblance in form between original and created body, not the mechanics of extraction.

The chapter's closing colophon, naming this the twelfth chapter, marks the treatise's own internal transition into what its source manuscript calls the "Second Part" (Dutiyo bhāgo) — even as, within the treatise's own three-fold structure of virtue, concentration, and wisdom, this chapter and the next belong to the concluding portion of the concentration division, immediately preceding the turn toward wisdom that begins in earnest a few chapters later.

Iddhividhaniddesa Complete

With the power of the mind-made body explained, the twelfth chapter of the Visuddhimagga comes to its close — the opening chapter of the source manuscript's own "second part" (Dutiyo bhāgo), even as it continues, within the treatise's own three-fold sīla–samādhi–paññā structure, straight on from the concentration division that chapter eleven completed. Beginning from the etymology of "iddhi" itself and the sober warning that mastery of these powers is rare even among those who possess all the necessary jhāna attainments, the chapter moved through the sole detailed treatment of the power of resolve — its four grounds, four bases, eight footings, and sixteen roots of imperturbability — into a long sequence of concrete demonstrations: Cūḷapanthaka's thousand-fold multiplication, the descent from Tāvatiṃsa with its triple staircase, the taming of the nāga king Nandopananda, and finally the exercises of passing through solid matter, diving through earth, walking on water, flying, and touching the sun and moon. It closed by tracing the more restrained but equally precise powers of transformation and mind-made duplication, before their final application in reaching, and moving with mastery through, the Brahmā-world itself.

The next chapter, the Abhiññāniddesa ("Discussion of the Direct Knowledges"), verified from the Visuddhimagga's own table of contents, opens the treatise's second volume by returning to the remaining five of the six direct knowledges introduced at this chapter's outset — the divine ear, penetration of others' minds, recollection of past lives, the divine eye, and the destruction of the taints — each of which iddhividha, the supernormal power just completed, was itself only the first and most elaborately treated member.
