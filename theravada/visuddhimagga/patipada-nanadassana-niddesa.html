Chapter 21 · Paṭipadāñāṇadassanavisuddhiniddesa · Complete

The Nine Knowledges of the Way

This page carries Ru-Yi's translation of the Visuddhimagga's chapter 21, Paṭipadāñāṇadassanavisuddhiniddesa ("The Description of Purification by Knowledge and Vision of the Way") — the fourth of the five "body" purifications, and the treatise's classic, systematic account of insight's own maturing sequence. Beginning where chapter twenty left off, with knowledge of rise and fall now freed of its ten corruptions, it walks through dissolution, fear, danger, disenchantment, desire for deliverance, reflection, and — at greatest length — equanimity toward formations, before closing with conformity-knowledge and a final accounting of insight leading to emergence. Few stretches of the Visuddhimagga are richer in image and story: the woman who releases her youngest son to the executioner's blade, the villager mistaking a snake for a fish, the wife no longer loved, the crow released at sea in search of land, and six more narrative similes — the bat, the black snake, the burning house, the stolen royal cattle, the man and the ogress, the mother and the wrong child — each mapping the entire path onto an unforgettable image.

Purification by Knowledge and Vision of the Way Defined; Why Redo Knowledge of Rise and Fall

The twenty-first chapter opens by naming its own subject: insight that has reached its culmination by way of eight successive knowledges, together with a ninth — conformity-knowledge. Before working through these nine in turn, Buddhaghosa first explains why the meditator must return to knowledge of rise and fall, the very knowledge chapter twenty ended with: only once genuinely freed of the ten corruptions can it discern the three characteristics according to their true nature.

Pali Source

Aṭṭhannaṃ pana ñāṇānaṃ vasena sikhāppattā vipassanā, navamañca saccānulomikañāṇanti ayaṃ "paṭipadāñāṇadassanavisuddhi" nāma. Aṭṭhannanti cettha upakkilesavimuttaṃ vīthipaṭipannavipassanāsaṅkhātaṃ udayabbayānupassanāñāṇaṃ, bhaṅgānupassanāñāṇaṃ, bhayatupaṭṭhānañāṇaṃ, ādīnavānupassanāñāṇaṃ, nibbidānupassanāñāṇaṃ, muñcitukamyatāñāṇaṃ, paṭisaṅkhānupassanāñāṇaṃ, saṅkhārupekkhāñāṇanti imāni aṭṭha ñāṇāni veditabbāni. Navamaṃ saccānulomikañāṇanti anulomassetaṃ adhivacanaṃ. Tasmā taṃ sampādetukāmena upakkilesavimuttaṃ udayabbayañāṇaṃ ādiṃ katvā etesu ñāṇesu yogo karaṇīyo.

Puna udayabbayañāṇe yogo kimatthiyoti ce? Lakkhaṇasallakkhaṇattho. Udayabbayañāṇaṃ hi heṭṭhā dasahi upakkilesehi upakkiliṭṭhaṃ hutvā yāthāvasarasato tilakkhaṇaṃ sallakkhetuṃ nāsakkhi. Upakkilesavimuttaṃ pana sakkoti. Tasmā lakkhaṇasallakkhaṇatthamettha puna yogo karaṇīyo.

Ru-Yi Translation

Insight that has reached its culmination by way of eight knowledges, together with a ninth — knowledge conforming to the truths — this is called "purification by knowledge and vision of the way." Herein, "by way of eight" should be understood as these eight knowledges: knowledge of the contemplation of rise and fall, freed of corruption and reckoned as insight proceeding along its own course; knowledge of the contemplation of dissolution; knowledge of the appearance as fear; knowledge of the contemplation of danger; knowledge of the contemplation of disenchantment; knowledge of desire for deliverance; knowledge of the contemplation of reflection; and knowledge of equanimity toward formations. "The ninth, knowledge conforming to the truths" is a designation for conformity-knowledge. Therefore, one desiring to accomplish this should make the effort in these knowledges, beginning with knowledge of rise and fall freed of corruption.

If it is asked, what is the purpose of the further effort in knowledge of rise and fall? — it is for the sake of discerning the characteristics. For knowledge of rise and fall, earlier, being corrupted by the ten corruptions, was unable to discern the three characteristics according to their own true nature; but freed of corruption, it is able to do so. Therefore, the further effort here should be made for the sake of discerning the characteristics.

Reading note — Ru-Yi

The nine knowledges named here are not nine unrelated techniques but a single continuous deepening of the same act of attention, each one growing directly out of what came before — a structure this chapter's own closing section will describe as many different names for what is, at bottom, one process of "insight leading to emergence." The careful qualifier "freed of corruption and reckoned as insight proceeding along its own course," repeated at both the start of this chapter and the close of the last, marks the precise handoff point between the two chapters: chapter twenty ended by teaching the meditator to recognize and set aside illumination, rapture, and the other nine corruptions; this chapter begins by putting that now-purified knowledge back to work.

The reason offered for redoing knowledge of rise and fall is worth taking seriously on its own terms: it is not that the earlier attainment was false, but that it was incomplete, its view of the three characteristics obstructed by the very corruptions that made it feel so convincing. Only once those obstructions are cleared away can the same knowledge see clearly what it was, in a sense, already looking at all along.

lakkhaṇasallakkhaṇa

"the discerning of characteristics" — lakkhaṇa (characteristic) + sallakkhaṇa (discerning, marking out); the specific purpose named for redoing knowledge of rise and fall, distinguishing this purified round of practice from the same knowledge's earlier, corruption-obstructed form.

Why the Three Characteristics Are Hidden, and How They Become Manifest

A precise diagnostic follows: each of the three characteristics is obscured by a specific, ordinary feature of experience — impermanence by the seeming continuity of a stream of events, suffering by the postures that constantly relieve one discomfort by shifting into another, not-self by the apparent compactness of what is actually many distinct elements. Only once continuity is disrupted, the postures exposed, and compactness broken apart do the three characteristics finally appear as they truly are.

Ru-Yi Translation

But through failure to attend to what, and through being concealed by what, do the characteristics fail to appear? The characteristic of impermanence, first, fails to appear through failure to attend to rise and fall, being concealed by continuity. The characteristic of suffering fails to appear through failure to attend to continual affliction, being concealed by the postures. The characteristic of not-self fails to appear through failure to attend to the separating-out of the various elements, being concealed by compactness. But when rise and fall have been grasped and continuity has been disrupted, the characteristic of impermanence appears according to its true nature. When continual affliction has been attended to and the postures have been exposed, the characteristic of suffering appears according to its true nature. When the various elements have been separated out and compactness has been broken up, the characteristic of not-self appears according to its true nature.

Herein, the following distinction should be understood: impermanence and the characteristic of impermanence; suffering and the characteristic of suffering; not-self and the characteristic of not-self. Herein, "impermanent" is the fivefold aggregates. Why? Because of their arising, passing away, and alteration, or because of their ceasing to exist after having been. Their arising, passing away, and alteration is the characteristic of impermanence — or else, the mode of alteration reckoned as their ceasing to exist after having been.

But, from the statement "what is impermanent is suffering," that same fivefold aggregates is suffering. Why? Because of continual affliction. The mode of continual affliction is the characteristic of suffering.

And from the statement "what is suffering is not-self," that same fivefold aggregates is not-self. Why? Because of not submitting to one's will. The mode of not submitting to one's will is the characteristic of not-self.

All of this the meditator discerns according to its true nature, by means of the knowledge of the contemplation of rise and fall, freed of corruption and reckoned as insight proceeding along its own course.

Knowledge of rise and fall freed of corruption is finished.

Reading note — Ru-Yi

This is one of the most practically useful diagnostic passages in the whole treatise, since it names exactly what a meditator must do to make each characteristic manifest rather than merely inferred: disrupt continuity to see impermanence (which is precisely what chapter twenty's entire elaborate progression of ever-finer temporal division accomplished), attend to the postures' constant covert relief of discomfort to see suffering (since ordinary shifting from sitting to standing to lying down continuously masks how uncomfortable each single posture actually becomes if held too long), and separate out the distinct elements to see not-self (undoing the perceptual "compactness" — ghana — that makes a body feel like one thing rather than many).

The distinction drawn between "impermanence" and "the characteristic of impermanence" is a small but important piece of technical precision: the aggregates themselves are what is impermanent, while their arising-passing-alteration (or, in the alternative formula, their ceasing-to-exist-after-having-been) is the characteristic — the specific feature by which that impermanence becomes knowable. The same distinction, mechanically applied, yields suffering's characteristic (continual affliction) from suffering itself, and not-self's characteristic (not submitting to one's will) from not-self itself — three parallel derivations built on the classic scriptural chain "what is impermanent is suffering; what is suffering is not-self."

Key vocabulary — for Pāli learners
santati / iriyāpatha / ghana

continuity / posture / compactness — the three ordinary features of experience identified here as concealing impermanence, suffering, and not-self respectively; disrupting, exposing, and breaking apart each of these three is what this and the following chapters' methods are, in large part, designed to accomplish.

uppādavayaññathatta

"arising, passing away, and alteration" — uppāda (arising) + vaya (passing away) + aññathatta (otherwiseness, change); the compound naming the characteristic of impermanence directly, alongside the alternative formula hutvā abhāva, "ceasing to exist after having been."

Knowledge of Dissolution: The Paṭisambhidāmagga's Definition

As the purified knowledge of rise and fall grows sharper still, it stops reaching all the way to a state's arising, persisting, or sign at all — attention now rests on nothing but destruction, passing away, and cessation. This marks the arising of a new knowledge, contemplation of dissolution, defined here through a full Paṭisambhidāmagga citation: consciousness that has taken some object arises and breaks up, and a further act of reflection contemplates that very breaking-up — not as permanent, pleasant, or self, but as impermanent, suffering, and not-self, disenchanted, dispassionate, causing cessation, and relinquishing rather than grasping.

Tassevaṃ sallakkhetvā punappunaṃ "aniccaṃ dukkhamanattā"ti rūpārūpadhamme tulayato tīrayato taṃ ñāṇaṃ tikkhaṃ hutvā vahati, saṅkhārā lahuṃ upaṭṭhahanti, ñāṇe tikkhe vahante saṅkhāresu lahuṃ upaṭṭhahantesu uppādaṃ vā ṭhitiṃ vā pavattaṃ vā nimittaṃ vā na sampāpuṇāti. Ru-Yi Translation

For one who, having thus discerned the characteristics, weighs and judges material and immaterial states again and again as "impermanent, suffering, not-self," that knowledge proceeds, having become sharp; the formations present themselves swiftly; and, as knowledge proceeds sharply and the formations present themselves swiftly, it no longer reaches as far as arising, or standing, or occurrence, or the sign; mindfulness comes to rest on nothing but destruction, passing away, breaking-up, and cessation. For one who thus sees, "having arisen in this way, this particular course of formations ceases" — at this point there arises the insight-knowledge called the contemplation of dissolution. With reference to this it was said:

"How is there knowledge of insight, which is the wisdom of contemplating dissolution by way of reflecting on the object? Consciousness, having visible form as its object, arises and breaks up; reflecting on that object, one contemplates the dissolution of that consciousness. 'One contemplates' — how does one contemplate? One contemplates as impermanent, not as permanent; one contemplates as suffering, not as pleasant; one contemplates as not-self, not as self; one grows disenchanted, one does not delight; one becomes dispassionate, one does not become passionate; one causes cessation, one does not cause origination; one relinquishes, one does not grasp.

"Contemplating as impermanent, one abandons the perception of permanence. Contemplating as suffering, the perception of pleasure; as not-self, the perception of self; growing disenchanted, delight; becoming dispassionate, lust; causing cessation, origination; relinquishing, one abandons grasping.

"Consciousness having feeling as its object … having perception as its object … having the formations as its object … having consciousness as its object … having the eye as its object … and so on … having aging-and-death as its object, arises and breaks up … and so on … relinquishing, one abandons grasping.

"The passing-over of the basis, and the turning-back of wisdom; and the power of adverting — this is the insight of reflecting.

"By inference from the object, a single determination of both; resolve upon cessation — this is the insight of the characteristic of passing away.

"Reflecting on the object, and contemplating dissolution; and its presenting itself as void — this is the insight of higher wisdom.

"Skilled in the three contemplations, and in the four insights; skilled in the three presentations — he does not tremble amid the various views."

"That is knowledge in the sense of the known. It is wisdom in the sense of understanding. Hence it is called: knowledge of insight, the wisdom of contemplating dissolution by way of reflecting on the object."

Reading note — Ru-Yi

This is a genuine escalation from chapter twenty's own knowledge of rise and fall, and the difference is worth stating precisely: rise-and-fall knowledge still attended to both halves of the cycle, coming-to-be and passing-away, in roughly equal measure. Dissolution-knowledge narrows attention deliberately and entirely onto the second half alone — arising, standing, and even the "sign" (nimitta, the recognizable mark by which an object is normally identified) all drop out of view, leaving only breaking-up, over and over, as the sole content of awareness. This narrowing is not a loss but a sharpening: by refusing to let attention linger on anything but cessation, the meditator makes dissolution itself vivid and undeniable in a way that a more balanced attention to both rise and fall could not.

The closing verses compress an entire technical vocabulary that the next section will unpack term by term — "the insight of reflecting," "the insight of the characteristic of passing away," and "the insight of higher wisdom" name three progressively deeper ways of contemplating the same dissolution, distinguished by exactly how the meditator's attention relates the breaking-up of the object to the breaking-up of the consciousness that cognized it.

Key vocabulary — for Pāli learners
bhaṅgānupassanā

contemplation of dissolution — bhaṅga (breaking, dissolution) + anupassanā (contemplation); the second of the nine knowledges, marking a deliberate narrowing of attention onto cessation alone, in contrast to the balanced rise-and-fall of the previous knowledge.

ārammaṇapaṭisaṅkhā

"reflecting on the object" — ārammaṇa (object) + paṭisaṅkhā (reflection, reconsideration); the technical name for the specific method this knowledge uses — first knowing an object as destroyed, then contemplating the dissolution of the very consciousness that knew it.

vayalakkhaṇavipassanā / adhipaññāvipassanā

the insight of the characteristic of passing away / the insight of higher wisdom — two further named refinements of dissolution-contemplation, the first inferring past-and-future dissolution from what is directly seen, the second adding the presentation of voidness (suññato upaṭṭhāna) — no one behind the breaking-up, only the breaking-up itself.

Commentary on Dissolution-Knowledge: Reflecting, Passing Away, and Higher Wisdom

Buddhaghosa now unpacks the Paṭisambhidāmagga's compressed verses term by term, explaining precisely how "reflecting on the object" differs from "the insight of the characteristic of passing away" and from "the insight of higher wisdom" — three progressively subtler ways the same underlying dissolution can be contemplated, from simply following one consciousness's breaking-up with another, to inferring past and future dissolution from what is directly seen, to a final presentation of formations as utterly void, with no one there to break up at all.

So evaṃ arajjanto lokikeneva tāva ñāṇena rāgaṃ nirodheti, no samudeti. Samudayaṃ na karotīti attho.

Atha vā so evaṃ viratto yathā diṭṭhaṃ saṅkhāragataṃ, tathā adiṭṭhampi anvayañāṇavasena nirodheti, no samudeti. Nirodhatova manasikaroti. Nirodhamevassa passati, no samudayanti attho.

So evaṃ paṭipanno paṭinissajjati, no ādiyati. Kiṃ vuttaṃ hoti? Ayampi aniccādianupassanā tadaṅgavasena saddhiṃ khandhābhisaṅkhārehi kilesānaṃ pariccajanato, saṅkhatadosadassanena ca tabbiparīte nibbāne tanninnatāya pakkhandanato pariccāgapaṭinissaggo ceva pakkhandanapaṭinissaggo cāti vuccati. Tasmā tāya samannāgato bhikkhu yathāvuttena nayena kilese pariccajati, nibbāne ca pakkhandati. Nāpi nibbattanavasena kilese ādiyati, na adosadassitāvasena saṅkhatārammaṇaṃ. Ru-Yi Translation

Herein, "reflecting on the object" means: having known whatever object by way of reflection, having seen it by way of ending and passing away. "The wisdom of contemplating dissolution" refers to the wisdom that contemplates the dissolution of the knowledge that arose from reflecting on the object by way of ending and passing away; this is said to be "knowledge of insight." "How does that come about?" — this, for now, is the meaning of the question asked out of a desire to explain. Then, in order to show how it comes about, "consciousness having visible form as its object" and so on was said.

Herein, "consciousness having visible form as its object arises and breaks up" means: consciousness having visible form as its object arises and breaks up. Or else, the meaning is: consciousness arises and breaks up in the state of having visible form as its object. "Reflecting on that object" means: having known that visible-form object by way of reflection, having seen it by way of ending and passing away. "One contemplates the dissolution of that consciousness" means: by a further consciousness, one contemplates the dissolution of the consciousness by which that visible-form object was seen by way of ending and passing away. Hence the ancients said: "one sees with insight both the known and the knowing."

And here, "one contemplates" means: one sees again and again — the meaning is, one sees repeatedly, in manifold ways. Hence it was said: "'one contemplates' — how does one contemplate? One contemplates as impermanent" and so on.

Herein, since dissolution is the very furthest extremity of impermanence, therefore the meditator contemplating dissolution contemplates the entire course of formations as impermanent, not as permanent. From there, because what is impermanent is suffering, and what is suffering is not-self, he contemplates that same thing as suffering, not as pleasant; as not-self, not as self.

And since what is impermanent, suffering, not-self should not be delighted in, and since what should not be delighted in should not be lusted after, therefore, in accordance with this contemplation of dissolution, he grows disenchanted, not delighted, with the course of formations seen as "impermanent, suffering, not-self." He becomes dispassionate, not passionate. Not becoming passionate in this way, by mundane knowledge alone, for now, he causes the cessation of lust, not its origination — the meaning is, he does not produce its arising.

Or else: having thus become dispassionate, by way of inferential knowledge, just as he causes the cessation of the formations he has seen, so too does he cause the cessation of those not seen, not their origination — he attends to them precisely as ceasing; he sees only their cessation, not their arising, the meaning is.

Practicing in this way, he relinquishes, he does not grasp. What is meant by this? This contemplation of impermanence and the rest is called both "relinquishment by giving up" and "relinquishment by entering into," because, by way of temporary abandonment, it gives up the defilements together with the aggregates and formations, and because, by seeing the fault in the conditioned, it enters into an inclination toward Nibbāna, the opposite of that. Therefore a monk endowed with it, by the method already stated, gives up the defilements and enters into Nibbāna. He neither grasps the defilements by way of their production, nor grasps a conditioned object by way of failing to see its fault. Hence it is said: "he relinquishes, he does not grasp."

Now, in order to show which states are abandoned by these knowledges, "contemplating as impermanent, one abandons the perception of permanence" and so on was said. Herein, "delight" is craving accompanied by rapture. The rest is by the method already stated.

In the verses, "the passing-over of the basis" means: having seen the dissolution of visible form, then, by way of seeing the dissolution of the very consciousness by which that dissolution was seen, the passing-over from the prior basis to a further basis. "The turning-back of wisdom" means: wisdom's coming to rest on passing away, having given up arising. "The power of adverting" means: the capacity, having seen the dissolution of visible form, to advert immediately afterward for the purpose of seeing the dissolution of the consciousness that has that dissolution as its object. "The insight of reflecting" is the name for this contemplation of dissolution by way of reflecting on the object.

"By inference from the object, a single determination of both" means: by inference from, by following after, the object seen directly — reasoning "just as this is, so too did the course of formations break up in the past, and so too will it break up in the future" — thus, the meaning is, the determination of both past and future by way of a single, uniform nature.

And this was said by the ancients:

"One whose seeing is purified with respect to what actually exists infers, by that same inference, to the past and the future: all things gone to formations are disintegrating, like a dewdrop when the sun has risen."

"Resolve upon cessation" means: having thus made a single determination of both by way of dissolution, the resolve, the deference, the inclination, the leaning, the tendency toward that very cessation reckoned as dissolution — this is the meaning. "The insight of the characteristic of passing away" — this, it is said, is the name for this insight.

"Reflecting on the object" means: having known the prior object, visible form and the rest. "One contemplates dissolution" means: having seen the dissolution of that object, one contemplates the dissolution of the consciousness that has that object. "Its presenting itself as void" means: for one thus contemplating dissolution in this way, the presentation as void — "it is only formations that break up; their breaking-up is death; there exists no one else at all" — is accomplished.

Hence the ancients said:

"The aggregates cease, and there is no other; the breaking-up of the aggregates is called death. One who is heedful sees their destruction, wisely, as one who bores through a gem with a diamond."

"The insight of higher wisdom" — reflecting on the object, contemplating dissolution, and the presentation as void: this, it is said, is the name for the insight of higher wisdom.

"Skilled in the three contemplations" means: a monk skilled in the three contemplations beginning with impermanence. "And in the four insights" means: and in the four insights beginning with disenchantment. "Skilled in the three presentations" means: skilled in this threefold presentation — as ending, as passing away, and as void. "He does not tremble amid the various views" means: he does not waver amid the various kinds of views beginning with the eternalist view.

Reading note — Ru-Yi

The quoted ancients' saying "one sees with insight both the known and the knowing" (ñātañca ñāṇañca ubhopi vipassati) states this section's structural principle in a single compact phrase: dissolution-contemplation always has two layers, the object's own dissolution (the "known") and, immediately following, the dissolution of the very consciousness that knew it (the "knowing"). This doubling is what makes the practice genuinely reflexive rather than a simple observation of external objects breaking apart — the observing mind itself is included in what breaks apart, moment by moment, at every single step.

The diamond-boring-through-a-gem simile in the second porāṇā verse is a striking, precise image: just as a diamond, the hardest of materials, can bore cleanly through even a precious gem, so too does heedful seeing bore cleanly through the aggregates' apparent solidity, down to the bare fact of their destruction — no metaphor of gentle dissolving or fading, but of forceful, deliberate penetration through what only seemed impenetrable.

The Water-Bubbles Simile; the Eight Benefits of Dissolution-Knowledge

A vivid simile now closes the subhead: just as a sighted man standing by a pond in heavy rain watches enormous bubbles rise on the water's surface and burst almost as soon as they appear, so does the meditator now see all formations doing nothing but breaking, breaking up — a seeing the Buddha himself, in the Dhammapada, said keeps the King of Death from finding such a person at all. Eight concrete benefits follow from this knowledge growing strong, closing the subhead with a warning to pursue it as urgently as one would put out a fire in one's own hair.

Ru-Yi Translation

Not wavering in this way, with his attention proceeding thus, "only what is unceased ceases, only what is unbroken breaks" — like a fragile vessel being broken, like a fine speck of dust being scattered, like sesame seeds being roasted — he gives up the sign of arising, standing, and occurrence in all formations, and sees only their breaking-up. Just as a sighted man, standing on the bank of a lotus pond or a river while thick raindrops fall from the sky, might see great big bubbles arising on the water's surface and breaking up swiftly, one after another — in just the same way he sees all formations breaking, breaking up. For it was with reference to a meditator of this kind that the Blessed One said:

"As one would see a bubble, as one would see a mirage — so seeing the world, the King of Death does not see him."

For one who thus continually sees "all formations are breaking, breaking up," dissolution-knowledge, surrounded by eight benefits, attains strength. Herein, these are the eight benefits: the abandoning of the view of being; the giving up of attachment to life; being constantly, diligently applied; a purified livelihood; the abandoning of anxious striving; freedom from fear; the attainment of patience and gentleness; and the ability to bear discontent and delight.

Hence the ancients said:

"Having seen these eight excellent qualities, the sage, like one whose head or clothes are ablaze, contemplates that again and again, a contemplator of dissolution, for the attainment of the deathless."

Knowledge of the contemplation of dissolution is finished.

Reading note — Ru-Yi

The eight benefits, read together, describe a meditator's whole life reorganizing itself around this one seeing: freedom from the view that "being" is something solid and lasting naturally loosens attachment to one's own continued existence; a livelihood becomes purified once the anxious striving that ordinarily drives dishonest or corner-cutting behavior has nothing left to grasp at; and the capacity to bear both discontent and delight evenly follows directly from having already seen that neither state, being merely more breaking-up formations, deserves any special weight. None of these is presented as a separate practice to undertake — all eight simply fall out, as natural consequences, from dissolution-knowledge growing strong.

The closing verse's image — contemplating dissolution "like one whose head or clothes are ablaze" — is a stock canonical simile for the most extreme possible urgency (compare the Buddha's own frequent use of it elsewhere for the urgency of practice generally), and its appearance here, right before the chapter turns to fear, danger, and disenchantment, is a deliberate hinge: dissolution-knowledge itself is not yet fear, but it produces exactly the clarity that makes the next three knowledges' response of fear entirely appropriate rather than excessive.

Fear Manifest: The Formations Appear as a Great Terror

The third subhead marks a genuine emotional shift: as dissolution-contemplation continues, formations throughout every possible mode of existence begin to present themselves as one great terror, likened to lions, tigers, serpents, thunderbolts, and blazing pits of embers. Two unforgettable similes drive the point home — a mother watching her eldest son's head already struck off and her middle son's being struck, who releases her attachment to her youngest before the blade even reaches him; and a mother who, having already lost nine children and watching a tenth die in her arms, gives up hope for the one still in her womb.

Ru-Yi Translation

For one who cultivates, develops, and practices again and again this contemplation of dissolution, which has as its object the ending, passing away, breaking-up, and cessation of all formations, the disintegrating formations throughout every mode of being, every womb, every destination, every station, and every abode of beings present themselves as a great terror, like a lion, a tiger, a leopard, a bear, a hyena, a demon, a fierce ox, a fierce dog, a rutting rogue elephant, a dreadful serpent, a thunderbolt, cemeteries, a battlefield, a blazing pit of embers, and the like, to a timid man who wishes to live comfortably. For one who thus sees, "past formations have ceased; present ones are ceasing; formations still to arise in the future will likewise cease" — at this point there arises the knowledge called the appearance as fear.

Here, then, is a simile: a certain woman, it is said, had three sons who had offended the king, and the king ordered their heads cut off. She went to the place of execution together with her sons. Then, having cut off the eldest son's head, they began to cut off the middle son's. Seeing the eldest's head already cut off and the middle one's head being cut, she gave up her attachment to the youngest, thinking, "this one too will be just like these." Herein: as that woman's seeing of the eldest son's already-cut-off head is like the meditator's seeing of the cessation of past formations; her seeing of the middle son's head being cut is like his seeing of the cessation of present formations; her giving up of attachment to the youngest son, thinking "this one too will be just like these," is like his seeing of the cessation of future formations, thinking, "formations still to arise in the future too will break up." For one who thus sees, at this point, the knowledge of appearance as fear arises.

Here is another simile: a certain woman with a sickly womb, it is said, gave birth to ten children. Of these, nine died; a tenth was dying in her arms; another was still in her womb. Seeing the nine children dead and the tenth dying, she gave up her attachment to the one still in her womb, thinking, "this one too will be just like these." Herein: as that woman's remembrance of the death of the nine children is like the meditator's seeing of the cessation of past formations; her seeing the state of the one dying in her arms is like his seeing of the cessation of present formations; her giving up of attachment to the one still in her womb is like his seeing of the cessation of future formations. For one who thus sees, at this moment, the knowledge of appearance as fear arises.

Reading note — Ru-Yi

Both similes share an identical logical structure, applied to two of the most emotionally unbearable scenarios the treatise ever invokes: a witnessed past, a witnessed present, and an inferred future, all converging on the same conclusion. What makes these similes work as similes, rather than mere shock, is precisely their logical rigor — the mother's grief is not treated as excessive or irrational but as the entirely reasonable response of someone who has correctly inferred a pattern from direct evidence. The meditator's own fear-knowledge is offered as exactly this same kind of reasonable inference, applied not to three sons or ten children but to every formation that has ever arisen or ever will.

It is worth noting how carefully these similes are calibrated to a specifically maternal register — a mother watching her children die is invoked twice in succession, rather than any of the equally available images of personal danger the treatise uses elsewhere. This choice underscores that the fear being cultivated here is not self-protective anxiety but something closer to grief rightly directed: sorrow at witnessing an inevitable, repeating pattern of loss, precisely the register the next subhead's knowledge of danger will develop even further.

Fear-Knowledge Does Not Itself Fear: The Three Pits of Embers

A careful clarification follows: fear-knowledge itself does not experience fear, any more than a sighted man looking at three pits of embers or three execution-stakes at a city gate is himself afraid — each merely makes the accurate judgment that whoever falls into them will suffer. A canonical formula then specifies exactly what appears as fear under each of the three characteristics: attending to impermanence, it is the "sign" of formations that frightens; attending to suffering, it is their very occurrence; attending to not-self, both together.

Ru-Yi Translation

But does the knowledge of appearance as fear itself fear, or not? It does not fear. For it consists merely in the judgment "past formations have ceased; present ones are ceasing; future ones will cease." Therefore, just as a sighted man looking at three pits of embers at a city gate does not himself fear, but merely makes the judgment, "whoever falls into these will all experience no small suffering" — or just as a sighted man looking at a row of three stakes set up, one of acacia wood, one of iron, one of gold, does not himself fear, but merely makes the judgment, "whoever falls upon these stakes will all experience no small suffering" — in just the same way, the knowledge of appearance as fear does not itself fear; it merely makes the judgment, with respect to the three kinds of being resembling the three pits of embers and the three stakes, "past formations have ceased; present ones are ceasing; future ones will cease." But since, for it, the formations belonging to every mode of being, womb, destination, station, and abode, having fallen into ruin, present themselves as fearsome, entailing danger, it is therefore called "the appearance as fear."

As for this appearing as fear, this is the canonical text:

"For one attending as impermanent, what appears as fear? Suffering. For one attending as suffering, what appears as fear? Not-self. For one attending as not-self, what appears as fear? For one attending as impermanent, the sign appears as fear. For one attending as suffering, occurrence appears as fear. For one attending as not-self, both the sign and occurrence appear as fear."

Herein, "the sign" is the sign of the formations — this is a designation precisely for past, future, and present formations. For one attending as impermanent sees nothing but the death of the formations, and hence the sign appears to him as fear. "Occurrence" is the occurrence of material and immaterial existence. For one attending as suffering sees nothing but the continual affliction inherent even in occurrence reckoned as pleasant, and hence occurrence appears to him as fear. But one attending as not-self sees both of these — like an empty village, like a mirage or a phantom city — as void, vain, empty, without an owner, without a guide. Hence both the sign and occurrence appear to him as fear.

Knowledge of appearance as fear is finished.

Reading note — Ru-Yi

This distinction between the knowledge itself and what it observes resolves a question a careful reader might otherwise raise about the previous section's vivid imagery: if formations genuinely appear as lions, serpents, and execution grounds, is the meditator simply terrified in an ordinary, disabling sense? The answer given here is precise — the knowledge is a clear-eyed judgment about danger, structurally identical to a bystander correctly assessing the risk of a pit of embers without falling into it himself. This is what allows fear-knowledge to function as insight rather than as mere anxiety: it sees danger accurately without being overwhelmed by it, exactly the composed clarity the following knowledge, disenchantment, will need as its own foundation.

The threefold mapping — impermanence reveals the sign as fearsome, suffering reveals occurrence as fearsome, not-self reveals both — gives each of the three characteristics a distinct diagnostic function within this one knowledge, rather than treating them as interchangeable near-synonyms. Only not-self, uniting both sign and occurrence under the single image of an empty village or a phantom city, delivers the full and final verdict: nothing here has ever had an owner, and nothing here has ever had a guide.

Reading note — Ru-Yi

The systematic sweep through the entire architecture already built across chapters fourteen through seventeen — the three kinds of being, four elements, five aggregates, six internal and external bases, seven stations of consciousness, nine abodes of beings — is a deliberate closing of every possible exit. Each doctrinal category the treatise spent chapters carefully distinguishing is now revisited one final time, not as a neutral classification but as a specific kind of hazard: elements as poison, aggregates as armed killers, sense bases as an emptied village on one side and marauding bandits on the other. The effect is cumulative and total — there is, by the end of the list, no remaining category of experience that has not been shown to be dangerous.

The eight everyday dangers that follow (the beast-infested thicket, the crocodile-filled water, armed enemies, poisoned food, a thief-ridden road, a burning house, an army arrayed for battle) work differently from the earlier doctrinal list: rather than mapping technical categories onto hazards, they simply saturate ordinary experience itself with danger, so that literally nothing — not even a pleasant-looking forest — offers a moment's relief. This totality is the entire point: danger-knowledge is not fear of any one particular thing, but the collapse of the very category of "a safe place" anywhere within conditioned existence.

The Full Danger-Knowledge Formula: Birth, Aging, Death, and Their Opposite

A detailed Paṭisambhidāmagga formula now runs through fourteen terms — arising, occurrence, the sign, accumulation, rebirth-linking, destiny, production, rebirth, birth, aging, sickness, death, sorrow, lamentation, and despair — declaring each one fear, suffering, and tainted by the flesh in turn, before pairing each with its precise opposite: non-arising, safety, happiness, untainted, Nibbāna. Reading note — Ru-Yi

The formula's repeated four-part rhythm — arising alone is fear, non-arising alone is safety, arising-versus-non-arising together yields safety-knowledge — is not empty repetition but a deliberate pedagogical structure: each of the fourteen terms is first condemned in isolation, then redeemed by its opposite in isolation, then the two are explicitly paired, ensuring the meditator's understanding of danger never exists without an equally clear understanding of what danger's absence would actually be. This is the formal, technical version of exactly the reassurance the commentary explains in its own words: danger-knowledge, rightly developed, never leaves the meditator stranded in fear alone.

The phrase "tainted by the flesh" (sāmisa) deserves a moment's attention, since its ordinary sense — literally, "having meat, flesh, or material substance" — has been extended here into a technical term for anything still entangled in the round of rebirth, the world, or the defilements. Its opposite, nirāmisa, "untainted by the flesh," becomes correspondingly a term of art for what stands entirely free of that entanglement — which is to say, for Nibbāna itself, exactly as the passage's own fourth and final round of the formula makes explicit.

Disenchantment: The Golden Swan, the Lion, and the Six-Tusked Elephant

Seeing danger everywhere naturally ripens into the fifth knowledge, disenchantment — a state illustrated by three royal animals, each refusing an inferior setting for the one place suited to its true nature: a golden swan that will not settle for a filthy village puddle when the seven great lakes await it, a lion that finds no joy even in a golden cage, and the great white elephant Chaddanta who forsakes the heart of a city for his own thicket in the Himalayas. A closing note reveals that fear-knowledge, danger-knowledge, and disenchantment are, in truth, one and the same thing under three different names.

This, however, is one in meaning with the previous two knowledges. Hence the ancients said:

"The appearance as fear is a single thing that receives three names: since it saw all formations as fear, it came to be called 'the appearance as fear.' Since it produces danger with respect to those same formations, it came to be called 'the contemplation of danger.' Reading note — Ru-Yi

The three royal animals — swan, lion, and the six-tusked elephant king Chaddanta, a figure familiar from his own Jātaka tale — are each chosen for the same underlying structure: a creature of genuine majesty, placed somewhere objectively pleasant by ordinary standards (a golden cage, the heart of a city, a clean puddle by comparison with a swan's usual haunts), who nonetheless refuses to settle, because something in its own nature recognizes that only one particular place is actually suited to it. The comparison flatters the meditator without flattering the meditator's circumstances: disenchantment is not a failure to appreciate what conditioned existence offers, but the accurate recognition, by a being of genuine spiritual stature, that none of it is actually home.

The closing identification of fear, danger, and disenchantment as "one in meaning, differing only in the letter" retroactively reframes the two preceding subheads: they were never three separate stages to pass through in sequence, but three names for a single continuous deepening of the same recognition, each name simply foregrounding a different aspect — the initial shock (fear), the settled assessment (danger), and the resulting loss of appetite (disenchantment) — of one unified seeing.

Desire for Deliverance: Nine Similes of Longing to Escape

Disenchantment ripens directly into a sixth knowledge: the mind no longer clings to even a single formation, and longs only to be free of the entire course of conditioned existence. Nine vivid images of creatures and things desperate to escape their predicament — a fish in a net, a frog in a snake's mouth, a wild fowl in a cage, a deer in a snare, a snake in a charmer's grip, an elephant sunk in mire, a serpent-king in a garuḍa's beak, the moon swallowed by Rāhu, a man surrounded by enemies — together convey the sheer urgency of this longing.

Reading note — Ru-Yi

The nine creatures chosen span an entire spectrum of vulnerability and even mythic power — from a helpless fish and frog, through ordinary animals like the deer and the caged fowl, up to genuinely formidable beings like a serpent-king and even the moon itself, cosmically swallowed by the eclipse-demon Rāhu. This range matters: desire for deliverance is not being claimed as an emotion only the weak or trapped feel, but one that grips every degree of power and stature alike, the instant any of them recognizes its own predicament clearly. Even something as vast and untouchable as the moon, on this logic, wants nothing more than to escape once it understands what has actually swallowed it.

This knowledge's brevity in the source text — a single paragraph, by far the shortest of the nine — is itself meaningful: unlike the elaborately staged similes of fear, danger, and the equanimity-subhead still to come, desire for deliverance needs no extended narrative to make its point, since the nine rapid-fire images accumulate their force precisely through quick succession rather than sustained development, mirroring the knowledge's own restless, urgent quality.

Reflection-Knowledge: Reapplying the Three Characteristics, and the Snake-Catcher's Simile

Desire for deliverance now drives the meditator back to the formations once more, this time specifically to work out the actual means of release: the three characteristics are reapplied through an extensive catalogue of reasons — impermanent, suffering, foul, not-self — not for their own sake but expressly "for the sake of accomplishing the means of deliverance." A wonderful extended simile makes the whole preceding sequence vivid at once: a man who reaches into water to catch a fish, seizes a snake by mistake, and — recognizing his danger — carefully, methodically weakens and releases it, each stage of his handling mapped precisely onto one of the knowledges from fear through reflection.

He sees all formations as impermanent, by reasons such as: not being everlasting, being temporary, being delimited by arising and passing away, disintegrating, fickle, breaking up, unstable, subject to change, coreless, subject to annihilation, conditioned, subject to death.

He sees them as suffering, by reasons such as: continual affliction, being a basis for suffering, being a disease, a boil, a dart, affliction, illness, a calamity, a misfortune, danger, a menace, no protection, no shelter, no refuge, a wretchedness, the root of wretchedness, a murderer, tainted by the cankers, Māra's bait, subject to birth, subject to aging, subject to sickness, subject to sorrow, subject to lamentation, subject to despair, subject to defilement.

He sees them as foul, being an attendant on the characteristic of suffering, by reasons such as: being ignoble, foul-smelling, disgusting, repulsive, unfit for adornment, deformed, hideous.

He sees them as not-self, by reasons such as: being alien, empty, vain, void, without an owner, without a master, not submitting to one's will.

For one seeing in this way, the formations, with the three characteristics applied, are said to be grasped. But why does he grasp them in this way? For the sake of accomplishing the means of deliverance.

Here, then, is a simile: a certain man, it is said, wishing to catch fish, took up a fish-trap and set it out in the water. Reaching in his hand swiftly, he seized, within the water, a snake by the neck, and, thinking "I've caught a fish!", was delighted. Lifting it up, thinking "what a big fish I've caught!", and looking at it, he recognized it as a snake by seeing its three curved markings; frightened, having seen the danger, disenchanted with the catch, wishing to release it, and making the means for releasing it, he unwound his grip starting from the tip of the tail, lifted his arm, whirled it two or three times above his head, weakened the snake, and, letting go, said, "go, wicked snake!" — then, climbing swiftly onto the pond's bank, and thinking, "truly, I am freed from the mouth of a great snake!", he stood watching the path he had come by.

Herein: that man's moment of delight, thinking "a fish!", upon seizing the snake by the neck, is like this meditator's own moment of delight, at the very start, upon obtaining his own person; his drawing the head out of the fish-trap's opening and seeing the three curved markings is like this meditator's breaking up compactness and seeing the three characteristics in the formations; his moment of fright is like this meditator's knowledge of appearance as fear. Then, his seeing the danger thereafter is like the knowledge of the contemplation of danger; his disenchantment with the catch is like the knowledge of the contemplation of disenchantment. His wish to release the snake is like the knowledge of desire for deliverance; his making the means for releasing it is like the application of the three characteristics to the formations by the knowledge of the contemplation of reflection. For just as that man, having whirled the snake and weakened it, turned it back, brought it to a state of being unable to bite, and released it, well and truly freed — in just the same way, this meditator, having whirled the formations by the application of the three characteristics and weakened them, brings them to a state of being unable any longer to present themselves in the guise of permanent, pleasant, and beautiful selfhood, and releases them, well and truly freed. Hence it was said: "he grasps them in this way for the sake of accomplishing the means of deliverance."

To this extent, reflection-knowledge has arisen for him. With reference to this it was said:

"For one attending as impermanent, what reflection-knowledge arises? Suffering. For one attending as not-self, what reflection-knowledge arises? For one attending as impermanent, reflection-knowledge of the sign arises. For one attending as suffering, reflection-knowledge of occurrence arises. For one attending as not-self, reflection-knowledge of both the sign and occurrence arises."

Herein, "reflection-knowledge of the sign" means: having known the sign of the formations, by way of the characteristic of impermanence, as "unstable, temporary." Although, admittedly, it is not the case that one first knows and only afterward does the knowledge arise, still, it is spoken of in this way by way of ordinary usage, just as in expressions such as "dependent on mind and on mind-objects, mind-consciousness arises." Or else, it should be understood as spoken thus by way of the method of unity, treating what is prior and what is subsequent as one. By this same method, the meaning should be understood for the other two terms as well.

Reading note — Ru-Yi

The snake-catcher's simile earns its place as one of the treatise's finest precisely because every single beat of the narrative maps onto a specific, named knowledge, turning nine abstract technical terms into one continuous, rememberable story: the innocent delight of first grasping a body ("a fish!") mirrors ordinary beings' initial, uncomplicated pleasure in simply existing; the moment of recognizing the three curved markings mirrors seeing the three characteristics once compactness is broken apart; fright, danger, disenchantment, and desire for deliverance follow in the man's own emotional sequence exactly as they arose across the previous four subheads; and the careful, methodical whirling and weakening of the snake — not a panicked fling, but a controlled technique aimed at rendering it harmless before release — is reflection-knowledge itself, applying the three characteristics not as an end but as the actual mechanism of safe deliverance.

The final image — standing on the bank, looking back at the path just traveled, safe — anticipates exactly where this chapter's own closing subheads will eventually arrive: a vantage point from which the entire prior ordeal can be surveyed with relief rather than dread. It is worth noting, too, that the man in the story never blames the water, the trap, or fish-catching itself for what happened; he simply learns to recognize what he has actually caught and handles it accordingly, exactly as reflection-knowledge asks of the meditator with respect to the aggregates themselves.

The Fourfold to Forty-Two-Fold Analysis of Voidness

The eighth and by far the largest subhead opens with a systematic escalation: voidness, already grasped simply as "all formations are void," is now analyzed by two aspects (void of self, void of what belongs to self), then four (no self anywhere, mine or another's), then six, eight, ten, twelve, and finally forty-two aspects — the same forty-two-term list already met in chapter twenty, now explicitly reapplied as a culmination of "full understanding through scrutiny." Nine similes for coreless things — a reed, a castor-oil plant, a fig tree, foam, a water-bubble, a mirage, a banana trunk, a magic trick — anchor the eightfold stage in vivid, everyday images before the sequence closes with a Sutta Nipāta verse urging the reader, by name, to see the world as void.

Ru-Yi Translation

Having thus grasped, by the knowledge of the contemplation of reflection, "all formations are void," he next grasps the twofold voidness: "this is void of self, or of what belongs to self." Thus seeing neither a self, nor anything else established as a self's own possession, he next grasps the fourfold voidness spoken of thus: "I am not anywhere a something belonging to anyone; nor is there anywhere, in anything, a something of mine."

How? "I am not anywhere" means: he does not see a self anywhere. "A something belonging to anyone" means: he does not see his own self as something to be offered up as belonging to anyone else — the meaning is, he does not see it as fit to be reckoned and offered up in the place of a brother, or in the place of a companion, or in the place of a possession. "Nor is there anywhere" — setting aside, for the moment, the word "mine" — this phrase means: nor does he see another's self anywhere. Now, bringing in the word "mine": "a something of mine" means: he does not see another's self as existing as any kind of something belonging to him. The meaning is: he does not see another's self, in any respect, as fit to be offered up, in the place of his own brother, or companion, or possession. Thus, since he sees a self nowhere at all, nor sees it as fit to be offered up as belonging to another, nor sees another's self, nor sees another's self as fit to be offered up as his own — for this reason, by him, the fourfold voidness has been grasped.

Having thus grasped the fourfold voidness, he next grasps voidness by six aspects. How? The eye is void of self, or of what belongs to self, or of the permanent, or of the stable, or of the eternal, or of the unchanging … and so on … the mind is void. Visible forms are void … and so on … mind-objects are void. Eye-consciousness … and so on … mind-consciousness. Eye-contact — thus, the method should be extended all the way up to aging-and-death.

Having thus grasped voidness by six aspects, he next grasps it by eight aspects. That is: matter is coreless, without a core, gone from any core — whether a core of permanence, a core of stability, a core of pleasure, a core of self — or of the permanent, the stable, the eternal, the unchanging. Feeling … perception … the formations … consciousness … the eye … and so on … aging-and-death is coreless, without a core, gone from any core — whether a core of permanence, a core of stability, a core of pleasure, a core of self — or of the permanent, the stable, the eternal, the unchanging. Just as a reed is coreless, without a core, gone from any core; just as a castor-oil plant … just as a cluster-fig tree … just as a white-flowered vine … just as a Pāṭali tree … just as a lump of foam … just as a water-bubble … just as a mirage … just as a banana trunk … just as a magic trick is coreless, without a core, gone from any core — in just the same way, matter … and so on … aging-and-death is coreless, without a core, gone from any core, whether a core of permanence … and so on … the unchanging.

Having thus grasped voidness by eight aspects, he next grasps it by ten aspects: he sees matter as vain. He sees it as empty, as void, as not-self, as without mastery, as not acting according to desire, as unobtainable, as not submitting to one's will, as alien, as isolated. Feeling … and so on … consciousness he sees as vain … and so on … as isolated.

Having thus grasped voidness by ten aspects, he next grasps it by twelve aspects. That is: matter is not a being, not a life, not a man, not a youth, not a woman, not a man, not a self, not what belongs to a self; not I, not mine, not another's, not anyone's. Feeling … and so on … consciousness is not anyone's.

Having thus grasped voidness by twelve aspects, he next grasps voidness by forty-two aspects, by way of full understanding through scrutiny. He sees matter as impermanent … as suffering … as a disease … as a boil … as a dart … as affliction … as illness … as alien … as disintegrating … as a calamity … as a misfortune … as danger … as a menace … as fickle … as breaking up … as unstable … as no protection … as no shelter … as no refuge … as not become a refuge … as vain … as empty … as void … as not-self … as without relish … as a wretchedness … as subject to change … as coreless … as the root of wretchedness … as a murderer … as subject to annihilation … as tainted by the cankers … as conditioned … as Māra's bait … as subject to birth … subject to aging … subject to sickness … subject to death … subject to sorrow, lamentation, pain, grief, and despair … as origination … as passing away … as without relish … as danger … as escape. Feeling … and so on … consciousness he sees as impermanent … and so on … as escape.

And this was said: "one who sees matter as impermanent … and so on … as escape regards the world as void. One who sees feeling … and so on … consciousness as impermanent … and so on … as escape regards the world as void."

"Regard the world as void, Mogharāja, always mindful; having uprooted the view of self, thus you may cross beyond death. So regarding the world, the King of Death does not see him."

Reading note — Ru-Yi

The steady doubling and expansion of aspects — two, four, six, eight, ten, twelve, forty-two — is not arithmetic for its own sake but a deliberate exhaustion strategy: each new count of aspects approaches the same underlying voidness from a genuinely different angle (grammatical persons in the fourfold analysis, the six sense-doors in the sixfold, comparison with coreless natural objects in the eightfold, negated qualities of mastery and desire in the tenfold, negated categories of personhood in the twelvefold, and finally the full forty-two-term catalogue already built in chapter twenty), so that by the time the sequence closes, voidness has been demonstrated from every direction a meditator's mind might still find a foothold for self.

The nine "coreless" similes at the eightfold stage are worth lingering on for their specificity: a reed, a castor-oil plant, and a fig tree are all plants proverbial in Indian tradition for lacking substantial heartwood, quite unlike a teak or sāl tree; a white-flowered vine and a Pāṭali tree continue the botanical theme; and foam, a water-bubble, a mirage, and a magic trick move from plant life to pure appearance-without-substance. The direct address to "Mogharāja" by name in the closing verse — quoting a specific question-and-answer exchange from the Sutta Nipāta's Pārāyanavagga — gives this entire abstract progression a startlingly personal frame: this is not a general doctrine but the Buddha's own answer to one particular seeker's urgent question about how to escape the sight of death.

The Released Husband; the Crow at Sea

Having seen the formations as thoroughly void, the meditator abandons both fear and delight and becomes genuinely neutral toward them — like a husband who has released an unfaithful wife and no longer reacts, whether with jealousy or affection, to anything she does. This equanimity is then tested against a single crucial question: has it actually glimpsed Nibbāna as peaceful? If so, it leaps toward it entirely, like a ship's scout-crow released at sea that flies straight for land the instant it sights shore; if not, it simply circles back to the formations again and again, exactly as that same crow returns to the masthead when no land is in view.

Having thus seen it as void, and having applied the three characteristics, grasping the formations, he gives up both fear and delight, becomes indifferent and neutral toward the formations, and does not grasp them as "I" or "mine" — like a man who has released his wife.

Just as a man might have a wife who was dear, beloved, pleasing to him, and he could not bear to be without her even for a moment, and would cherish her exceedingly; and, seeing that woman standing, or sitting, or talking, or laughing together with another man, he would become angry, displeased, and would experience intense sorrow. But at some later time, seeing a fault in that woman, and wishing to be rid of her, he releases her, and no longer grasps her as "mine." From that point on, even seeing her doing anything at all with anyone whatsoever, he would no longer become angry, would no longer fall into sorrow, but would simply remain indifferent, neutral. In just the same way, this meditator, wishing to be freed from all formations, grasping the formations by the contemplation of reflection, not seeing anything fit to be grasped as "I" or "mine," gives up both fear and delight, and becomes indifferent, neutral, toward all formations.

For one thus knowing, thus seeing, the mind draws back, shrinks back, turns away, and does not extend itself toward the three kinds of being, the four wombs, the five destinies, the seven stations of consciousness, and the nine abodes of beings; only equanimity, or a sense of aversion, becomes established.

Just as water droplets on a slightly tilted lotus leaf draw back, shrink back, turn away, and do not spread out — in just the same way … and so on … just as a chicken feather, or a strip of sinew, thrown into a fire, draws back, shrinks back, turns away, and does not spread out — in just the same way, his mind, with respect to the three kinds of being … and so on … only equanimity, or a sense of aversion, becomes established. Thus, for him, the knowledge called equanimity toward formations, it is said, has arisen.

Now, if this knowledge sees the state of peace, Nibbāna, as peaceful, it gives up the entire occurrence of formations and leaps forward into Nibbāna alone. But if it does not see Nibbāna as peaceful, it continues occurring, again and again, with the formations alone as its object — like the direction-finding crow of seafarers. Seafaring merchants, it is said, embarking on a ship, take along what is called a direction-finding crow. When the ship, driven off course by the wind, sails into foreign waters and the shore can no longer be seen, they release the direction-finding crow. Standing on the mast-top, it leaps into the sky and searches every direction and quarter; if it sees the shore, it flies straight toward it. But if it does not see the shore, it comes back again and again and settles right back on the mast-top. In just the same way, if the knowledge of equanimity toward formations sees the state of peace, Nibbāna, as peaceful, it gives up the entire occurrence of formations and leaps forward into Nibbāna alone. But if it does not see it, it continues occurring, again and again, with the formations alone as its object.

This is like flour being kneaded on a well-cleaned surface, or like ginned cotton being carded — having grasped the formations in various ways, and having given up both fear and delight, it stands neutral in its examination of the formations, standing by way of the threefold contemplation. So standing, it enters into a threefold state of being a door to liberation, and becomes a condition for the distinguishing of the seven noble persons.

Reading note — Ru-Yi

The released-husband simile is remarkable for refusing an easy resolution: this is not a man who has learned to love his wife despite her faults, nor one who has grown to hate her — he has simply, completely stopped being invested in the outcome either way. This precise emotional register is exactly what distinguishes equanimity toward formations (saṅkhārupekkhā) from the disenchantment and fear that preceded it: those earlier knowledges still involved a charged, aversive relationship to the formations; equanimity has moved past charge altogether, in either direction.

The scout-crow simile (disākāka, a documented practice among ancient Indian Ocean mariners before reliable navigation instruments existed) supplies this subhead's single most important practical clarification: equanimity toward formations is not yet the supramundane path itself, but a threshold state whose very next move depends entirely on whether Nibbāna has actually come into view. A meditator whose equanimity has not yet glimpsed the peaceful state simply continues cycling through insight's objects — not a failure, but the crow's own patient return to the masthead, ready to launch again the moment land appears.

The Three Doors to Liberation

Standing in equanimity by way of the "threefold contemplation," the meditator's practice now enters into what the canon calls the three doors to liberation: the contemplation of impermanence, regarding formations as bounded round by rise and fall, opens onto the signless door; the contemplation of suffering, stirring the mind with urgency, opens onto the desireless door; the contemplation of not-self, regarding all states as alien, opens onto the void door. A careful clarification follows, explaining why the Abhidhamma itself, in one well-known passage, names only two of these three liberations rather than all three.

Herein, this "entering into a threefold state of being a door to liberation" occurs by way of the dominance of three faculties, through occurring by way of the threefold contemplation. For the three contemplations are called the three doors to liberation. As it is said —

"Now, these three doors to liberation lead to deliverance from the world: through regarding all formations as delimited and bounded round, and the mind's leaping forward into the signless element; through the mind's being roused with respect to all formations, and its leaping forward into the desireless element; through regarding all states as alien, and the mind's leaping forward into the void element — these three doors to liberation lead to deliverance from the world."

Herein, "as delimited and bounded round" means: delimited and bounded round by way of rise and fall. For the contemplation of impermanence, having delimited "the formations did not exist before their arising," and searching for their destination, regards them as bounded round thus: "they do not go on beyond passing away; they simply vanish right there." "The mind's being roused" means: the mind's being stirred with a sense of urgency. For the contemplation of suffering stirs the mind with respect to the formations. "Regarding as alien" means: regarding as not-self thus: "not I, not mine." Thus these three phrases should be understood as spoken by way of the contemplations of impermanence and the rest. Hence it was said, in the very next answer to the question: "for one attending as impermanent, the formations present themselves as ending. For one attending as suffering, the formations present themselves as fearsome. For one attending as not-self, the formations present themselves as void."

But what are those liberations for which these contemplations are doors? These three: the signless, the desireless, and the void. For this was said: "attending as impermanent, abounding in resolution, one attains the signless liberation. Attending as suffering, abounding in tranquility, one attains the desireless liberation. Attending as not-self, abounding in enthusiasm, one attains the void liberation."

Herein, "the signless liberation" is the noble path occurring with Nibbāna as its object under the signless aspect. For it is "signless" because it arose from the signless element; and it is "liberation" because it is freed from the defilements. By this same method, that occurring with Nibbāna as its object under the desireless aspect should be understood as "desireless"; and that occurring with Nibbāna as its object under the void aspect, as "void."

But as for what was said in the Abhidhamma — "at the time when one develops the supramundane jhāna, leading out, going to diminution, for the abandonment of wrong views, for the attainment of the first stage, one dwells, having attained the first jhāna, quite secluded from sensual pleasures … desireless, void" — there, only two liberations are named, and this is said with reference specifically to the approach through insight. For, although in the Paṭisambhidāmagga, insight-knowledge —

"Knowledge of the contemplation of impermanence releases adherence to the permanent — this is the void liberation. Knowledge of the contemplation of suffering releases adherence to the pleasant. Knowledge of the contemplation of not-self releases adherence to self — this is the void liberation" — thus, is called the void liberation, by way of releasing adherence; and,

"Knowledge of the contemplation of impermanence releases the sign of the permanent — this is the signless liberation. Knowledge of the contemplation of suffering releases the sign of the pleasant; knowledge of the contemplation of not-self releases the sign of self — this is the signless liberation" — thus, is called the signless liberation, by way of releasing the sign; and,

"Knowledge of the contemplation of impermanence releases longing for the permanent — this is the desireless liberation. Knowledge of the contemplation of suffering releases longing for the pleasant. Knowledge of the contemplation of not-self releases longing for self — this is the desireless liberation" — thus, is called the desireless liberation, by way of releasing longing —

— all this was said. Even so, since insight does not give up the sign of the formations, it is not, strictly speaking, "signless." But it is, strictly speaking, both void and desireless. And it is by way of this insight's own approach that the liberation at the moment of the noble path was drawn out. Therefore it should be understood that only these two liberations — the desireless and the void — were named. This, so far, is the discussion of liberation.

Reading note — Ru-Yi

The explanation for why the Abhidhamma names only two of the three liberations is a genuine piece of technical precision worth following closely: strictly speaking, insight-knowledge itself never actually gives up the "sign" of the formations — it continues taking formations as its object, marked and recognizable, right up until the very moment the path arises and takes Nibbāna, not any formation, as its object. Only at the path-moment itself, retroactively as it were, does the whole preceding approach through insight get called "signless" by association, since that is the name the resulting liberation bears. Void and desireless, however, are genuinely and directly insight's own accomplishment throughout — hence the Abhidhamma's own careful restriction to naming only those two.

The three doors correspond so precisely to the three characteristics, and each door in turn to a specific dominant faculty (resolution/faith for the signless door, tranquility/concentration for the desireless door, enthusiasm/wisdom for the void door), that this passage effectively supplies the master key for everything the rest of the subhead will unfold: the seven noble persons, the varying jhāna factors, and the four kinds of progress the chapter is about to classify all trace back to which of these three doors a given meditator happens to pass through.

Reading note — Ru-Yi

The seven noble persons are not seven different levels of attainment but seven different temperamental routes to the same four noble paths and fruits, distinguished entirely by which faculty happens to predominate as a given meditator's equanimity ripens: the faith-follower and one liberated by faith travel by way of resolution and confidence; the body-witness and one liberated both ways by way of tranquility and concentration (the latter specifically for those who have also mastered an immaterial jhāna); the Dhamma-follower, one who has attained view, and one liberated by wisdom by way of direct understanding. The terminology shifts as practitioners advance — "follower" names the path-moment itself, while the corresponding fruit-attainment earns a different title — but the underlying temperamental orientation stays constant throughout a single person's progress.

The final revelation — that equanimity toward formations is simply desire for deliverance and reflection under a third name — retroactively unifies the chapter's sixth, seventh, and eighth knowledges into a single continuous arc, exactly as fear, danger, and disenchantment were already shown to be one arc earlier in the chapter. Six of this chapter's nine named knowledges, in other words, resolve into just two genuinely distinct underlying processes once their surface names are set aside — a reminder that the elaborate nine-part scheme, however useful pedagogically, describes one continuous deepening rather than nine discrete achievements.

How? Here, someone, right from the start, adheres to internal formations, and, having adhered, sees them. But since emergence into the path does not occur merely through seeing what is purely internal, the external too must be seen; therefore he sees "impermanent, suffering, not-self" also with respect to another's aggregates and the not-clung-to formations. He comprehends the internal at one time, the external at another. For one comprehending in this way, at the time of comprehending the internal, insight becomes joined together with the path. This is called "having adhered internally, one emerges internally."

But if, for him, at the time of comprehending the external, insight becomes joined together with the path, this is called "having adhered internally, one emerges externally." This same method applies also to emergence internally and externally after having adhered externally.

Another, right from the start, adheres to matter, and, having adhered, sees it, gathering both the great elements and derived matter together into a mass. But since emergence does not occur merely through seeing pure matter, the immaterial too must be seen. Therefore, taking that matter as object, he sees, as "this is the immaterial," the feeling, perception, formations, and consciousness that arise. He comprehends matter at one time, the immaterial at another. For one comprehending in this way, at the time of comprehending matter, insight becomes joined together with the path. This is called "having adhered in matter, one emerges in matter."

But if, for him, at the time of comprehending the immaterial, insight becomes joined together with the path, this is called "having adhered in the immaterial, one emerges in the immaterial." This same method applies also to emergence in the immaterial and in matter after having adhered in the immaterial.

"Whatever has the nature to arise, all that has the nature to cease" — having adhered in this way, and at the very moment of emergence seeing it in just this way, this is called "one emerges, with a single stroke, by way of all five aggregates."

One, right from the start, comprehends the formations as impermanent. But since emergence does not occur merely through comprehending them as impermanent, they must be comprehended as suffering and as not-self as well; therefore he comprehends them as suffering and as not-self too. For one thus practicing, at the time of comprehending as impermanent, emergence occurs. This is called "having adhered as impermanent, one emerges as impermanent."

But if, for him, at the time of comprehending as suffering or as not-self, emergence occurs, this is called "having adhered as impermanent, one emerges as suffering, as not-self." This same method applies to the remaining instances of emergence after having adhered as suffering or as not-self.

And herein, whether one has adhered as impermanent, or as suffering, or as not-self, if emergence occurs as impermanent, then all three such persons abound in resolution, attain the faith faculty, are liberated by the signless liberation, become faith-followers at the moment of the first path, and, in seven instances, are those liberated by faith. But if emergence occurs as suffering, all three such persons abound in tranquility, attain the concentration faculty, are liberated by the desireless liberation, and, in every instance, are body-witnesses; and one among them for whom an immaterial jhāna is the basis becomes, at the highest fruit, one liberated both ways. And if, for them, emergence occurs as not-self, all three such persons abound in enthusiasm, attain the wisdom faculty, are liberated by the void liberation, become Dhamma-followers at the moment of the first path, are, in six instances, those who have attained view, and, at the highest fruit, those liberated by wisdom.

Reading note — Ru-Yi

The final paragraph's observation deserves particular attention, since it slightly complicates what might otherwise look like a tidy one-to-one mapping: a meditator's prior "adherence" (which object insight happened to work with most) does not actually determine which of the seven noble persons they become — only the object of "emergence" (whichever characteristic the mind happens to be attending to at the exact instant of breakthrough) does. Someone who has spent most of their practice adhering to impermanence can still emerge as a body-witness if the actual moment of path-attainment happens to occur while attending to suffering. This distinction between sustained practice and the precise instant of arising is a subtle but important piece of psychological realism: the path itself is not simply the sum of everything that led up to it, but is decisively shaped by its own single, final moment.

The Bat and the Black Snake

Twelve similes now illuminate this insight leading to emergence, spanning knowledges from fear through fruition. First, a bat searching a five-branched tree, branch by branch, for fruit — finding none, giving up her attachment entirely, and flying off to settle in a genuinely fruit-bearing tree elsewhere — maps every stage of the meditator's own search onto her flight. The black snake, already met as the central image of reflection-knowledge, is briefly revisited here to extend the same mapping through conformity, the path, and the fruit.

Ru-Yi Translation

Now, together with the preceding and following knowledges, twelve similes should be understood for the sake of making manifest this insight leading to emergence. Here is their summary list:

"The bat, and the black snake, the house, the cattle, the ogress, the child; hunger, thirst, heat and cold, darkness, and poison."

These similes might fittingly be introduced while standing at any knowledge at all, from the appearance as fear onward. But when introduced at this particular point, everything from the appearance as fear up to fruition-knowledge becomes evident; therefore it was said that they should be introduced right here.

"The bat": a certain bat, it is said, thinking "I shall get a flower or a fruit here," settled in a five-branched honey tree, felt around one branch, and found nothing there fit to be taken, neither flower nor fruit. Just as with the first, so with the second, the third, the fourth. Having felt around the fifth branch too, she found nothing. Thinking, "this tree is truly fruitless; there is nothing here fit to be taken," she gave up her attachment to that tree, climbed up along a straight branch, drew her head out through a gap in the foliage, looked upward, flew up into the sky, and settled in another, fruit-bearing tree.

Herein, the meditator should be seen as like the bat; the five aggregates subject to clinging, as like the five-branched honey tree; the meditator's adherence to the five aggregates, as like the bat's settling there; the meditator's comprehending the matter-aggregate and, finding nothing there fit to be taken, going on to comprehend the remaining aggregates, as like her feeling around one branch after another and, finding nothing fit to be taken, feeling around the remaining branches; the trio of knowledges — desire for deliverance and the rest — belonging to the meditator disenchanted, through seeing the characteristic of impermanence and the rest in all five aggregates, as like her giving up her attachment to the tree, thinking, "this tree is truly fruitless"; conformity-knowledge, as like her climbing up along the straight branch; change-of-lineage knowledge, as like her drawing her head out and looking upward; path-knowledge, as like her flying up into the sky; and fruition-knowledge, as like her settling in another, fruit-bearing tree.

The black-snake simile was already given under reflection-knowledge. In correlating the simile here, the distinction is this: the releasing of the snake is like change-of-lineage knowledge; the man's standing and looking back at the path he had come by, having released it, is like path-knowledge; and his standing, having gone to a place of safety, is like fruition-knowledge.

Reading note — Ru-Yi

The bat simile is the first of these twelve to map the entire nine-knowledge sequence plus conformity, change-of-lineage, path, and fruit all onto a single continuous image, and its choice of creature is apt: a bat searching branch by branch is neither panicked nor despairing, simply methodical, moving on the instant one location proves fruitless rather than lingering out of attachment to the search itself. The five branches of the "honey tree" standing for the five aggregates makes the point with unusual precision — it is not that the tree is actively hostile, like the burning house or the poisonous snake in similes still to come, but simply, disappointingly barren, which is arguably the more common experience of formations for a meditator at this stage: not dramatic danger so much as a quiet, thorough absence of anything worth clinging to.

By cross-referencing rather than re-narrating the black snake, Buddhaghosa signals something important about how these twelve similes function together: they are not twelve independent stories but variations circling the same underlying structure, and a reader who has followed the chapter this far is now expected to supply the earlier narrative from memory and simply register the new correlations — conformity, change-of-lineage, path, and fruit — that this later stage of the chapter is specifically concerned with.

The Burning House and the Stolen Royal Cattle

Two more similes, drawn from thoroughly ordinary domestic and agricultural mishaps rather than exotic dangers: a man who wakes to find his own house ablaze and calmly, swiftly finds his way to safety; and a farmer who, chasing down his cattle after they break loose in the night, realizes with a jolt of fear that he has been driving off the king's own herd rather than his own, and flees before he can be mistaken for a thief.

"The house": a house-owner, it is said, having eaten in the evening, gone up to bed, and fallen asleep, found that the house had caught fire. Waking, and seeing the fire, frightened, thinking, "good indeed if I could get out without being burned," he looked around, saw a way out, went out, and, going swiftly to a place of safety, stood there. Herein: the house-owner's eating and going up to bed and falling asleep is like the ordinary worldling's grasping the five aggregates as "I" and "mine." His moment of fright upon waking and seeing the fire is like the knowledge of appearance as fear, arisen from practicing rightly and seeing the characteristic. His looking around for the way out is like the knowledge of desire for deliverance. His seeing the path is like conformity-knowledge. His going out is like change-of-lineage knowledge. "The cattle": a certain farmer, it is said, having fallen asleep during the night, found that his cattle had broken through the pen and run off. Going there at dawn and looking around, and realizing they had run off, he followed their tracks and saw the king's own cattle. Mistaking them for "my cattle," and driving them off, he realized at daybreak, "these are not my cattle, they are the king's cattle," and, frightened, thinking, "before the king's men seize me as a thief and bring me to ruin and disaster, I shall flee at once," he abandoned the cattle, fled swiftly, and stood in a place free of danger. Herein: his mistaking the king's cattle for "my cattle" is like the ordinary worldling's grasping the aggregates as "I" and "mine." His realizing at daybreak, "the king's cattle," is like the meditator's realizing, by way of the three characteristics, "the aggregates are impermanent, suffering, not-self." His moment of fright is like the knowledge of appearance as fear. His wish to abandon them and go is like desire for deliverance. His abandoning them is like change-of-lineage knowledge. His fleeing is like the path. His standing, having fled to a place free of danger, is like the fruit.

Reading note — Ru-Yi

Both similes turn on a moment of sudden realization about something previously taken for granted: the house-owner never suspected his own house of danger until woken by fire; the farmer never doubted his own ownership of the cattle until daylight revealed the royal brand. This structure precisely mirrors what dissolution-knowledge and the appearance as fear accomplish for the meditator — not the discovery of some new external threat, but the sudden, correct recognition of danger that was always latent in something already considered safely one's own. In both stories, the protagonist's response is notably free of denial or delay: recognition and flight happen almost as a single motion, exactly the decisiveness the chapter has been building toward across fear, danger, disenchantment, and desire for deliverance.

The stolen-cattle simile carries a particularly sharp edge for what it implies about ordinary selfhood: the farmer's cattle were never truly his to begin with — he had simply, in the confusion of night and tracking, picked up someone else's property and assumed it was his own. This is a strikingly literal dramatization of not-self itself: the aggregates a worldling calls "mine" were never actually his in any ultimate sense, and the moment of insight is simply daylight arriving to reveal what was true all along.

The Man and the Ogress; the Woman and the Wrong Child

Reading note — Ru-Yi

The ogress simile is the most viscerally frightening of the whole set, and deliberately so — a companion one has literally shared a bed with, revealed as a corpse-eating demon the instant her true nature is observed unguarded. This is perhaps the sharpest possible image the chapter offers for how radically insight can recontextualize something previously considered intimate and safe, without that thing itself having changed at all: the ogress was always an ogress; only the man's knowledge of her changed.

The mother-and-wrong-child simile stands apart from every other simile in this set for its complete absence of danger, fear, or violence — a gentle, almost domestic misunderstanding, corrected with quiet embarrassment rather than terror. Its inclusion is a deliberate counterweight to the burning house, the ogress, and the stolen cattle: not every correction of a mistaken "mine" need be dramatic or frightening. Sometimes recognizing "this is not mine" is simply a small, undramatic act of honesty, quickly and gracefully performed, which the woman's own lack of fuss — she does not weep or panic, simply sets the child down and returns to where she belongs — models for the meditator's own eventual relationship with equanimity toward formations.

Reading note — Ru-Yi

Unlike the six preceding narrative similes, these final six deliberately abandon story altogether for pure, urgent physical sensation — hunger, thirst, cold, heat, darkness, poison — each one among the most primally motivating states a body can experience. The shift in register is itself the point: by this stage of the chapter, the meditator's orientation toward the supramundane is no longer being explained through comparison to a realization or an escape, as the narrative similes did, but is being characterized as something closer to a physical drive, as involuntary and undeniable as thirst itself. This is what "leaning, inclining, tending toward" (ninna-poṇa-pabbhāra) actually feels like from the inside, the passage suggests — not a considered preference but a bodily urgency.

The closing verse's phrase "does not show himself in any abode of being" (attānaṃ bhavane na dassaye) gives the whole equanimity subhead its final, compact image: a meditator who has genuinely withdrawn in this way no longer presents themselves, in any observable way, as belonging to or invested in any of the nine abodes of beings catalogued throughout this subhead — not a literal physical disappearance, but the complete absence of the clinging that would make any of those abodes feel like home.

How Equanimity Shapes the Path: Jhāna Factors, Enlightenment Factors, and the Four Kinds of Progress

Equanimity toward formations turns out to determine far more than which of the seven noble persons a meditator becomes: it shapes the exact configuration of enlightenment factors, path factors, and jhāna factors the resulting noble path will have, depending on which jhāna (if any) served as its basis — a genuine point of debate among the ancient teachers, three of whose competing theories Buddhaghosa records side by side. It further determines whether progress toward the path is painful or pleasant, and whether the resulting direct knowledge is sluggish or swift — with the Buddha and Sāriputta alone said to have experienced all four paths as uniformly pleasant and swift.

Reading note — Ru-Yi

Buddhaghosa's decision to present three competing elder-teachers' theories side by side, without declaring a single winner, is a striking moment of scholarly restraint within an otherwise highly systematic treatise. Rather than resolving the question of what exactly determines a path's jhāna-factor configuration — the basis-jhāna itself, the specific aggregates insight examines, or the individual's own temperamental inclination — he simply records all three views and notes that, whichever is correct, the preliminary insight leading to emergence remains the operative factor throughout. This is a rare admission that a genuine, unresolved technical debate exists within the tradition he is otherwise so confident in systematizing.

The chameleon-and-ground simile captures the first theory's claim with real precision: a chameleon's color is famous for matching whatever surface it rests on, without the surface itself creating the chameleon — similarly, the basis-jhāna one emerges from does not manufacture the resulting path's characteristics from nothing, but the path, arising in close proximity to that jhāna, simply comes to resemble it. The striking fact that even the Buddha's own attainment of all four paths, and Sāriputta's, were uniformly "pleasant progress, swift direct knowledge," while even an eminent disciple like Mahāmoggallāna experienced painful, sluggish progress for three of his four paths, is offered without any suggestion that this reflects unequal spiritual worth — simply a genuine, acknowledged variation in how different individuals, even among the most accomplished, actually traverse the same path.

Five Reasons for the Path's Name

The subhead closes by explaining, systematically, why the path bears the names it does — void, signless, desireless — offering five distinct, complementary reasons: by its own essential nature (which characteristic the equanimity emerged through), by its opposite (what it has abandoned), by its own quality (what it lacks), by its object (Nibbāna's own void, signless, desireless nature), and by approach (the name insight itself bears being passed on to the path, and the path's name in turn passed on to the fruit).

Ru-Yi Translation

Moreover, the path receives its name by five reasons: by its own essential nature, by its opposite, by its own quality, by its object, or by its approach. For if equanimity toward formations comprehends the formations as impermanent and emerges, it is liberated by the signless liberation. If it comprehends them as suffering and emerges, it is liberated by the desireless liberation. If it comprehends them as not-self and emerges, it is liberated by the void liberation. This is called the naming "by its own essential nature."

Now, since this path, by the contemplation of impermanence, having broken up the compactness of the formations, comes having abandoned the sign of permanence, the sign of stability, the sign of eternity, it is therefore "signless." By the contemplation of suffering, having abandoned the perception of pleasure, and having dried up longing and craving, it comes; hence "desireless." By the contemplation of not-self, having abandoned the perception of a self, a being, a person, and having seen the formations as void, it is "void." This is called the naming "by its opposite."

Since it is void of lust and the rest, it is "void"; since it is without the signs of visible form and the rest, or without the signs of lust and the rest, it is "signless"; since it is without the longing of lust and the rest, it is "desireless" — this is its naming "by its own quality."

And this path is called void, signless, and desireless also because it takes as its object Nibbāna, which is void, signless, and desireless. This is its naming "by its object."

Now, approach is twofold: the approach of insight, and the approach of the path. Herein, the path receives the approach of insight; the fruit, the approach of the path. For the contemplation of not-self is called "void," and the path is void by way of void insight; the contemplation of impermanence is called "signless," and the path is signless by way of signless insight. But this name is not obtained by the method of the Abhidhamma; it is obtained by the method of the Suttanta. For there, it is said that change-of-lineage knowledge, taking signless Nibbāna as its object, becomes itself named "signless," and, standing at the point of approach, gives its name to the path; by this, the path is called "signless." And by the approach of the path, it is indeed fitting that the fruit be called "signless." The contemplation of suffering, having dried up longing with respect to the formations, is thus called "desireless"; the path is desireless by way of desireless insight; and the fruit of the desireless path is "desireless." Thus insight gives its own name to the path, and the path to the fruit — this is the naming "by approach." Thus this equanimity toward formations determines the distinction of liberation.

Knowledge of equanimity toward formations is finished.

Reading note — Ru-Yi

These five reasons are not five competing explanations but five genuinely compatible layers of the same naming, each answerable to a slightly different question: essential nature answers "what characteristic did this path's insight emerge through?"; opposite answers "what wrong perception has this path left behind?"; quality answers "what does this path itself now lack?"; object answers "what does this path perceive?"; and approach answers "where did this very name historically come from?" Taken together, they model a thoroughness Buddhaghosa applies throughout the treatise: a single technical term is rarely given just one justification when several independent lines of reasoning can each be shown to converge on it.

The final "by approach" reason contains an unusually candid piece of textual-critical honesty: Buddhaghosa notes that the "signless" liberation's name cannot actually be derived by strict Abhidhamma reasoning (since insight itself, as chapter twenty-one's earlier discussion of the three doors already established, never fully gives up the sign of the formations) and can only be justified by the looser, more narrative logic of the Suttanta, where change-of-lineage knowledge's own object gets retroactively attributed to the path that follows it. Rather than silently smoothing over this seam between two different explanatory traditions, he names it directly — a small but genuine instance of scholarly transparency about where a piece of standard terminology's justification actually, technically, comes from.

Conformity-Knowledge: The Righteous King

The ninth knowledge, conformity, arises as equanimity toward formations grows sharper still: three moments of impulsion-consciousness — preparation, access, and conformity itself — take the formations as object one final time before the mind descends into the life-continuum and, immediately after, the path arises. A simile of a righteous king agreeing with his judges' verdict, itself in conformity with ancient law, explains why this knowledge is named "conformity": it looks backward toward the eight knowledges already completed, and forward toward the thirty-seven states belonging to enlightenment still to come.

Rājā viya hi anulomañāṇaṃ, aṭṭha vohārikamahāmattā viya aṭṭha ñāṇāni, porāṇo rājadhammo viya sattatiṃsa bodhipakkhiyā. Teneva "saccānulomikañāṇa"nti vuccatīti.

Ru-Yi Translation

For one who cultivates, develops, and practices again and again this knowledge of equanimity toward formations, faith accompanied by resolution arises more powerfully; energy becomes well exerted; mindfulness, well established; the mind, well concentrated; and a sharper equanimity toward formations arises. For him, equanimity toward formations, comprehending the formations as impermanent, or as suffering, or as not-self, thinking "now the path will arise," descends into the life-continuum. Immediately after the life-continuum, mind-door adverting arises, taking the formations as object, by the very same method already followed by equanimity toward formations, as impermanent, or as suffering, or as not-self. Then, immediately after that functional consciousness, which arose having turned away from the life-continuum, following on without interval, there arises, taking the formations as object in that very same way, the first impulsion-consciousness, which is called "preparation." Immediately after that, taking the formations as object in that very same way, there arises the second impulsion-consciousness, which is called "access." Immediately after that too, taking the formations as object in that very same way, there arises the third impulsion-consciousness, which is called "conformity." This is their individual name.

Without distinction, however, all three of these may also be called "repetition," or "preparation," or "access," or "conformity." To what does it conform? To the earlier and the later portions. For it conforms, through its very functioning, to the eight prior insight-knowledges, and to the thirty-seven states belonging to enlightenment above. For, occurring with respect to the formations by way of the characteristic of impermanence and the rest, it conforms, through its very functioning — as if declaring, in meaning: "surely it is only of states possessing rise and fall that rise-and-fall-knowledge saw the rise and fall"; "surely it is only of things possessing dissolution that dissolution-contemplation saw the dissolution"; "surely it is only what is genuinely fearsome that appeared as fear to the appearance-as-fear"; "surely it is only what is genuinely dangerous that danger-contemplation saw as danger"; "surely it is only what deserves to be disenchanted with that disenchantment-knowledge grew disenchanted with"; "surely it is only what deserves to be released that desire-for-deliverance-knowledge arose wishing to release"; "surely it is only what deserves to be reflected upon that was reflected upon by reflection-knowledge"; "surely it is only what deserves to be regarded with equanimity that was regarded with equanimity by equanimity toward formations" — to these eight knowledges, and, because they are to be attained by that very practice, to the thirty-seven states belonging to enlightenment above.

Just as a righteous king, seated at the place of judgment, having heard the judges' verdict, giving up any wrong course, and remaining neutral, approving it, thinking "so let it be," conforms both to their verdict and to the ancient royal law — this should be understood in just the same way. For conformity-knowledge is like the king; the eight knowledges are like the eight judges; the thirty-seven states belonging to enlightenment are like the ancient royal law. Herein, just as the king, saying "so let it be," conforms both to the judges' verdict and to the royal law — in just the same way, this knowledge, arising with respect to the formations by way of impermanence and the rest, conforms, through its very functioning, to the eight knowledges, and to the thirty-seven states belonging to enlightenment above. Hence it is called "knowledge conforming to the truths."

Conformity-knowledge is finished.

Reading note — Ru-Yi

The cognitive-process mechanics here are worth tracing carefully, since they show exactly how the path actually arises moment by moment: equanimity toward formations descends into the life-continuum; mind-door adverting arises; and then three, not two, impulsion-moments pass — preparation, access, and finally conformity itself — each still taking a formation as object, before (as the next subhead will explain) change-of-lineage knowledge finally shifts to Nibbāna and the path proper arises. Conformity is thus the very last moment of ordinary, formation-directed consciousness before the supramundane breakthrough, making its function as a bridge between the two realms exactly right.

The righteous-king simile does real interpretive work beyond mere illustration: a king who simply overrides his judges, or who ignores ancient law in favor of personal whim, would not be "conforming" to anything — he would be asserting independent authority. Conformity-knowledge's entire character is precisely the opposite: it asserts nothing new, adds nothing of its own, but simply ratifies, with full authority, what the eight prior knowledges already established and what the thirty-seven factors of enlightenment already require. This is why the chapter gives conformity-knowledge no new content of its own to discover — its entire function is agreement, not discovery.

Key vocabulary — for Pāli learners
parikamma / upacāra / anuloma

preparation / access / conformity — the individual names for the three successive impulsion-moments preceding the path, each taking the formations as object in the same way as equanimity toward formations itself, with only the third and final moment giving this whole knowledge its chapter-title name.

sattatiṃsa bodhipakkhiyadhammā

the thirty-seven states belonging to enlightenment — a standard canonical set (the four foundations of mindfulness, four right efforts, four bases of power, five faculties, five powers, seven enlightenment factors, and the eightfold path) representing the complete inventory of factors the path draws together, to which conformity-knowledge is said to conform "from above," just as it conforms to the eight prior insight-knowledges "from below."

Ru-Yi Translation

And this conformity-knowledge is the completion of insight leading to emergence, which has the formations as its object. But, in every respect, it is change-of-lineage knowledge that is the true completion of insight leading to emergence. Now, for the sake of freedom from confusion regarding this very insight leading to emergence, this correlation with the suttas should be understood.

That is:

This insight leading to emergence, in the Discourse on the Analysis of the Sixfold Base, was called "the state of not being fashioned out of that," thus: "bhikkhus, depending on the state of not being fashioned out of that, relying on the state of not being fashioned out of that, that equanimity which is grounded in unity, resting on unity — abandon that, transcend that."

In the Discourse on the Simile of the Snake, it was called "disenchantment," thus: "disenchanted, he becomes dispassionate; through dispassion, he is liberated."

In the Discourse to Susīma, it was called "knowledge of the stableness of phenomena," thus: "formerly, Susīma, there is knowledge of the stableness of phenomena; afterward, knowledge of Nibbāna."

In the Discourse to Poṭṭhapāda, it was called "the summit of perception," thus: "perception, Poṭṭhapāda, arises first; afterward, knowledge."

In the Discourse of the Tens, it was called "the factor of exertion for purification," thus: "purification by knowledge and vision of the way is a factor of exertion for purification."

In the Paṭisambhidāmagga, it was called by three names, thus: "desire for deliverance, and the contemplation of reflection, and equanimity toward formations — these states are one in meaning; they differ only in the letter."

In the Paṭṭhāna, it was called by three names, thus: "conformity is a condition for change-of-lineage; conformity is a condition for cleansing."

In the Discourse on the Relay of Chariots, it was called "purification by knowledge and vision of the way," thus: "but, friend, is it for the purpose of purification by knowledge and vision of the way that the holy life is lived under the Blessed One?"

Thus, by these many names, has this insight — leading to emergence, peaceful, purified — been praised by the Great Sage.

One wishing to emerge from the great, fearsome mire of the suffering of saṃsāra should, being of a wise nature, constantly apply himself there in practice.

Reading note — Ru-Yi

This closing cross-reference passage performs the same reassuring function chapter after chapter's closing sections have performed throughout this project: it grounds everything the chapter has just built — nine carefully distinguished knowledges, twelve elaborate similes, five reasons for a path's name — directly in the Buddha's own words across eight separate discourses and two further canonical collections, none of them freshly invented by later commentarial systematization. The sheer variety of registers across these citations is itself instructive: "the state of not being fashioned out of that" is abstract and technical; "disenchanted, he becomes dispassionate" is almost a proverb; "perception arises first, afterward knowledge" reads like a piece of practical psychology; and "is it for the purpose of purification by knowledge and vision of the way that the holy life is lived?" is a direct, urgent question a fellow monk once asked another. One single insight-process, in other words, was significant enough to the earliest tradition to be described, independently, in at least eight quite different idioms.

The chapter's very last line — "should constantly apply himself there in practice" — closes an entire chapter that has, in a real sense, been building the reader's own motivation as carefully as its technical content: fear, danger, and disenchantment were never presented merely as facts to be understood intellectually, but as an emotional and practical urgency meant to be felt and then acted upon. With purification by knowledge and vision of the way now complete — the ninth knowledge, conformity, marking insight's own final preparation — the treatise stands, in its own terms, at the very threshold of the supramundane path itself, with only the actual moment of breakthrough, treated in the next chapter, still to come.

Key vocabulary — for Pāli learners
atammayatā

"the state of not being fashioned out of that" — a + tad (not, that) + maya (made of, consisting of) + tā (abstract noun suffix); a distinctive term from the Saḷāyatanavibhaṅga Sutta for a mind no longer constituted by or identified with its objects, cited here as one of this insight's many canonical names.

saññagga

"the summit of perception" — saññā (perception) + agga (peak, summit); the Poṭṭhapāda Sutta's own term for this same insight, drawn from a discourse specifically concerned with the successive refinement of perception through the meditative attainments up to its very highest point.

Rathavinītasutta

the Discourse on the Relay of Chariots — a Majjhima Nikāya discourse (featuring a dialogue between the elders Sāriputta and Puṇṇa Mantāniputta) that maps out all seven purifications as successive stages of a single journey, exactly the same architecture this entire multi-chapter translation project has been following since chapter eighteen.
