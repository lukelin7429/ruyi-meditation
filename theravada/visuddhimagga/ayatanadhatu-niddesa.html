The Sense Bases and Elements

This page carries Ru-Yi's translation of the Visuddhimagga's chapter 15, Āyatanadhātuniddesa ("The Discussion of the Sense Bases and Elements") — continuing wisdom's ground by turning from the five aggregates to two further classical lenses on the very same reality. The twelve sense bases pair each faculty with its object; the eighteen elements insert the resulting consciousness as a third term. Both are worked out through the same rigorous method — meaning, characteristic, exact number, order, scope, condition, and simile — with the chapter's recurring purpose being to dissolve any lingering sense of a unified, persisting self.

The Sense Bases: Twelve Doorways, Each Named for What It Does

The fifteenth chapter turns wisdom's ground from the five aggregates to a second analytical lens on the very same reality: the twelve sense bases — eye, visible form, ear, sound, nose, smell, tongue, taste, body, tangible object, mind, and mental objects. A summarizing verse promises a seven-part method for understanding them: by meaning, by characteristic, by exact number, by order, by concise and detailed scope, and by how they should be regarded. The commentary opens with meaning, giving each of the twelve a precise etymology rooted in its own function rather than any static property — the eye is "eye" because it sees, in the specific sense of tasting and clarifying visible form; visible form is "form" because it renders (rūpayati), making known what has entered the heart through change of color; the ear hears; sound is uttered; the nose smells; smell signals its own location; the tongue calls forth life; taste is what beings relish; the body is the "coming" (āya) of base, taint-ridden states, "coming" here meaning simply the place where they arise; the tangible is touched; mind understands; and mental objects are so called because they bear their own individual characteristic.

Ru-Yi Translation

"The sense bases": the twelve sense bases are these — the eye-base, the form-base, the ear-base, the sound-base, the nose-base, the smell-base, the tongue-base, the taste-base, the body-base, the tangible-base, the mind-base, and the mental-object-base. Herein: the determination should be known by meaning, characteristic, exact number, order, concise and detailed scope, and by how they should be regarded.

Herein, by way of specific meaning, firstly: it sees (cakkhati), therefore "eye" — the meaning is that it tastes and clarifies visible form. It renders (rūpayati), therefore "form" — the meaning is that, undergoing a change of color, it makes known what has come into the heart. It hears, therefore "ear." It is uttered (sappati), therefore "sound" — the meaning is that it is spoken forth. It smells, therefore "nose." It signals (gandhayati), therefore "smell" — the meaning is that it points to its own substrate. It calls forth (avhayati) life, therefore "tongue." Beings relish it (rasanti), therefore "taste" — the meaning is that they enjoy it. It is the "coming" (āya) of base, taint-ridden states, therefore "body" (kāya) — "coming" here meaning the place of their arising. It is touched, therefore "tangible object." It understands, therefore "mind." They bear their own characteristic, therefore "mental objects" (dhammā).

Reading note — Ru-Yi

This chapter opens exactly where the previous one left off, but with a subtly different lens: rather than defining rūpa, vedanā, saññā, saṅkhāra, and viññāṇa as five internal categories of experience, it now names twelve "doorways" through which experience actually enters and is transacted — the same underlying phenomena, re-sorted by function as sense faculty and corresponding object, six paired doorways in all.

The etymologies given here are uniformly functional rather than descriptive — nothing is named for what it looks like or what it's made of, only for what it does (seeing, hearing, calling forth, being touched) — a small but consistent reminder that this entire analytical tradition treats even the most basic components of experience as defined by their activity, never by any static essence.

Key vocabulary — for Pāli learners
āyatana

a sense base, a "sphere" or "field" of sense activity — the twelve-fold scheme (six internal faculties, six external objects) treated in this chapter as a second complete lens on the same reality already analyzed via the five aggregates.

gandhayati

signals, points to — the verb chosen to etymologize "gandha" (smell), specifically in the sense that a smell "points to" or indicates the presence of its own physical source, even at a distance.

kucchita sāsavadhamma

base, taint-ridden states — kucchita (base, contemptible) + sāsava (with taints) + dhamma (states); the specific class of phenomena etymologically linked to "kāya" (body) via āya, "coming" or "place of arising."

Why "Base" at All? Five Ordinary-Language Senses of the Word

Beyond the specific etymology of each individual base, the commentary now explains the general term "āyatana" itself in two complementary ways. First, non-specifically: mind and mental-factor states arise (āyatanti) at their respective doors and objects, straining and struggling to perform their function; those same states extend (tananti) the very "comings" that produce them; and the whole business leads on (nayanti) the vastly extended suffering of beginningless saṃsāra for as long as it continues unchecked — three verbs, all converging on the single word "āyatana." Second, the commentary reaches for five entirely ordinary uses of this word already current in everyday and canonical language — a dwelling-place (as in "Vāsudeva's abode"), a source or mine (as in "a gold mine"), a meeting-place (as in birds gathering at a delightful grove), a birthplace or region of origin (as in "the southern road is cattle country"), and a reason or cause (as in "reaches eyewitness capability given the right conditions") — and shows how each sense maps naturally onto the eye and its companions: they are where mental states dwell, the mine from which they are drawn, the place where they converge, the very ground of their birth, and the specific reason without which they simply could not exist.

Ru-Yi Translation

But non-specifically, it should be understood as "āyatana" in the sense of extending, of stretching out what has come, and of leading onward what has been extended. For, with respect to the eye and visible form and the rest, mind and mental-factor states, belonging to their respective doors and objects, arise, spring up, strive, and exert themselves by way of their respective functions such as experiencing — this is what is meant. And these states extend, stretch out, that very coming-into-being — this is what is meant. And this suffering of saṃsāra, occurring since time immemorial, exceedingly extended, they lead onward, keep in motion, for as long as it does not cease — this is what is meant. Thus, all these states are called "āyatana" in the sense of extending, of stretching out what has come, and of leading onward what has been extended.

Moreover, "āyatana" should be understood in the sense of a dwelling-place, a mine, a meeting-place, a birthplace, and a cause. For thus, in ordinary usage, in such phrases as "the abode of the lord, the abode of Vāsudeva," a dwelling-place is called "āyatana." In such phrases as "a gold mine, a silver mine," it means a mine. But in the teaching, in such phrases as "the birds resort to that delightful haunt," it means a meeting-place. In such phrases as "the southern road is the birthplace of cattle," it means a place of origin. In such phrases as "one attains eyewitness capability in this or that matter, given the right condition," it means a cause.

And in the eye and the rest, too, those various mind and mental-factor states dwell, since their occurrence depends on them — hence the eye and the rest are their dwelling-place. And in the eye and the rest, they are diffused, since they are dependent on them and take them as object — hence the eye and the rest are their mine. And the eye and the rest are their meeting-place, since they converge there by way of basis, door, and object in each respective case. And the eye and the rest are their place of origin, since they arise there, by way of being their support and object. And the eye and the rest are their cause, since, in their absence, those states too are absent. Thus, by these reasons as well — the sense of a dwelling-place, a mine, a meeting-place, a place of origin, and a cause — these states are called "āyatana," a base.

Therefore, by the meaning already stated: the eye, and that which is a base, is the "eye-base" ... and mental objects, and that which is a base, is the "mental-object-base." Thus, firstly, the determination by meaning should be understood here.

Reading note — Ru-Yi

The commentary's two-track etymology — a purely technical derivation from three obscure verb-senses, followed by five everyday, immediately recognizable uses of the same word borrowed straight from ordinary speech — is a deliberate pedagogical doubling: the specialist meaning is anchored in language everyone already understands, so that a reader unconvinced by the abstract "arising-extending-leading-onward" account can fall back on the far more intuitive image of a dwelling, a mine, a meeting-place, a birthplace, and a cause.

The five-fold everyday sense of "āyatana" quietly encodes a whole miniature theory of how sense faculty and mental event relate: not merely that the eye causes seeing, but that seeing actually lives there, is mined from there, converges there, is born there, and simply could not exist without it — five distinct kinds of dependency compressed into what looks, on the surface, like a single ordinary word.

Key vocabulary — for Pāli learners
ākara

a mine, a source-vein — used in ordinary speech for a gold or silver mine, and applied here to the sense bases as the "mine" from which the associated mental states are, in a sense, continually drawn or extracted.

samosaraṇaṭṭhāna

a meeting-place, a place of convergence — samosaraṇa (a coming-together) + ṭhāna (place); the sense in which the eye and the other bases serve as the specific point where basis, door, and object all converge to produce a mental event.

sañjātidesa

a birthplace, region of origin — sañjāti (birth, origin) + desa (place, region); one of the five ordinary senses of "āyatana," mapped onto the sense bases as the actual location where the corresponding mental states first arise.

Why Exactly Twelve? The Six Consciousness-Groups and Their Doors

Having settled meaning, the commentary moves swiftly through characteristic (simply cross-referenced back to the previous chapter's own definitions, since the eye and the rest have already been fully described there), then addresses a genuinely interesting question: since the eye and the rest are, after all, still just instances of mental and material phenomena already classified as name and form, why not simply call the whole scheme "the mental-object base" and leave it at that, rather than distinguishing twelve separate bases? The answer lies in the determination of doors and objects for the six consciousness-groups: each of the first five kinds of sense-consciousness has exactly one dedicated door and exactly one dedicated object, while the sixth, mind-consciousness, has as its door only a portion of the mind-base (the life-continuum) and, as its object, mental objects specifically not shared with the other five — and it is this six-way differentiation of doors and objects that yields exactly twelve bases, no more and no fewer. The order of teaching then follows a double logic: internally, the eye comes first as the most obvious sense object (visible, impinging), and, externally, that same principle of "greatest benefit" places the ear right behind it, since sight and hearing alone can grasp otherwise unattainable truths; the concise summary reduces all twelve bases to mere name-and-form, while the detailed treatment reveals each one branching into a virtually unlimited variety by condition, destiny, class, and person.

Ru-Yi Translation

"By characteristic": the determination should be understood here by way of the characteristics of the eye and the rest. And their characteristics should be understood in exactly the manner already stated in the discussion of the aggregates.

"By exact number": that is, by their being of just this many. This is what is meant: since the eye and the rest are, after all, simply mental-object states, why, this being so, were twelve bases spoken of, rather than simply saying "the mental-object base" alone? By reason of the determination of the doors and objects for the arising of the six consciousness-groups, this distinction among them arises, and so twelve were stated. For the consciousness-group comprised within the eye-consciousness process has the eye-base alone as its door of arising, and the form-base alone as its object; likewise for the others, each with its own respective pair. But for the sixth, only a portion of the mind-base, called the life-continuum, is its door of arising, and only the mental-object base, which is not shared with the others, is its object. Thus, by reason of the determination of the doors and objects for the arising of the six consciousness-groups, twelve were stated. Thus, the determination by exact number should be understood here.

"By order": here too, among the orders already mentioned — order of arising and the rest — only order of teaching fits. For among the internal bases, the eye-base, having a visible and impinging object, is evident, and was therefore taught first; then the ear-base and the rest, having invisible but impinging objects. Or alternatively: among the internal bases, the eye-base and the ear-base were taught first, being of great benefit as the cause of unsurpassed seeing and unsurpassed hearing; then the three beginning with the nose-base; and, since all five have their own respective domain-objects, the mind-base comes last, while, among the external bases, the form-base and the rest, being the domain of the eye-base and the others, come in between, each in its proper place. Moreover, this order should also be understood by way of the determination of the causes for the arising of consciousness. For it was said: "in dependence on the eye and forms, eye-consciousness arises ... in dependence on the mind and mental objects, mind-consciousness arises." Thus, the determination by order should be understood here.

"By concise and detailed scope": concisely, since the mind-base and a portion of the mental-object base are included under the name "name," and the remaining bases under "form," all twelve bases amount to nothing more than name-and-form. In detail, however, among the internal bases, firstly, the eye-base, by way of its own nature, is simply eye-sensitivity — but, by way of the distinction of condition, destiny, class, and person, it is of virtually unlimited variety. Likewise the four bases beginning with the ear. The mind-base, by way of the distinction of wholesome, unwholesome, resultant, and functional consciousness, is of eighty-nine varieties, or a hundred and twenty-one varieties; but, by way of the distinction of physical basis, mode of practice, and the rest, it is of virtually unlimited variety. The form-, sound-, smell-, and taste-bases are of virtually unlimited variety by way of the distinction of dissimilar conditions and the rest. The tangible-base is of three varieties, by way of the earth-, fire-, and wind-elements; but by way of the distinction of conditions and the rest, it is of many varieties. The mental-object base is of many varieties, by way of the distinction in the intrinsic nature and variety of the aggregates of feeling, perception, and formations, of subtle matter, and of Nibbāna. Thus, the determination by concise and detailed scope should be understood.

Reading note — Ru-Yi

The answer to "why exactly twelve" is a genuinely elegant piece of structural reasoning: rather than justifying the number by appeal to tradition or convenience, the commentary derives it directly from a prior, independently motivated fact — the six consciousness-groups each need their own dedicated door and object to be distinguished at all — so that twelve bases turn out to be the necessary and sufficient consequence of there being exactly six kinds of consciousness, not an arbitrary further choice.

The concise-versus-detailed contrast neatly mirrors the same move made for the five aggregates in the previous chapter: the same underlying reality can be described with radical simplicity (mere name-and-form) or with virtually unlimited granularity (each base branching by condition, destiny, class, and person), and neither description is more "true" than the other — they are simply calibrated to different pedagogical needs.

Key vocabulary — for Pāli learners
viññāṇakāya

a consciousness-group — viññāṇa (consciousness) + kāya (body, group, collection); the six-fold classification (eye-, ear-, nose-, tongue-, body-, and mind-consciousness) whose need for distinct doors and objects directly generates the twelve-fold scheme of sense bases.

dassanānuttariya / savanānuttariya

unsurpassed seeing / unsurpassed hearing — dassana/savana (seeing/hearing) + anuttariya (unsurpassed, supreme); the special benefit cited for why the eye- and ear-bases are taught before the other internal bases, since sight and hearing alone can access certain otherwise unattainable truths.

gocaravisaya

a domain-object, a proper sphere of activity — gocara (pasture, domain) + visaya (object, range); used here to explain why the mind-base, unlike the five physical sense-bases, is taught last, since it alone ranges over the objects proper to all five others as well as its own.

An Empty Village, Marauding Bandits, and Six Wild Animals: How to Regard the Bases

The āyatana section closes with its final, most experientially oriented method — how the sense bases should actually be regarded in meditation. All conditioned bases, the commentary insists, come from nowhere and go nowhere: they possess no prior existence before arising and no continued existence after dissolving, occurring only in dependence on conditions in the interval between. They are, moreover, entirely without striving or activity of their own: the eye and visible form never think, "oh, may consciousness arise from our combination" — consciousness simply arises, as a matter of sheer natural law, whenever eye and form and the rest happen to coincide. Three similes drive the point home with escalating vividness: the internal bases should be seen as an empty village, devoid of anything permanent, beautiful, pleasurable, or self; the external bases as village-raiding bandits, since they assault the internal bases exactly as the canonical text describes the eye being "struck" by pleasing and displeasing forms; and, together, the six internal bases as six different wild animals, each irresistibly drawn to its own proper feeding-ground among the six external bases.

Reading note — Ru-Yi

The "neither coming nor going" analysis performs the same demolition of ordinary continuity-thinking that this treatise has applied to matter and consciousness throughout — a sense base is not a persisting thing that shows up, does its work, and then departs, but a momentary occurrence entirely constituted by its own conditions, present only in the narrow interval between not-yet-existing and no-longer-existing.

The escalation across the three similes — empty village, then raiding bandits, then hungry animals drawn irresistibly to feeding-grounds — moves from a purely negative claim (nothing stable or self exists here) to an actively hostile one (external objects assault internal faculties) to a compulsive, appetite-driven one (the internal senses cannot help but be drawn toward their objects), building a genuinely unflattering but precise picture of ordinary sensory experience as something closer to a battlefield or a hunting-ground than a peaceful exchange.

The Eighteen Elements: A Third Lens on the Same Reality

The chapter's second subheading introduces a third analytical scheme for the very same underlying phenomena: the eighteen elements. Where the twelve sense bases paired each faculty with its object, the elements insert a third term between them — the eye-element, the form-element, and eye-consciousness-element, and so on through all six sense-doors, yielding six triads of eighteen in total. Following the same summarizing verse structure used for the bases, the commentary works through meaning first, giving each individual item's specific sense exactly as before (the eye sees, form renders, eye-consciousness is the eye's own consciousness), then turns to the shared, non-specific meaning of "dhātu" itself: a "dhātu" is that which arranges (vidahati), or that which is borne, upheld (dhīyate) — worldly elements like gold and silver ore likewise "arrange" a manifold product, and living beings, like porters carrying a burden, are "borne along" by this manifold suffering of saṃsāra which these same elements arrange, uphold, and set in place.

Reading note — Ru-Yi

The three-lens structure now fully visible across chapters fourteen and fifteen — aggregates, then bases, then elements — is not redundant repetition but a deliberate methodological choice: each scheme carves the identical underlying reality along a different joint (aggregates by kind of mental/physical function, bases by doorway-and-object pairing, elements by inserting the resulting consciousness as a third term), so that a student who has genuinely understood all three can no longer mistake any single classification for the one true, exhaustive description of experience.

The etymological link between "dhātu" (element) and being "borne" like a burden carried by a porter reframes the entire eighteen-fold scheme in a distinctly unflattering light: these are not neutral descriptive categories but the very mechanism by which saṃsāra's suffering is actively "arranged" and "meted out" to beings, continuing this treatise's consistent refusal to present any part of ordinary experience as innocuous.

Not a Soul, But Not Nothing Either: Elements as Colored Mineral Veins

The commentary adds three further images to deepen the sense of "element." First, a careful philosophical distinction: unlike the imagined, self-existent "self" (attā) of non-Buddhist teachers, which has no genuine intrinsic nature at all, these elements are not fictions — they do bear their own real, intrinsic nature, only without being any kind of soul. Second, a simile drawn from rock formations: just as the world speaks of the varied, richly colored mineral veins running through stone — orpiment, red arsenic, and the rest — as "elements," so too the eye and its companions, equally varied and equally the proper objects of genuine knowledge, are elements in just the same sense. Third, a simile drawn from the body itself: just as the very word "element" is used for the mutually distinct constituent parts of an organic whole (blood, and the like, within the aggregate called "the body"), so too it should be understood for the constituent parts of the aggregate called "a personality" — the eye and its companions, each bounded by its own mutually distinguishing characteristic. And finally, the commentary notes that "dhātu" is simply a term for what is lifeless, citing the Buddha's own deliberate use of an elemental analysis — "this person, monks, is six elements" — specifically to demolish the deeply rooted, long-held perception of a living soul.

The careful distinction drawn at the outset — these elements genuinely "bear their own intrinsic nature," unlike the non-Buddhist self, which "has no intrinsic nature at all" — is a precise, almost paradoxical middle position worth pausing on: this tradition is not denying that anything real exists, only denying that what exists is a persisting, unified soul; the eighteen elements are offered as the real, characterized constituents that remain once the fictional self has been cleared away.

Why Exactly Eighteen? Absorbing Every Other "Element" the Canon Ever Names

Characteristic is again simply cross-referenced to the aggregates chapter, and order follows a cause-and-effect logic distinct from the sense bases: the eye-element and form-element are named first as cause, eye-consciousness-element second as their effect, and so on throughout. The chapter's most demanding technical puzzle then follows: scattered across the discourses and Abhidhamma, dozens of other "elements" are named — the element of radiance, of beauty, of infinite space, of infinite consciousness, of nothingness, of neither-perception-nor-non-perception, of cessation; the elements of sensuality, ill will, and cruelty, and their wholesome opposites; the elements of pleasure, pain, joy, displeasure, and equanimity; elements of initiative, endeavor, and exertion; inferior, middling, and superior elements; the four familiar material elements plus space and consciousness; conditioned and unconditioned elements; and the sweeping phrase "the world of manifold, diverse elements." Rather than expanding the count to accommodate every one of these, the commentary painstakingly shows that each and every one of them is already fully contained within the original eighteen — the element of radiance is simply the form-element under another name; the element of beauty is simply a wholesome-resultant object among form and the rest; the four immaterial attainments and cessation are simply mind-consciousness-element and mental-object-element variously combined or subtracted; the element of sensuality is simply the mental-object-element, or, taken in its fullest canonical sense, all eighteen elements found beneath the highest heaven; and so on, category by category, until every apparent addition dissolves back into the original eighteen without remainder.

Breaking the Sense of a Living Soul — and How Many Actual "Things" Each Element Names

Having shown that eighteen elements absorb every other elemental scheme the canon mentions, the commentary offers a second, complementary reason for exactly this number: beings who hold a deep, long-standing perception of a living soul precisely within the very faculty of consciousness need to see that "consciousness" itself is not a single, unified thing at all, but genuinely differentiated into eye-, ear-, nose-, tongue-, body-, mind-, and mind-consciousness-elements, each dependent on its own conditions and therefore impermanent — and the eighteen-fold scheme is calibrated exactly to accomplish this, tailored to what beings of the relevant disposition are actually capable of absorbing. The chapter then turns to counting exactly how many distinct "things" each element-name actually covers: the eye-element names only a single item, eye-sensitivity itself, while the tangible-element covers three (earth, fire, and wind), eye-consciousness-element covers two (its wholesome- and unwholesome-resultant varieties), the mind-element covers three (five-door-adverting plus the two receiving-consciousnesses), the mental-object-element sweeps up twenty distinct items (the three formless aggregates, sixteen kinds of subtle matter, and the unconditioned element itself), and the mind-consciousness-element, broadest of all, covers a full seventy-six.

Reading note — Ru-Yi

The two reasons given for exactly eighteen elements — absorbing every other canonical elemental scheme, and specifically breaking the perception of a unified soul lodged in consciousness — are not competing explanations but two views of the same design choice: a number small enough to be genuinely learnable, yet fine-grained enough that "consciousness," the single word most likely to be mistaken for a soul, gets split into seven distinct, condition-dependent varieties the moment it is examined closely.

The wildly uneven "how many things" tally — a single item for the eye-element, but seventy-six for mind-consciousness-element — reveals that the eighteen-fold scheme is a classification by function and doorway, not by population size: an element-name marks a structural role in the cognitive process, and some roles (like "eye-sensitivity") admit of only one occupant, while others (like "mind-consciousness") admit of dozens, without this difference threatening the coherence of the eighteen-fold count itself.

Drum and Drumstick, Mirror and Face: How the Eighteen Elements Should Be Seen

The chapter's final two sections address how the elements condition one another and how they should ultimately be regarded. Conditionality is worked out precisely through the entire cognitive process already familiar from earlier chapters — the eye-element conditions eye-consciousness-element by six distinct conditioning relations, the form-element by four, and the whole sequence of adverting, receiving, investigating, determining, and impulsion-consciousness conditions each successive stage exactly as before, with the reminder that not only faculty and object but also light and attention are needed for eye-consciousness to arise, a formula the ancient teachers themselves preserved for each of the six doors. Then, in closing, the treatise supplies its richest cluster of similes yet: the eye-, form-, and eye-consciousness-elements are like a drum-skin, drumstick, and the resulting sound, or a mirror's surface, a face, and its reflection, or sugarcane, a mill, and the extracted juice, or two fire-sticks and the flame they produce together. The mental-object-element's feeling aggregate is like a dart or spear; perception and formations are like one afflicted by that dart, or like an empty fist deceiving a forest deer; formations alone are like men flung into a pit of embers; and the whole conditioned realm, ever pursued by birth as though by a king's men pursuing a thief, is regarded as the seed of every possible misfortune — against which the unconditioned element alone stands as deathless, peaceful, and secure.

"By condition": here, the eye-element, firstly, is a condition for eye-consciousness-element by way of six conditioning relations — dissociation, pre-arising, presence, non-disappearance, support, and faculty. The form-element is a condition by way of four — pre-arising, presence, non-disappearance, and object. As for the five sense-consciousnesses, the adverting mind-element is a condition for them by way of five relations — proximity, contiguity, absence, disappearance, and immediately-preceding support — and all five of these, in turn, are conditions for the receiving mind-element. Likewise, the receiving mind-element is a condition for the investigating mind-consciousness-element; that, for the determining mind-consciousness-element; and the determining mind-consciousness-element, for the impulsion mind-consciousness-element. The impulsion mind-consciousness-element, however, is a condition for an immediately following impulsion mind-consciousness-element by way of those same five relations together with the relation of repetition, making six.

At the mind-door, however, the life-continuum mind-consciousness-element is a condition for the adverting mind-consciousness-element; and the adverting mind-consciousness-element is a condition for the impulsion mind-consciousness-element by the same five relations already stated. And it is not only the eye and visible form and the rest that are conditions for eye-consciousness-element and the rest — light and the other factors are conditions as well. Hence the ancient teachers said: "in dependence on the eye, visible form, light, and attention, eye-consciousness arises. In dependence on the ear, sound, an aperture, and attention, ear-consciousness arises. In dependence on the nose, smell, air, and attention, nose-consciousness arises. In dependence on the tongue, taste, moisture, and attention, tongue-consciousness arises. In dependence on the body, the tangible, the earth-element, and attention, body-consciousness arises. In dependence on the life-continuum, the mind, and mental objects, and attention, mind-consciousness arises."

"By how they should be regarded": the meaning is that the determination should be understood here by way of how they should be regarded. For all conditioned elements should be regarded as devoid of anything prior or posterior, as empty of any stable, beautiful, pleasurable, or self-nature, and as occurring entirely dependent on conditions.

In particular, here, the eye-element should be regarded as like the surface of a drum, the form-element like the drumstick, and the eye-consciousness-element like the resulting sound. Likewise, the eye-element is like a mirror's surface, the form-element like a face, and the eye-consciousness-element like the reflected image of the face. Or again: the eye-element is like sesame seeds and sugarcane, the form-element like the mill and its pressing-pole, and the eye-consciousness-element like the extracted oil and juice. Likewise, the eye-element is like the lower fire-stick, the form-element like the upper fire-stick, and the eye-consciousness-element like the resulting flame. The same method applies to the ear-element and the rest. The mind-element, however, according to how it actually arises, should be regarded as either preceding or following the eye-consciousness-element and the rest.

Within the mental-object-element, the feeling aggregate should be regarded as like a dart or a spear. The perception and formations aggregates should be regarded as like one afflicted by the union of that dart or spear — or, for worldlings, perception should be regarded as like an empty fist, since it produces the suffering of mere craving-for-taste; and, since it grasps a sign that does not correspond to reality, as like a forest deer deceived by a scarecrow. Formations should be regarded as like men who are flung into a pit of embers, since they cast one into rebirth-linking. Birth should be regarded as like a thief pursued by the king's men, on account of the suffering that dogs it. The stream of the aggregates, being the cause of every misfortune, should be regarded as like the seed of a poison tree. Matter should be regarded as like a razor-wheel, on account of being the sign for manifold calamities. The unconditioned element, however, should be regarded as deathless, peaceful, and secure. Why? Because it stands opposed to everything that brings misfortune.

The mind-consciousness-element, on account of its lack of any fixed determination among its objects, should be regarded as like a forest monkey; on account of its being hard to tame, like a wild, untrained colt; on account of its falling wherever it pleases, like a stick thrown up into the air; and, on account of its being yoked to the manifold varieties of defilement such as greed and hatred, like a dancer dressed in costume.

Āyatanadhātuniddesa Complete

With the eighteen elements now fully classified by meaning, characteristic, exact number, order, how many actual things each covers, condition, and how they should be regarded, the fifteenth chapter of the Visuddhimagga comes to its close. Together with the fourteenth chapter's treatment of the five aggregates, this chapter completes wisdom's three parallel analytical lenses on the totality of experience — aggregates, sense bases, and elements — each cutting the same underlying reality along a different, complementary joint: the aggregates by kind of mental and physical function, the bases by doorway and object, and the elements by inserting the resulting consciousness as a third term between them. Both chapters share the same underlying purpose the commentary has now stated explicitly more than once: to dissolve, piece by piece, the deeply rooted perception of a unified, persisting self or living soul, replacing it with a precise, impersonal account of conditioned states arising and passing in strict dependence on one another.

The sixteenth chapter, the Indriyasaccaniddesa ("Discussion of the Faculties and the Truths"), verified from the Visuddhimagga's own table of contents, continues wisdom's ground still further — turning from the bases and elements to two further classical categories, the twenty-two faculties and the Four Noble Truths, completing the full roster of analytical frameworks that must be mastered before the treatise's central seven purifications, beginning with purification of view, can properly be taken up.
