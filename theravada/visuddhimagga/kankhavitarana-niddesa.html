Chapter 19 · Kaṅkhāvitaraṇavisuddhiniddesa · Complete

No Doer, No Experiencer

This page carries Ru-Yi's translation of the Visuddhimagga's chapter 19, Kaṅkhāvitaraṇavisuddhiniddesa ("The Description of Purification by Overcoming Doubt") — the second of the five purifications making up wisdom's "body," following directly on from purification of view. Where chapter eighteen determined what mind-and-matter is, this chapter determines what causes and conditions it, tracing that causal structure back through nine distinct methods: the repulsive circumstances of the body's own conception, the common and uncommon conditions of mind and matter, dependent origination in both forward and reverse order, and the round of kamma and the round of result worked through kamma's own sixteenfold internal classification. It closes with two of the most celebrated passages in the entire treatise — the "ancients'" verses declaring that there is no doer of kamma and no experiencer of its result, only bare phenomena proceeding by way of conditions, and the four classic similes (a pupil's recitation, a sick man's medicine, a mirror's reflection, one lamp lit from another) for how nothing whatsoever transmigrates from one existence to the next, and yet nothing fails to arise in dependence on what came before.

Kaṅkhāvitaraṇavisuddhi Defined; the Physician and the Abandoned Child

The nineteenth chapter opens with a one-sentence definition, exactly as the eighteenth did: purification by overcoming doubt is simply the knowledge that has crossed over doubt about the three time-periods — past, present, and future — by grasping mind-and-matter's causes and conditions. Two similes then motivate the search itself, before a short piece of preliminary reasoning rules out two false starting points: that mind-and-matter has no cause at all, and that it is caused by a creator-god.

Pali Source

Etasseva pana nāmarūpassa paccayapariggahaṇena tīsu addhāsu kaṅkhaṃ vitaritvā ṭhitaṃ ñāṇaṃ "kaṅkhāvitaraṇavisuddhi" nāma.

Taṃ sampādetukāmo bhikkhu yathā nāma kusalo bhisakko rogaṃ disvā tassa samuṭṭhānaṃ pariyesati. Yathā vā pana anukampako puriso daharaṃ kumāraṃ mandaṃ uttānaseyyakaṃ rathikāya nipannaṃ disvā "kassa nu kho ayaṃ puttako"ti tassa mātāpitaro āvajjati, evameva tassa nāmarūpassa hetupaccayapariyesanaṃ āpajjati.

So āditova iti paṭisañcikkhati "na tāvidhaṃ nāmarūpaṃ ahetukaṃ, sabbattha sabbadā sabbesañca ekasadisabhāvāpattito, na issarādihetukaṃ, nāmarūpato uddhaṃ issarādīnaṃ abhāvato. Yepi nāmarūpamattameva issarādayoti vadanti, tesaṃ issarādisaṅkhātanāmarūpassa ahetukabhāvappattito. Tasmā bhavitabbamassa hetupaccayehi, ke nu kho te"ti.

Ru-Yi Translation

Now, the knowledge that stands having crossed over doubt with respect to the three time-periods, by way of grasping the conditions of this very mind-and-matter, is called "purification by overcoming doubt."

A monk desiring to accomplish this, just as a skilled physician, seeing a disease, seeks out its origin — or just as a compassionate man, seeing a helpless infant lying on its back in the street, wonders "whose little child could this be?" and turns his attention to its mother and father — in just the same way undertakes a search for the causes and conditions of this mind-and-matter.

He first reflects thus: "This mind-and-matter is surely not without a cause, since it would then have to be everywhere, always, and for everyone of one and the same nature. Nor is it caused by a creator-god or the like, since apart from mind-and-matter itself no creator-god or the like exists. And as for those who say that a creator-god and the like are themselves nothing but mind-and-matter — for them, that very mind-and-matter reckoned as a creator-god and the like would end up being without a cause. Therefore it must have causes and conditions — now, what are they?"

Reading note — Ru-Yi

The two opening similes work as a matched pair: the physician diagnoses a condition already present in the body before him, while the compassionate man traces an already-existing infant back to its origin. Both point toward the same method this whole chapter will use — starting from mind-and-matter as an established fact and searching backward for what produced it, rather than starting from a theory about origins and searching forward for confirmation.

The refutation of an uncaused or divinely-caused mind-and-matter is compressed into two clean moves. Against "no cause": if mind-and-matter arose without any cause, nothing would explain the manifest differences between beings, since an uncaused thing should be uniform everywhere and always. Against "a creator-god as cause": either the creator-god is something other than mind-and-matter, in which case no such thing can be found to exist, or the creator-god is itself only some form of mind-and-matter, in which case the same question — what caused it? — simply reapplies to the god, and nothing has actually been explained. This same argument-shape, applied to a first uncaused cause, will recur throughout the chapter's remaining methods, all of which insist on a genuinely beginningless, self-sustaining causal structure rather than any fixed starting point.

Key vocabulary — for Pāli learners
kaṅkhāvitaraṇavisuddhi

purification by overcoming doubt — kaṅkhā (doubt) + vitaraṇa (crossing over, overcoming) + visuddhi (purification); the second of the five "body" purifications, defined here as knowledge that has crossed over doubt about the three time-periods by grasping mind-and-matter's causes.

hetupaccaya

"causes and conditions" — hetu (cause, root-cause) + paccaya (condition, supporting circumstance); a standing pair throughout this chapter, with hetu typically marking what actually produces a state and paccaya marking what merely supports or sustains it — a distinction made explicit in the very next section.

issara

a lord, a creator-god — the term used here for any posited divine first cause, whose existence the text rules out on the grounds that nothing exists "above" mind-and-matter, and that any being so described would itself have to be a form of mind-and-matter requiring its own cause.

The Repulsive Origin of the Body: Four Causes and One Condition

The first working method turns to the body's own conception for its answer: far from arising amid anything auspicious, it is generated in the most confined and repellent circumstances imaginable — likened to a worm born in a rotting fish or a cesspool. Four causes (ignorance, craving, clinging, and kamma) and one condition (nutriment) are then identified for the matter-body, with mother, father, and nurse offered as parallel images, before the same search is extended to the mind-body through the standard sense-door formula.

Pali Source

So evaṃ nāmarūpassa hetupaccaye āvajjetvā imassa tāva rūpakāyassa evaṃ hetupaccaye pariggaṇhāti – "ayaṃ kāyo nibbattamāno neva uppalapadumapuṇḍarīkasogandhikādīnaṃ abbhantare nibbattati, na maṇimuttāhārādīnaṃ, atha kho āmāsayapakkāsayānaṃ antare udarapaṭalaṃ pacchato piṭṭhikaṇṭakaṃ purato katvā antaantaguṇaparivārito sayampi duggandhajegucchapaṭikkūlo duggandhajegucchapaṭikkūle paramasambādhe okāse pūtimacchapūtikummāsaoḷigallacandanikādīsu kimiva nibbattati. Tassevaṃ nibbattamānassa 'avijjā taṇhā upādānaṃ kamma'nti ime cattāro dhammā nibbattakattā hetu, āhāro upatthambhakattā paccayoti pañca dhammā hetupaccayā honti. Tesupi avijjādayo tayo imassa kāyassa mātā viya dārakassa upanissayā honti. Kammaṃ pitā viya puttassa janakaṃ. Āhāro dhāti viya dārakassa sandhārako"ti. Evaṃ rūpakāyassa paccayapariggahaṃ katvā, puna "cakkhuñca paṭicca rūpe ca uppajjati cakkhuviññāṇa"ntiādinā nayena nāmakāyassa paccayapariggahaṃ karoti.

So evaṃ paccayato nāmarūpassa pavattiṃ disvā yathā idaṃ etarahi, evaṃ atītepi addhāne paccayato pavattittha, anāgatepi paccayato pavattissatīti samanupassati.

Ru-Yi Translation

Having thus turned his attention to the causes and conditions of mind-and-matter, he first grasps the causes and conditions of this matter-body in this way: "This body, while being generated, is not generated inside blue lotuses, pink lotuses, white lotuses, or fragrant flowers, nor inside gems, pearls, or garlands; rather, it is generated, like a worm, between the stomach and the bowels, with the abdominal membrane behind and the spine in front, hemmed in by intestine upon intestine, itself foul-smelling, disgusting, repulsive, in a place that is foul-smelling, disgusting, repulsive, and utterly confined — like a rotten fish, rotten porridge, a cesspool, or a sewer. For it, being thus generated, these four states — ignorance, craving, clinging, and kamma — are its cause, in the sense of generating it; nutriment is its condition, in the sense of sustaining it; thus these five states are its causes and conditions. Among these, the first three — ignorance and the rest — are, for this body, like a mother to her child, on whom it depends; kamma is like a father, who begets a son; nutriment is like a wet-nurse, who sustains the child." Having thus grasped the causes and conditions of the matter-body, he then grasps the causes and conditions of the mind-body as well, by the method beginning: "dependent on the eye and visible forms, eye-consciousness arises."

Having thus seen the occurrence of mind-and-matter by way of its conditions, he considers: "just as it occurs by way of conditions now, so too it occurred by way of conditions in the past, and it will occur by way of conditions in the future."

Reading note — Ru-Yi

The unsparing description of the womb is a deliberate continuation of the same rhetorical register chapter eleven used for the thirty-two parts of the body and chapter eighteen's own body-analysis: dismantling any romantic or dignified picture of embodied existence is treated as a precondition for seeing its actual causal structure clearly. The mother/father/nurse similes that follow, however, strike a notably gentler note by contrast — ignorance, craving, and clinging are not villains here but simply necessary, if unglamorous, conditions, exactly as a mother is simply necessary and unglamorous from the standpoint of pure biology, however much affection surrounds her in ordinary life.

The hetu/paccaya distinction drawn here (the four mental factors "in the sense of generating," nutriment "in the sense of sustaining") will run through several of the chapter's later methods in a more technical form, particularly the fourfold analysis of matter's conditions in the very next section. It is worth noting that this first method covers only the causes operating within a single lifetime; the extension to past and future lives, briefly asserted in the closing sentence, is what the sixteen kinds of doubt in the next section are specifically about.

Key vocabulary — for Pāli learners
upanissaya

"that on which one depends" — upa + nissaya (support, reliance); used here specifically of the mother-like relationship of ignorance, craving, and clinging to the body they condition, distinguished from kamma's more active, "begetting" role.

sandhāraka

"one who sustains" — saṃ + dhāraka (holder, sustainer); the function assigned to nutriment in the mother/father/nurse simile, paralleling a wet-nurse's role of keeping an already-born child alive rather than producing it in the first place.

The Sixteen Kinds of Doubt Removed

Having seen mind-and-matter's occurrence by way of conditions in the present, and inferred the same for past and future, the meditator abandons all sixteen kinds of doubt named in the Sabbāsava Sutta — five concerning the past, five concerning the future, and six concerning the present — each cast as a first-person question a confused mind might ask about its own existence across time.

Pali Source

Tassevaṃ samanupassato yā sā pubbantaṃ ārabbha "ahosiṃ nu kho ahaṃ atītamaddhānaṃ, na nu kho ahosiṃ atītamaddhānaṃ, kiṃ nu kho ahosiṃ atītamaddhānaṃ, kathaṃ nu kho ahosiṃ atītamaddhānaṃ, kiṃ hutvā kiṃ ahosiṃ nu kho ahaṃ atītamaddhāna"nti pañcavidhā vicikicchā vuttā, yāpi aparantaṃ ārabbha "bhavissāmi nu kho ahaṃ anāgatamaddhānaṃ, na nu kho bhavissāmi anāgatamaddhānaṃ, kiṃ nu kho bhavissāmi anāgatamaddhānaṃ, kathaṃ nu kho bhavissāmi anāgatamaddhānaṃ, kiṃ hutvā kiṃ bhavissāmi nu kho ahaṃ anāgatamaddhāna"nti pañcavidhā vicikicchā vuttā, yāpi paccuppannaṃ ārabbha "etarahi vā pana paccuppannaṃ addhānaṃ ajjhattaṃ kathaṃkathī hoti – ahaṃ nu khosmi, no nu khosmi, kiṃ nu khosmi, kathaṃ nu khosmi, ayaṃ nu kho satto kuto āgato, so kuhiṃ gāmī bhavissatī"ti chabbidhā vicikicchā vuttā, sā sabbāpi pahīyati.

Ru-Yi Translation

For one who considers in this way, all of the doubt is abandoned — both the fivefold doubt stated with reference to the past: "Was I in the past? Was I not in the past? What was I in the past? How was I in the past? Having been what, what did I become in the past?" — and the fivefold doubt stated with reference to the future: "Shall I be in the future? Shall I not be in the future? What shall I be in the future? How shall I be in the future? Having been what, what shall I become in the future?" — and the sixfold doubt stated with reference to the present: "Or else, one is inwardly uncertain about the present time itself: Am I? Am I not? What am I? How am I? Where has this being come from? Where will it go?"

Reading note — Ru-Yi

This is a direct quotation of the Sabbāsava Sutta's own catalogue of the doubts an "unwise" person entertains through improper attention, quoted here to show that the analytical work just performed is not a piece of Abhidhamma refinement invented by commentators, but a direct application of the Buddha's own diagnosis. The asymmetry between the five-item past and future lists and the six-item present list is itself meaningful: the extra item in the present set — "where has this being come from, where will it go?" — is precisely the question that this chapter's whole apparatus of causal grasping is designed to dissolve, by showing that "this being" was never a coherent unit that could meaningfully "come from" or "go to" anywhere in the first place.

Key vocabulary — for Pāli learners
pubbanta / aparanta

the prior end / the further end — standard Pāli terms for the past and future extremities of time, framing the doubt catalogue's first two sets of five questions each.

kathaṃkathī

inwardly uncertain, of two minds — kathaṃ (how?) reduplicated as a noun of state; the term used specifically for present-moment doubt about one's own existence, as opposed to vicikicchā, the more general term for doubt used throughout the rest of the chapter.

Common and Uncommon Conditions of Mind and Matter

A second method sorts mind's conditions into two kinds — the common (the six sense doors and their six objects, shared by every kind of consciousness) and the uncommon (attention and the rest, which determine specifically whether a given mind is wholesome, unwholesome, resultant, or functional) — and matter's conditions into the familiar fourfold scheme of kamma, mind, temperature, and nutriment, each active at its own distinct moment.

Pali Source

Aparo sādhāraṇāsādhāraṇavasena duvidhaṃ nāmassa paccayaṃ passati, kammādivasena catubbidhaṃ rūpassa. Duvidho hi nāmassa paccayo sādhāraṇo asādhāraṇo ca. Tattha cakkhādīni cha dvārāni, rūpādīni cha ārammaṇāni nāmassa sādhāraṇo paccayo, kusalādibhedato sabbappakārassāpi tato pavattito. Manasikārādiko asādhāraṇo. Yoniso manasikārasaddhammassavanādiko hi kusalasseva hoti, viparīto akusalassa, kammādiko vipākassa, bhavaṅgādiko kiriyassāti.

Rūpassa pana kammaṃ cittaṃ utu āhāroti ayaṃ kammādiko catubbidho paccayo. Tattha kammaṃ atītameva kammasamuṭṭhānassa rūpassa paccayo hoti. Cittaṃ cittasamuṭṭhānassa uppajjamānaṃ. Utuāhārā utuāhārasamuṭṭhānassa ṭhitikkhaṇe paccayā hontīti. Evameveko nāmarūpassa paccayapariggahaṃ karoti.

So evaṃ paccayato nāmarūpassa pavattiṃ disvā yathā idaṃ etarahi, evaṃ atītepi addhāne paccayato pavattittha, anāgatepi paccayato pavattissatīti samanupassati. Tassevaṃ samanupassato vuttanayeneva tīsupi addhāsu vicikicchā pahīyati.

Ru-Yi Translation

Another sees the condition of mind as twofold, by way of the common and the uncommon, and the condition of matter as fourfold, by way of kamma and the rest. For the condition of mind is twofold: common and uncommon. Herein, the six doors beginning with the eye, and the six objects beginning with visible form, are mind's common condition, since every kind of mind — wholesome and the rest — occurs dependent on them. That beginning with attention is the uncommon condition: for wise attention and the hearing of the true Dhamma and the like belong only to the wholesome; their opposites, to the unwholesome; kamma and the rest, to the resultant; the life-continuum and the rest, to the functional.

As for matter, kamma, mind, temperature, and nutriment — this is its fourfold condition, beginning with kamma. Herein, only past kamma is a condition for kamma-originated matter. Mind is a condition for mind-originated matter, at the moment of its own arising. Temperature and nutriment are conditions for temperature-originated and nutriment-originated matter, at the moment of their persisting. This is how one such person grasps the conditions of mind-and-matter.

Having thus seen the occurrence of mind-and-matter by way of its conditions, he considers: "just as it occurs by way of conditions now, so too it occurred by way of conditions in the past, and it will occur by way of conditions in the future." For one who considers in this way, doubt with respect to all three time-periods is abandoned, by the method already stated.

Reading note — Ru-Yi

The common/uncommon distinction for mind is a genuinely useful piece of psychological mapping: the six doors and six objects explain why any mental event occurs at all, but say nothing about its ethical quality — a wholesome and an unwholesome mind can arise from exactly the same eye-and-visible-form contact. What actually determines whether the resulting mind is wholesome, unwholesome, resultant, or functional is the second, "uncommon" layer of conditions — above all, wise or unwise attention (yoniso/ayoniso manasikāra), the single factor this treatise elsewhere identifies as the hinge on which an entire moment's ethical character turns.

Matter's kamma-originated component is a condition only as past kamma — never present kamma — which is worth pausing on: it means that whatever matter is arising in this very moment as a result of kamma was already determined by an action performed at some earlier time, never by anything happening simultaneously with it. Mind-originated, temperature-originated, and nutriment-originated matter, by contrast, are each conditioned by a cause operating at essentially the same time as the effect — arising for mind-originated matter, persisting for the other two — a small but precise piece of Abhidhamma bookkeeping that distinguishes kamma sharply from the other three origins of matter.

Key vocabulary — for Pāli learners
sādhāraṇa / asādhāraṇa

common / uncommon — sādhāraṇa (shared, general) and a + sādhāraṇa (not shared, specific); the chapter's own terms for the two layers of mind's conditions, the first shared by all mental events regardless of ethical quality, the second determining that quality specifically.

yoniso manasikāra

wise attention — yoniso (by way of the origin, thoroughly, wisely) + manasikāra (attention, bringing to mind); the single uncommon condition singled out here as what makes a mind wholesome, its opposite (ayoniso manasikāra) being what makes a mind unwholesome.

ṭhitikkhaṇa

"the moment of persisting" — ṭhiti (standing, persistence) + khaṇa (moment); one of the three moments (arising, persisting, and dissolution) into which the Abhidhamma divides every conditioned phenomenon's lifespan, specified here as the particular moment at which temperature and nutriment exert their conditioning influence on matter.

The Reverse and Forward Orders of Dependent Origination

Two further methods apply the full twelve-factor formula of dependent origination — already treated at exhaustive length in chapter seventeen — specifically as a way of grasping mind-and-matter's conditions: first traced in reverse, from aging-and-death back to ignorance, then confirmed again in forward order, from ignorance forward to aging-and-death.

Pali Source

Aparo tesaṃyeva nāmarūpasaṅkhātānaṃ saṅkhārānaṃ jarāpattiṃ jiṇṇānañca bhaṅgaṃ disvā idaṃ saṅkhārānaṃ jarāmaraṇaṃ nāma jātiyā sati hoti, jāti bhave sati, bhavo upādāne sati, upādānaṃ taṇhāya sati, taṇhā vedanāya sati, vedanā phasse sati, phasso saḷāyatane sati, saḷāyatanaṃ nāmarūpe sati, nāmarūpaṃ viññāṇe sati, viññāṇaṃ saṅkhāresu sati, saṅkhārā avijjāya satīti evaṃ paṭilomapaṭiccasamuppādavasena nāmarūpassa paccayapariggahaṃ karoti. Athassa vuttanayeneva vicikicchā pahīyati.

Aparo "iti kho avijjāpaccayā saṅkhārā"ti pubbe vitthāretvā dassitaanulomapaṭiccasamuppādavaseneva nāmarūpassa paccayapariggahaṃ karoti. Athassa vuttanayeneva kaṅkhā pahīyati.

Ru-Yi Translation

Another, having seen these very formations reckoned as mind-and-matter reaching old age, and the breaking-up of what has aged, reflects: "this aging-and-death of the formations occurs when birth is present; birth, when being is present; being, when clinging is present; clinging, when craving is present; craving, when feeling is present; feeling, when contact is present; contact, when the sixfold base is present; the sixfold base, when mind-and-matter is present; mind-and-matter, when consciousness is present; consciousness, when the formations are present; the formations, when ignorance is present" — thus grasping the conditions of mind-and-matter by way of dependent origination in reverse order. Then, by the method already stated, his doubt is abandoned.

Another grasps the conditions of mind-and-matter by way of dependent origination in forward order alone, exactly as already shown in detail earlier: "thus, dependent on ignorance are the formations…" Then, by the method already stated, his doubt is abandoned.

Reading note — Ru-Yi

These two brief paragraphs are easy to read past, but they are doing something methodologically important: rather than re-deriving dependent origination from scratch, Buddhaghosa simply points back to chapter seventeen's already-completed treatment and slots it in as two more items on this chapter's growing list of ways to grasp mind-and-matter's conditions. The reverse-order version is given first and in full, starting from the most immediately obvious fact — that the formations age and break up — and working backward to their most hidden root; the forward-order version is then dispatched in a single sentence, since chapter seventeen already gave it in exhaustive detail. The chapter's interest here is not in dependent origination's content, already established, but in dependent origination's use as one tool among several for overcoming doubt specifically.

Key vocabulary — for Pāli learners
paṭilomapaṭiccasamuppāda / anulomapaṭiccasamuppāda

dependent origination in reverse order / in forward order — paṭiloma (against the grain, reverse) and anuloma (with the grain, forward) + paṭiccasamuppāda; the two standard directions for reciting the twelve-factor formula, already introduced in chapter seventeen and here applied specifically as two distinct methods of paccayapariggaha.

The Round of Kamma and the Round of Result

A further method returns to the same three-connection, twenty-mode structure that closed chapter seventeen's own treatment of dependent origination — quoted here again, in full, directly from the Paṭisambhidāmagga — but now framed specifically as "the round of kamma and the round of result": five factors from a previous kamma-existence conditioning present rebirth-linking, five factors of the present rebirth-existence that are themselves the result of that past kamma, and five factors of the present kamma-existence conditioning a future rebirth-linking in turn.

Pali Source

Aparo "purimakammabhavasmiṃ moho avijjā, āyūhanā saṅkhārā, nikanti taṇhā, upagamanaṃ upādānaṃ, cetanā bhavoti ime pañca dhammā purimakammabhavasmiṃ idha paṭisandhiyā paccayā, idha paṭisandhi viññāṇaṃ, okkanti nāmarūpaṃ, pasādo āyatanaṃ, phuṭṭho phasso, vedayitaṃ vedanāti ime pañca dhammā idhūpapattibhavasmiṃ purekatassa kammassa paccayā. Idha paripakkattā āyatanānaṃ moho avijjā…pe… cetanā bhavoti ime pañca dhammā idha kammabhavasmiṃ āyatiṃ paṭisandhiyā paccayā"ti evaṃ kammavaṭṭavipākavaṭṭavasena nāmarūpassa paccayapariggahaṃ karoti.

Ru-Yi Translation

Another grasps the conditions of mind-and-matter by way of the round of kamma and the round of result, thus: "In the previous kamma-process of becoming: delusion is ignorance, accumulating is the formations, relishing is craving, approaching is clinging, volition is being — these five states, in the previous kamma-process of becoming, are conditions for rebirth-linking here. Here, rebirth-linking is consciousness, descent [into the womb] is mind-and-matter, sensitivity is the sense bases, what is touched is contact, what is felt is feeling — these five states, in the present rebirth-process of becoming, are conditions for the kamma already performed. Here, on account of the maturity of the sense bases, delusion is ignorance … and so on … volition is being — these five states, in the present kamma-process of becoming, are conditions for rebirth-linking in the future."

Reading note — Ru-Yi

This is the same Paṭisambhidāmagga passage already quoted at length in chapter seventeen's closing "wheel of becoming" section (five-past-causes, five-present-effects, five-present-causes, five-future-effects), reused here for a distinct purpose. In chapter seventeen it served to demonstrate dependent origination's own internal structure — the two roots, three connections, four sections, and twenty modes that make up the whole wheel. Here, that same structure is put to work specifically as one more instrument for overcoming doubt: seeing exactly which five factors bridge a past existence into this one, and exactly which five factors bridge this existence into the next, closes off any residual uncertainty about how continuity across lives could possibly work without a self doing the crossing over — a question this same chapter will return to, with four vivid similes, near its close.

Key vocabulary — for Pāli learners
kammavaṭṭa / vipākavaṭṭa

the round of kamma / the round of result — vaṭṭa (a circle, a round, a turning); the two halves of the causal cycle this method distinguishes, kamma-factors (ignorance, formations, craving, clinging, being) versus result-factors (consciousness, mind-and-matter, the sense bases, contact, feeling), alternating across the boundary of birth and death.

kammabhava / upapattibhava

kamma-process of becoming / rebirth-process of becoming — bhava (becoming, existence) qualified by kamma (action) or upapatti (arising, rebirth); the two aspects under which a single moment of existence can be described, either as the accumulating kammic activity that will produce a future result, or as the resultant existence itself that has already arisen from past kamma.

Four Kinds of Kamma by Time of Ripening

With the round of kamma now named as a distinct method, the chapter pauses to unpack kamma's own internal classification in detail, beginning with a fourfold scheme organized entirely around timing: kamma that ripens within the very life that performs it, kamma that ripens in the immediately following life, kamma that ripens whenever it finds an opportunity across the whole future course of saṃsāra, and kamma that has, in effect, expired.

Pali Source

Tattha catubbidhaṃ kammaṃ – diṭṭhadhammavedanīyaṃ, upapajjavedanīyaṃ, aparāpariyavedanīyaṃ, ahosikammanti. Tesu ekajavanavīthiyaṃ sattasu cittesu kusalā vā akusalā vā paṭhamajavanacetanā diṭṭhidhammavedanīyakammaṃ nāma. Taṃ imasmiññeva attabhāve vipākaṃ deti. Tathā asakkontaṃ pana "ahosikammaṃ nāhosi kammavipāko, na bhavissati kammavipāko, natthi kammavipāko"ti imassa tikassa vasena ahosikammaṃ nāma hoti. Atthasādhikā pana sattamajavanacetanā upapajjavedanīyakammaṃ nāma. Taṃ anantare attabhāve vipākaṃ deti. Tathā asakkontaṃ vuttanayeneva ahosikammaṃ nāma hoti. Ubhinnaṃ antare pañca javanacetanā aparāpariyavedanīyakammaṃ nāma. Taṃ anāgate yadā okāsaṃ labhati, tadā vipākaṃ deti. Sati saṃsārappavattiyā ahosikammaṃ nāma na hoti.

Ru-Yi Translation

Herein, kamma is fourfold: to be experienced in this very life, to be experienced upon rebirth, to be experienced in some life still further on, and lapsed kamma. Among these, in a single cognitive process of impulsion, of the seven moments of consciousness, the wholesome or unwholesome volition of the first impulsion is called kamma to be experienced in this very life. It gives its result within this very existence. Failing that, it becomes lapsed kamma, by way of the threefold formula: "lapsed kamma — the result of the kamma did not occur; the result of the kamma will not occur; there is no result of the kamma." The volition of the seventh impulsion, however, being the one that brings the process to completion, is called kamma to be experienced upon rebirth. It gives its result in the very next existence. Failing that, it likewise becomes lapsed kamma, by the method already stated. The volition of the five impulsions in between is called kamma to be experienced in some life still further on. It gives its result whenever, in the future, it finds an opportunity. As long as the round of rebirth continues, it never becomes lapsed kamma.

Reading note — Ru-Yi

This classification maps kamma's ripening window directly onto the seven-moment "impulsion" (javana) phase of a single cognitive process — the brief stretch of moral or immoral volitional activity that the Abhidhamma identifies within every act of full cognition. The first of the seven volitions carries the least kammic force and so must ripen quickly, within this very life, or not at all; the seventh, at the opposite end, carries a middling but still-time-limited force, ripening in the next life or not at all; while the middle five carry an indefinitely patient force, capable of ripening at any point across the entire remaining span of saṃsāra until it finally finds its opportunity. Only for the first two categories can kamma actually "expire" unripened — the middle category, by construction, never lapses as long as rebirth continues, which is precisely why it is the most consequential of the three from an ordinary being's perspective.

Key vocabulary — for Pāli learners
javana

impulsion — from javati (to speed, to run); the seven-moment phase of a cognitive process in which kammically active (wholesome or unwholesome) volition occurs, whose seven positions (first, seventh, and the middle five) map directly onto this section's threefold kamma classification.

ahosikamma

lapsed kamma — ahosi (it was, past tense of "to be") + kamma; kamma whose window for producing a result has closed without that result ever occurring, described by its own canonical threefold formula: "the result did not occur, will not occur, does not exist."

Kamma by Weight, Habit, Proximity, and Registration — and by Function

Two further fourfold classifications of kamma follow in quick succession: first, the well-known scheme governing which kamma actually ripens first when several compete — weighty, habitual, near-death, and merely-performed kamma, in that order of priority — and second, a functional scheme distinguishing kamma that produces a result outright from kamma that only supports, obstructs, or destroys another kamma's result. A closing remark notes that only a Buddha can trace every interaction among these twelve kinds in full detail.

Pali Source

Aparampi catubbidhaṃ kammaṃ – yaṃ garukaṃ, yaṃ bahulaṃ, yadāsannaṃ, kaṭattā vā pana kammanti. Tattha kusalaṃ vā hotu akusalaṃ vā, garukāgarukesu yaṃ garukaṃ mātughātādikammaṃ vā mahaggatakammaṃ vā, tadeva paṭhamaṃ vipaccati. Tathā bahulābahulesupi yaṃ bahulaṃ hoti susīlyaṃ vā dussīlyaṃ vā, tadeva paṭhamaṃ vipaccati. Yadāsannaṃ nāma maraṇakāle anussaritakammaṃ. Yañhi āsannamaraṇo anussarituṃ sakkoti, teneva upapajjati. Etehi pana tīhi muttaṃ punappunaṃ laddhāsevanaṃ kaṭattā vā pana kammaṃ nāma hoti, tesaṃ abhāve taṃ paṭisandhiṃ ākaḍḍhati.

Aparampi catubbidhaṃ kammaṃ – janakaṃ, upatthambhakaṃ, upapīḷakaṃ, upaghātakanti. Tattha janakaṃ nāma kusalampi hoti akusalampi. Taṃ paṭisandhiyampi pavattepi rūpārūpavipākakkhandhe janeti. Upatthambhakaṃ pana vipākaṃ janetuṃ na sakkoti, aññena kammena dinnāya paṭisandhiyā janite vipāke uppajjamānakasukhadukkhaṃ upatthambheti, addhānaṃ pavatteti. Upapīḷakaṃ aññena kammena dinnāya paṭisandhiyā janite vipāke uppajjamānakasukhadukkhaṃ pīḷeti bādhati, addhānaṃ pavattituṃ na deti. Upaghātakaṃ pana sayaṃ kusalampi akusalampi samānaṃ aññaṃ dubbalakammaṃ ghātetvā tassa vipākaṃ paṭibāhitvā attano vipākassa okāsaṃ karoti. Evaṃ pana kammena kate okāse taṃ vipākaṃ uppannaṃ nāma vuccati.

Iti imesaṃ dvādasannaṃ kammānaṃ kammantarañceva vipākantarañca buddhānaṃ kammavipākañāṇasseva yāthāvasarasato pākaṭaṃ hoti, asādhāraṇaṃ sāvakehi. Vipassakena pana kammantarañca vipākantarañca ekadesato jānitabbaṃ. Tasmā ayaṃ mukhamattadassanena kammaviseso pakāsitoti.

Ru-Yi Translation

Kamma is also fourfold in another way: weighty kamma, habitual kamma, kamma near death, and kamma performed merely once. Herein, whether wholesome or unwholesome, whichever is weighty among the weighty and the not-weighty — such as the killing of one's mother and the like, or exalted kamma — that alone ripens first. Likewise, whichever is habitual among the habitual and the not-habitual — whether good conduct or bad conduct — that alone ripens first. Kamma near death is kamma recollected at the time of dying; for whatever a dying person is able to recollect, by that very kamma he takes rebirth. But kamma that, excluded from these three, has been acquired again and again through repeated practice, is called "kamma performed"; in the absence of the other three, it is this that draws on rebirth-linking.

Kamma is also fourfold in yet another way: producing, supporting, obstructing, and destroying. Herein, producing kamma may be either wholesome or unwholesome; it produces the resultant material and immaterial aggregates both at rebirth-linking and in the course of life. Supporting kamma, however, is unable to produce a result; rather, it supports, and prolongs the continuance of, whatever pleasure or pain arises in the result produced by rebirth-linking given by another kamma. Obstructing kamma afflicts and hinders whatever pleasure or pain arises in the result produced by rebirth-linking given by another kamma, and does not allow it to continue. Destroying kamma, however, whether itself wholesome or unwholesome, destroys some other, weaker kamma, blocks its result, and makes an opportunity for its own result; and a result that has arisen in an opportunity made in this way is said to have "sprung up."

Thus the distinctions among these twelve kinds of kamma, and among their results, become evident, according to their own true nature, only to a Buddha's own knowledge of kamma and its result; they are not shared even by disciples. A practitioner of insight, however, need only know the distinctions of kamma and of result in part. Therefore, this particular treatment of kamma has been set forth here only by way of a bare outline.

Reading note — Ru-Yi

The weighty/habitual/near-death/registering priority order is one of the most practically consequential teachings in the entire treatise, since it directly explains why Buddhist cultures place such emphasis on a person's state of mind at the moment of death: absent any weighty or deeply habitual kamma dominating the field, it is specifically whatever kamma a dying person happens to recollect that determines the next rebirth — which is exactly why friends and family are traditionally encouraged to remind a dying person of their good deeds, or to create supportive conditions (a familiar chant, a revered image) at the bedside. The fourth category, "kamma performed," functions as a default: it takes over only when none of the first three are present to override it.

The producing/supporting/obstructing/destroying scheme then adds a second, independent axis to this picture — even after a particular kamma has determined a rebirth and started producing results, other kamma can still modulate that unfolding result without displacing it entirely (supporting or obstructing it) or can intervene decisively enough to redirect it altogether (destroying it and substituting its own result). Buddhaghosa's closing admission — that tracking all twelve kinds of kamma in interaction is a Buddha's exclusive province, with even accomplished disciples managing only a partial view — is a notably candid moment of epistemic humility placed at the very center of the chapter's most technical material.

Key vocabulary — for Pāli learners
garuka / bahula / āsanna / kaṭattā

weighty / habitual / near [death] / performed — the four Pāli terms naming this section's priority-ordered kamma classification, garuka including such extreme cases as matricide (mātughāta) at one end and the meditative attainments (mahaggata-kamma) at the other, since both carry unusually strong kammic weight.

janaka / upatthambhaka / upapīḷaka / upaghātaka

producing / supporting / obstructing / destroying — the four functional roles kamma can play with respect to a result, only the first of which actually generates resultant aggregates; the remaining three all act upon a result some other kamma has already produced.

mukhamattadassana

"a bare-outline showing" — mukha (face, opening, i.e. an introductory glimpse) + matta (mere) + dassana (showing); Buddhaghosa's own characterization of how much of this twelvefold kamma-classification he has actually presented, explicitly marking it as a summary rather than the exhaustive treatment only a Buddha's knowledge could provide.

Gathering the Twelvefold Kamma; No Agent, No Experiencer

The three fourfold kamma-classifications just given are now gathered back into the single "round of kamma and round of result" method, extended to past and future exactly as the chapter's earlier methods were, and sealed with a short verse. What follows is one of the chapter's key conclusions: seeing this entire causal fabric leaves no room for an agent standing above the cause, or an experiencer standing above the result — "agent" and "experiencer" are merely the wise world's own convenient way of speaking when a cause, or a result, happens to be present.

Pali Source

Iti imaṃ dvādasavidhaṃ kammaṃ kammavaṭṭe pakkhipitvā evaṃ eko kammavaṭṭavipākavaṭṭavasena nāmarūpassa paccayapariggahaṃ karoti. So evaṃ kammavaṭṭavipākavaṭṭavasena paccayato nāmarūpassa pavattiṃ disvā "yathā idaṃ etarahi, evaṃ atītepi addhāne kammavaṭṭavipākavaṭṭavasena paccayato pavattittha, anāgatepi kammavaṭṭavipākavaṭṭavaseneva paccayato pavattissatī"ti. Iti kammañceva kammavipāko ca, kammavaṭṭañca vipākavaṭṭañca, kammapavattañca vipākapavattañca, kammasantati ca vipākasantati ca, kiriyā ca kiriyāphalañca.

"Kammā vipākā vattanti, vipāko kammasambhavo; Kammā punabbhavo hoti, evaṃ loko pavattatī"ti.

Samanupassati. Tassevaṃ samanupassato yā sā pubbantādayo ārabbha "ahosiṃ nu kho aha"ntiādinā nayena vuttā soḷasavidhā vicikicchā, sā sabbā pahīyati. Sabbabhavayonigatiṭṭhitinivāsesu hetuphalasambandhavasena pavattamānaṃ nāmarūpamattameva khāyati. So neva kāraṇato uddhaṃ kārakaṃ passati, na vipākappavattito uddhaṃ vipākapaṭisaṃvedakaṃ. Kāraṇe pana sati "kārako"ti, vipākappavattiyā sati "paṭisaṃvedako"ti samaññāmattena paṇḍitā voharanticcevassa sammappaññāya sudiṭṭhaṃ hoti.

Ru-Yi Translation

Thus, placing this twelvefold kamma within the round of kamma, one such person grasps the conditions of mind-and-matter by way of the round of kamma and the round of result. Having thus seen the occurrence of mind-and-matter by way of conditions, through the round of kamma and the round of result, he considers: "just as it occurs by way of conditions now, through the round of kamma and the round of result, so too it occurred by way of conditions in the past, through the round of kamma and the round of result, and it will occur by way of conditions in the future, through the round of kamma and the round of result alone." Thus: kamma, and the result of kamma; the round of kamma, and the round of result; the occurrence of kamma, and the occurrence of result; the continuity of kamma, and the continuity of result; the act, and the fruit of the act —

"Kammas and results proceed; the result has its origin in kamma; from kamma comes renewed existence — thus does the world proceed" —

— he considers. For one who considers in this way, all sixteen kinds of doubt, stated by the method beginning "was I in the past?" with reference to the past and the rest, are abandoned. Throughout every mode of being, every womb, every destination, every station, and every abode, only mind-and-matter appears, occurring by way of the connection of cause and effect. He sees no agent over and above the cause, and no experiencer over and above the occurrence of the result. Rather, it is well seen by his right wisdom that the wise speak merely by way of convention — "an agent," when a cause is present; "an experiencer," when the occurrence of a result is present.

Reading note — Ru-Yi

This section functions as the hinge of the entire chapter: everything before it has been building toward exactly this conclusion, and everything after it — the ancients' verses immediately following, and the non-transmigration similes further on — will simply restate and reinforce the same point from different angles. "An agent," "an experiencer" are not being declared meaningless as ordinary words; they remain perfectly serviceable descriptions the wise themselves continue to use. What is being denied is only their treatment as names for something existing over and above the cause-and-effect process itself — precisely the same move chapter eighteen's chariot and house similes made for "a being," now applied specifically to the doing and the experiencing of kamma.

Key vocabulary — for Pāli learners
kāraka / paṭisaṃvedaka

an agent / an experiencer — kāraka (doer, from karoti, to do) and paṭisaṃvedaka (one who feels or experiences, from paṭisaṃvedeti); the two roles this passage explicitly denies as realities existing "above" cause and result, while affirming them as the wise world's own conventional manner of speech.

samaññāmatta

"by mere convention" — samaññā (designation, convention) + matta (mere, only); the qualifier marking exactly how "agent" and "experiencer" remain valid — as convenient conventional labels, not as claims about an independently existing doer or feeler.

The Ancients' Verses: No Doer of Kamma, No Experiencer of Result

The chapter's most celebrated passage now follows: ten verses of "the ancients," building from the bare declaration that there is no doer of kamma and no experiencer of its result, through the beginninglessness of the kamma-and-result cycle, the sixty-two wrong views that arise from misunderstanding it, and the fire-from-its-conditions simile — fire is found neither inside the sun, nor a gem, nor cow-dung, nor apart from them, and yet is born from their combination, exactly as a kammic result is found neither inside its kamma nor outside it, and yet is produced in dependence on it — closing with the flat denial that any god or Brahmā stands behind this round of existence at all.

Pali Source

Tenāhu porāṇā –

"Kammassa kārako natthi, vipākassa ca vedako; Suddhadhammā pavattanti, evetaṃ sammadassanaṃ.

"Evaṃ kamme vipāke ca, vattamāne sahetuke; Bījarukkhādikānaṃva, pubbā koṭi na nāyati; Anāgatepi saṃsāre, appavattaṃ na dissati.

"Etamatthaṃ anaññāya, titthiyā asayaṃvasī; Sattasaññaṃ gahetvāna, sassatucchedadassino; Dvāsaṭṭhidiṭṭhiṃ gaṇhanti, aññamaññavirodhitā.

"Diṭṭhibandhanabaddhā te, taṇhāsotena vuyhare; Taṇhāsotena vuyhantā, na te dukkhā pamuccare.

"Evametaṃ abhiññāya, bhikkhu buddhassa sāvako; Gambhīraṃ nipuṇaṃ suññaṃ, paccayaṃ paṭivijjhati.

"Kammaṃ natthi vipākamhi, pāko kamme na vijjati; Aññamaññaṃ ubho suññā, na ca kammaṃ vinā phalaṃ.

"Yathā na sūriye aggi, na maṇimhi na gomaye; Na tesaṃ bahi so atthi, sambhārehi ca jāyati.

"Tathā na anto kammassa, vipāko upalabbhati; Bahiddhāpi na kammassa, na kammaṃ tattha vijjati.

"Phalena suññaṃ taṃ kammaṃ, phalaṃ kamme na vijjati; Kammañca kho upādāya, tato nibbattate phalaṃ.

"Na hettha devo brahmā vā, saṃsārassatthikārako; Suddhadhammā pavattanti, hetusambhārapaccayā"ti.

Ru-Yi Translation

Hence the ancients said:

"There is no doer of the deed, nor anyone who experiences its result; bare phenomena alone proceed — this is right seeing.

"Thus, while kamma and result, together with their cause, are proceeding — as with a seed and a tree and the like — no first beginning is known; and in the future course of saṃsāra as well, no cessation of this occurrence is seen.

"Not understanding this meaning, sectarians, not masters of themselves, having taken up the perception of a being, seeing either eternity or annihilation, take hold of the sixty-two wrong views, contradicting one another.

"Bound by the bond of views, they are swept along by the stream of craving; and being swept along by the stream of craving, they are not released from suffering.

"Having thus directly known this, a bhikkhu, a disciple of the Buddha, penetrates the condition, which is profound, subtle, and void.

"Kamma is not within the result, nor is the ripening found within the kamma; each is void of the other, and yet there is no fruit without the kamma.

"Just as fire is not in the sun, nor in a gem, nor in cow-dung, nor does it exist apart from them, and yet it is born from their combination —

"so too, the result is not found within the kamma, nor is it found outside the kamma either; nor is the kamma found within it.

"That kamma is void of the fruit, and the fruit is not found within the kamma; yet, in dependence on the kamma, the fruit is produced from it.

"For herein there is no god, no Brahmā, who is the maker of this round of existence; bare phenomena alone proceed, dependent on the coming-together of their causes."

Reading note — Ru-Yi

This is among the most quoted verse-passages in the entire Theravāda commentarial tradition, and it earns that reputation by solving, with a single concrete image, a problem the rest of the chapter has approached only abstractly: how can a result be genuinely produced by its kamma without either being identical to that kamma (which would make kamma and result the same thing, absurdly) or existing somewhere else entirely separate from it (which would sever the causal link altogether)? The fire-from-sun-gem-and-cow-dung simile answers this precisely: none of the three "sources" ancient India recognized for kindling fire (focusing sunlight through a crystal, striking a gem, or burning dried cow-dung) contains fire as a hidden ingredient waiting to be released, and no fire exists floating free apart from all of them either — fire is simply what arises when the right conditions combine. Kamma and its result stand in exactly the same relationship: dependently arisen, without either containing or excluding the other.

The verses' middle section — the sixty-two wrong views, the bond of views, being swept along by craving's stream — is not a digression but the passage's real stakes: misunderstanding this precise dependently-arisen relationship between kamma and result is what generates the entire catalogue of speculative views the Brahmajāla Sutta enumerates, since nearly every one of the sixty-two ultimately rests on some mistaken picture of a self that either persists through kamma and result (eternalism) or is cut off from them (annihilationism). The closing line's flat denial of any divine "maker of this round of existence" is as direct a statement of Buddhist causal naturalism as the Visuddhimagga anywhere offers — not merely that a creator-god cannot be found, as chapter eighteen and this chapter's own opening reasoning argued, but that none is needed, since bare phenomena, dependently arisen, are entirely sufficient to account for the whole of saṃsāra's unfolding.

Key vocabulary — for Pāli learners
suddhadhammā

"bare phenomena" — suddha (bare, mere, pure) + dhammā (states, phenomena); the verses' own term for what actually proceeds through the round of kamma and result, in deliberate contrast to any posited doer, experiencer, or divine agent standing behind that process.

dvāsaṭṭhidiṭṭhi

the sixty-two views — the full catalogue of speculative wrong views enumerated in the Brahmajāla Sutta, invoked here as the direct consequence of failing to understand kamma and result as dependently arisen rather than tied to a persisting or annihilated self.

hetusambhārapaccayā

"dependent on the coming-together of causes" — hetu (cause) + sambhāra (assemblage, combination) + paccayā (by condition); the verses' closing formula for how bare phenomena proceed without any maker, directly echoing the fire simile's "born from their combination" (sambhārehi ca jāyati) two verses earlier.

Nothing Transmigrates: The Recitation, the Medicine, the Mirror, the Lamp

Having grasped mind-and-matter's conditions by way of the round of kamma and the round of result, the meditator arrives at "full understanding as the known" — every past, future, and present state now understood specifically by way of death and rebirth-linking. Four everyday similes then dissolve the last apparent puzzle: if nothing whatsoever passes from one existence to the next, how can the next existence still depend on the one before it? A closing set of verses answers with the exact mechanics of consciousness at the moment of rebirth-linking itself.

Pali Source

Tassevaṃ kammavaṭṭavipākavaṭṭavasena nāmarūpassa paccayapariggahaṃ katvā tīsu addhāsu pahīnavicikicchassa sabbe atītānāgatapaccuppannadhammā cutipaṭisandhivasena viditā honti, sāssa hoti "ñātapariññā".

So evaṃ pajānāti – ye atīte kammapaccayā nibbattā khandhā, te tattheva niruddhā, atītakammapaccayā pana imasmiṃ bhave aññe nibbattā, atītabhavato imaṃ bhavaṃ āgato ekadhammopi natthi, imasmimpi bhave kammapaccayena nibbattā khandhā nirujjhissanti, punabbhave aññe nibbattissanti, imamhā bhavā punabbhavaṃ ekadhammopi na gamissati. Apica kho yathā na ācariyamukhato sajjhāyo antevāsikassa mukhaṃ pavisati, na ca tappaccayā tassa mukhe sajjhāyo na vattati, na dūtena mantodakaṃ pītaṃ rogino udaraṃ pavisati, na ca tassa tappaccayā rogo na vūpasammati, na mukhe maṇḍanavidhānaṃ ādāsatalādīsu mukhanimittaṃ gacchati, na ca tattha tappaccayā maṇḍanavidhānaṃ na paññāyati, na ekissā vaṭṭiyā dīpasikhā aññaṃ vaṭṭiṃ saṅkamati, na ca tattha tappaccayā dīpasikhā na nibbattati, evameva na atītabhavato imaṃ bhavaṃ, ito vā punabbhavaṃ koci dhammo saṅkamati, na ca atītabhave khandhāyatanadhātupaccayā idha, idha vā khandhāyatanadhātupaccayā punabbhave khandhāyatanadhātuyo na nibbattantīti.

Yatheva cakkhuviññāṇaṃ, manodhātuanantaraṃ; Na ceva āgataṃ nāpi, na nibbattaṃ anantaraṃ.

Tatheva paṭisandhimhi, vattate cittasantati; Purimaṃ bhijjate cittaṃ, pacchimaṃ jāyate tato.

Tesaṃ antarikā natthi, vīci tesaṃ na vijjati; Na cito gacchati kiñci, paṭisandhi ca jāyatīti.

Ru-Yi Translation

For one who has thus grasped the conditions of mind-and-matter by way of the round of kamma and the round of result, and whose doubt with respect to the three time-periods has been abandoned, all past, future, and present states become known by way of death and rebirth-linking; and this is his "full understanding as the known."

He understands thus: those aggregates that were generated by past kamma as condition ceased right there; but other aggregates, generated by that same past kamma as condition, have arisen in this present existence; not a single state has come from that past existence into this present one. The aggregates generated by kamma as condition in this present existence, too, will cease; others will arise in the next existence; not a single state will go from this present existence into the next. And yet — just as recitation does not pass from the teacher's mouth into the pupil's mouth, and yet the recitation in the pupil's mouth does not fail to occur on that account; just as medicine-water drunk by a messenger does not pass into the sick man's belly, and yet his illness does not fail to be relieved on that account; just as the reflection of a face does not pass onto the surface of a mirror or the like, and yet the arrangement of an ornament is not, on that account, left unseen there; just as the flame of one lamp-wick does not pass over to another wick, and yet a lamp-flame does not fail to arise there on that account — in just the same way, no state passes over from a past existence into this one, or from this one into a future existence; and yet it is not the case that, dependent on the aggregates, bases, and elements of a past existence, the aggregates, bases, and elements here — or, dependent on those here, the aggregates, bases, and elements of a future existence — fail to arise.

"Just as eye-consciousness, occurring immediately after mind-element, has neither come from it, nor has it arisen without an immediately preceding condition —

"just so, at rebirth-linking, the continuity of consciousness proceeds: the former consciousness breaks up, and from that the latter is born.

"Between them there is no interval, no gap is found in them; nothing whatsoever goes across from the one, and yet rebirth-linking is born."

Reading note — Ru-Yi

Each of the four similes isolates a different facet of the same non-transmigration principle, and the choice of examples is precise rather than merely decorative. The teacher's recitation and the pupil's own recitation are genuinely two distinct events, yet the second could never have occurred without the first — exactly the relationship between a past existence's aggregates and a present existence's aggregates. The messenger's medicine-water never touches the sick man's body at all, and yet the illness still subsides — illustrating that the causal link need not involve any literal transfer of substance whatsoever, only the right conditions being met. The mirror's reflection makes the same point from the side of appearance: nothing has moved into the mirror, yet an accurate image genuinely appears there. And the lamp lit from another lamp is perhaps the most commonly cited of the four elsewhere in Buddhist literature, precisely because a flame seems, more than almost anything else in ordinary experience, as though it ought to be a single continuous thing passed from wick to wick — when in fact each flame is freshly produced, dependent on, but not identical with, the one before it.

The closing verses' eye-consciousness example gives the whole discussion its most technical grounding: within a single ordinary cognitive process, eye-consciousness itself is already an instance of one mental state arising strictly in dependence on an immediately preceding one (mind-element, functioning here as the "five-door adverting" moment) without anything being carried over between them — an entirely uncontroversial, observable-in-principle fact about how consciousness already works moment to moment, within a single lifetime. Rebirth-linking, the verses insist, is simply this same everyday mechanism operating across the boundary of death, not some special, additional kind of transfer requiring an enduring self to carry it out.

Kaṅkhāvitaraṇavisuddhi Complete: The Lesser Stream-Enterer

The chapter closes by naming the knowledge it has spent itself building — "knowledge of the stableness of phenomena," "knowledge of things as they truly are," "right seeing," and "the overcoming of doubt" are all shown, via a final Paṭisambhidāmagga quotation, to be one and the same thing under different names. A meditator who has reached this knowledge earns a distinctive title: a "lesser stream-enterer," one who, while not yet a noble one, has gained comfort, a footing, and a fixed destiny within the Buddha's dispensation.

Ru-Yi Translation

For one who has thus known all states by way of death and rebirth-linking, the knowledge that grasps the conditions of mind-and-matter in every respect becomes firmly established, and the sixteen kinds of doubt are more thoroughly abandoned. Not only that alone: the eightfold doubt as well, occurring by the method beginning "he is in doubt about the Teacher," is likewise abandoned, and the sixty-two kinds of wrong view are suppressed. Thus the knowledge that stands, having crossed over doubt with respect to the three time-periods, by way of grasping the conditions of mind-and-matter through these various methods, should be understood as "purification by overcoming doubt." "Knowledge of the stableness of phenomena," "knowledge of things as they truly are," and "right seeing" are likewise designations for this same thing. For this was said:

"Ignorance is a condition; the formations are conditionally arisen. Both these states are conditionally arisen" — the understanding involved in grasping the conditions is knowledge of the stableness of phenomena.

"Attending to things as impermanent, which states does one know and see as they truly are? How does right seeing come about? How, by extension from that, do all formations come to be well seen as impermanent? Wherein is doubt abandoned? … Attending to things as suffering … and so on … attending to things as not-self, which states does one know and see as they truly are? … and so on … wherein is doubt abandoned?

"Attending to things as impermanent, one knows and sees the sign as it truly is; hence it is called right seeing. In this way, by extension from that, all formations come to be well seen as impermanent. Herein doubt is abandoned. Attending to things as suffering, one knows and sees occurrence as it truly is … and so on … attending to things as not-self, one knows and sees both the sign and occurrence as they truly are; hence it is called right seeing. In this way, by extension from that, all states come to be well seen as not-self. Herein doubt is abandoned.

"Now, knowledge of things as they truly are, right seeing, and the overcoming of doubt — are these states different both in meaning and in letter, or are they one in meaning and different only in the letter? — Knowledge of things as they truly are, right seeing, and the overcoming of doubt: these states are one in meaning; they differ only in the letter."

A practitioner of insight endowed with this knowledge is called, within the Buddha's dispensation, one who has gained comfort, gained a footing, whose destiny is fixed — a "lesser stream-enterer."

"Therefore, ever mindful, let a monk who desires the overcoming of doubt grasp, in every respect, the conditions of mind-and-matter."

Thus, in the Path of Purification, composed for the joy of good people,
in the section on the development of wisdom,
the discussion called "The Description of Purification by Overcoming Doubt"
constitutes the nineteenth chapter.

Reading note — Ru-Yi

The four synonyms given here for this chapter's accomplishment are not interchangeable by accident but each highlight a different facet of the same seeing: dhammaṭṭhitiñāṇa ("knowledge of the stableness of phenomena") emphasizes the lawlike, dependable regularity of the conditioning relationship itself; yathābhūtañāṇa ("knowledge of things as they truly are") emphasizes accuracy against how things actually are, echoing chapter eighteen's own yathābhūtadassana; sammādassana ("right seeing") emphasizes ethical and soteriological correctness, in contrast to the wrong views this same knowledge suppresses; and kaṅkhāvitaraṇā names the chapter's own subjective experience of the accomplishment, the felt crossing-over of doubt itself. The Paṭisambhidāmagga's own closing question — are these different in meaning, or only in wording? — and its answer (one in meaning, different only in the letter) is a small but telling methodological moment: Buddhaghosa is careful to show his conclusions are not free commentarial invention but directly traceable to canonical precedent, down to this very point of terminological equivalence.

The title "cūḷasotāpanna," lesser or junior stream-enterer, is one of the more evocative terms in the whole Visuddhimagga: it names someone who has not yet attained any of the four noble paths and fruits — true stream-entry remains ahead of them — and yet whose insight has become secure enough that their eventual attainment is, in some meaningful sense, no longer in doubt. "Gained comfort" (laddhassāsa), "gained a footing" (laddhapatiṭṭha), and "of fixed destiny" (niyatagatika) together describe a kind of provisional confidence: not yet liberated, but no longer wandering blind either. With purification by overcoming doubt now complete, the treatise's very next task — named already in this chapter's own quoted Paṭisambhidāmagga passage on attending to impermanence, suffering, and not-self — is to develop exactly that threefold contemplation into the third purification, knowledge and vision of what is path and what is not path.

Key vocabulary — for Pāli learners
dhammaṭṭhitiñāṇa

knowledge of the stableness of phenomena — dhamma (phenomenon) + ṭhiti (standing, stability) + ñāṇa (knowledge); one of four names given here for purification by overcoming doubt, drawn directly from the Paṭisambhidāmagga's own definition in terms of ignorance and the formations as conditionally arisen.

cūḷasotāpanna

a lesser stream-enterer — cūḷa (small, minor) + sotāpanna (one who has entered the stream, the first of the four noble persons); an unofficial but well-attested title for a meditator whose insight into conditionality is secure enough to fix their eventual destiny, without yet having attained the genuine noble path of stream-entry itself.

laddhassāsa / laddhapatiṭṭha / niyatagatika

"gained comfort" / "gained a footing" / "of fixed destiny" — three participial descriptions compounding to characterize the cūḷasotāpanna's provisional but genuine security, distinct from both an ordinary worldling's total uncertainty and a noble one's complete attainment.
