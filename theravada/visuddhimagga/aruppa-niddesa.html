Chapter 10 · Āruppaniddesa · Complete

The Four Immaterial States

This page carries Ru-Yi's translation of the Visuddhimagga's chapter 10, Āruppaniddesa ("The Description of the Immaterial States") — the four formless attainments reached beyond the fine-material jhānas: the bases of infinite space, infinite consciousness, nothingness, and neither-perception-nor-non-perception. Each is reached not by dropping a jhāna factor, as with the four fine-material jhānas, but by transcending one object after another — form, then space, then consciousness, then that consciousness's own departure — illustrated throughout by some of this treatise's most memorable similes: a snake-fearing fugitive, a cloth blown from a jar's mouth, an empty meeting-hall, a king who admires craftsmen without envying them, and a shed built precariously over an unclean place.

Why Go Beyond Form at All? Two Similes for a Fugitive Mind

The chapter opens immediately after the divine abidings by turning to the four immaterial attainments, beginning with the base of infinite space. The meditator wishing to develop it must first see the danger inherent in form itself — since it is on account of form that beatings, the taking up of weapons, quarrels, and disputes all occur — and resolve to go beyond it entirely. Two vivid similes capture the specific quality of mind required: a man deathly afraid of snakes who flees even the mere sight of a rope or a crack in dried earth, and a man who has fled one village to escape an enemy only to panic at the mere sight of someone who merely resembles that enemy in another village. Neither man wants so much as to look at the thing that frightens him — and this, the chapter says, is exactly the attitude the meditator must take toward form itself, not merely toward the specific coarse form that first provoked the wish to escape it.

Ru-Yi Translation

Next after the divine abidings, among the four immaterial states set out, one wishing first to develop the base of infinite space, on account of the saying, "for it is on account of form that the taking up of sticks, the taking up of weapons, quarrels, disputes, and contention are seen to occur; that does not exist at all in the immaterial — having reflected thus, he is one who has entered upon the path to disenchantment with, dispassion toward, and cessation of forms themselves" (M.N. 2.103), having seen the danger in physical form born of past kamma by way of these instances of taking up sticks and the rest, and by way of the thousand afflictions such as diseases of the eye and ear, sets aside the delimited space-kasiṇa and produces the fourth jhāna by way of one of the nine kasiṇas beginning with the earth kasiṇa.

For him, although the physical form born of past kamma has been gone beyond by way of the fine-material-sphere fourth jhāna, still, since the kasiṇa's own form is merely a counterpart of that same form, he wishes to go beyond that too. How so? Just as a man deathly afraid of snakes, having been chased by a snake in the forest and fled at speed, becomes afraid and terrified merely on seeing, at the place of his flight, a streak resembling a snake's track, a palm leaf, a creeper, a rope, or a crack in dried earth, and has no wish at all to look at it — and just as a man living in a single village together with a malicious, hostile person, harassed by that person through killing, binding, and burning down his house, goes to live in another village, and there too, seeing a man of similar form, voice, and manner to his enemy, becomes afraid and terrified and has no wish at all to look at him —

here is the correspondence of this simile: just as the time when those men were harassed by the snake or the enemy corresponds to the monk's time of possessing physical form born of past kamma by way of an object; just as their fleeing at speed and going to another village corresponds to the monk's time of going beyond physical form born of past kamma by way of the fine-material-sphere fourth jhāna; so too, just as their wish, upon seeing at the place of their flight or in the other village a streak, a palm leaf, or the like, or a man resembling the enemy, not to see fear and terror, corresponds to the monk's own recognition that the kasiṇa's form, too, is merely a counterpart of that same form, and his consequent wish to go beyond that as well. The similes of a man mauled by a boar, or terrified of a dog, or of demons, should also be understood in this same connection.

Reading note — Ru-Yi

The chapter's opening move is a striking piece of psychological continuity across what might otherwise look like an abrupt technical transition: rather than treating the shift from fine-material to immaterial jhāna as simply "the next item on the list," the text insists on tracing the actual motivational thread — the same aversion to form that drove the meditator away from ordinary sense objects toward the kasiṇa disk in the first place logically extends, once seen clearly, to the kasiṇa disk itself, since it too is nothing but a counterpart of the very physical form being escaped.

Both similes share a precise structural feature worth noting: in neither case is the fleeing man's fear about the streak of rope, the crack in the earth, or the look-alike stranger themselves — he knows perfectly well these are not the snake or the actual enemy. The fear is a trained, generalized aversion carried over from the real danger, applied now even to harmless resemblances. This is exactly the meditator's own situation: the kasiṇa disk is not itself a threat, but its resemblance to ordinary form is reason enough to want to go beyond it, precisely because that resemblance is real.

Key vocabulary — for Pāli learners
āruppa

immaterial, formless — a- (not) + rūpa (form); the standard term for the four attainments this chapter treats, each taking a progressively more abstract, non-physical object than any fine-material jhāna.

karajarūpa

physical form born of past kamma — karaja (produced, born) + rūpa (form); the ordinary physical body and its associated sense-sphere objects, as distinct from the kasiṇa-disk's own "counterpart" form used as a meditation object.

tappaṭibhāga

a counterpart of that — tad- (that) + paṭibhāga (a counterpart, likeness); the technical description of the kasiṇa's own form as sharing enough in common with ordinary physical form to inherit the same danger and warrant the same wish to be transcended.

Removing the Kasiṇa Without Touching It

Having seen the danger even in the kasiṇa's own fourth jhāna — that it still takes a form as its object, that it has joy as a near enemy, and that it is gross compared to the peaceful liberation ahead — the meditator "removes" the kasiṇa disk in a purely mental sense: not by physically taking it away, but simply by ceasing to attend to it and attending instead only to the space it had occupied. The technique is illustrated by a striking image: a man who has been watching a colored cloth stretched over a jar's mouth, who, when the cloth is suddenly blown away, finds himself looking at nothing but the bare space where it had been — exactly the shift in attention by which the fourth-jhāna consciousness on the kasiṇa disk gives way to the new consciousness of infinite space.

Evaṃ so tasmā catutthajjhānassa ārammaṇabhūtā kasiṇarūpā nibbijja pakkamitukāmo pañcahākārehi ciṇṇavasī hutvā paguṇarūpāvacaracatutthajjhānato vuṭṭhāya tasmiṃ jhāne "imaṃ mayā nibbiṇṇaṃ rūpaṃ ārammaṇaṃ karotī"ti ca, "āsannasomanassapaccatthika"nti ca, "santavimokkhato oḷārika"nti ca ādīnavaṃ passati. Aṅgoḷārikatā panettha natthi. Yatheva hetaṃ rūpaṃ duvaṅgikaṃ, evaṃ āruppānipīti.

So tattha evaṃ ādīnavaṃ disvā nikantiṃ pariyādāya ākāsānañcāyatanaṃ santato anantato manasikaritvā cakkavāḷapariyantaṃ vā yattakaṃ icchati tattakaṃ vā kasiṇaṃ pattharitvā tena phuṭṭhokāsaṃ "ākāso ākāso"ti vā, "ananto ākāso"ti vā manasikaronto ugghāṭeti kasiṇaṃ. Tassevaṃ punappunaṃ āvajjayato takkāhataṃ vitakkāhataṃ karoto nīvaraṇāni vikkhambhanti, sati santiṭṭhati, upacārena cittaṃ samādhiyati. So taṃ nimittaṃ punappunaṃ āsevati, bhāveti, bahulīkaroti. Ru-Yi Translation

Thus, wishing to become disenchanted with and depart from the kasiṇa's own form, which is the object of that fourth jhāna, having gained mastery in the five ways, he emerges from that proficient fine-material-sphere fourth jhāna and sees the danger in it thus: "this jhāna has as its object a form from which I have become disenchanted," and "it has joy as a near enemy," and "it is gross compared to the peaceful liberation." There is, however, no grossness of factors involved here — for just as this jhāna has two factors, so too do the immaterial attainments.

Having thus seen the danger there, and having exhausted his delight in it, attending to the base of infinite space as peaceful and as infinite, having spread out the kasiṇa either to the limit of the world-system or to whatever extent he wishes, and attending to the space touched by it as "space, space," or "infinite space," he removes the kasiṇa. For in removing it, he neither rolls it up like a mat, nor lifts it away like a cake from a dish; he simply does not advert to it, does not attend to it, does not review it — and, by this very not-adverting, not-attending, and not-reviewing, but instead attending only to the space it touched as "space, space," he is said to "remove the kasiṇa." Even while it is being "removed," the kasiṇa itself neither rolls up nor unrolls; it is simply called "removed" in dependence on his non-attention to it together with his attention to "space, space" — only the mere space left by the removal of the kasiṇa becomes apparent. "The space left by the removal of the kasiṇa," "the space touched by the kasiṇa," and "the space vacated by the kasiṇa" — these are all one and the same thing.

He repeatedly adverts to this sign, the space left by the removal of the kasiṇa, as "space, space," striking at it and considering it again and again. As he thus repeatedly adverts to it in this way, the hindrances are suppressed, mindfulness becomes established, and the mind becomes concentrated by way of access. He repeatedly cultivates, develops, and makes much of that sign. As he thus repeatedly adverts to and attends to it, just as fine-material-sphere consciousness becomes absorbed in the earth kasiṇa and the rest, so too the consciousness of the base of infinite space becomes absorbed in space. Here too, in the preceding portion, three or four impulsion moments are sense-sphere consciousnesses associated with equanimous feeling; the fourth or fifth is immaterial-sphere consciousness. The rest is exactly as stated for the earth kasiṇa.

But here is a distinctive feature: when the immaterial-sphere consciousness has thus arisen, that monk is like a man who has bound some blue, or yellow, red, or white cloth over the opening of a cart-basket, a jar's mouth, or the like, and stands looking at it — and then, when that cloth is suddenly removed by a gust of wind or by some other means, stands looking at nothing but the bare space. Just so, having previously dwelt looking with the eye of jhāna at the kasiṇa disk, when that sign is suddenly removed by this preliminary attention of "space, space," he dwells looking at nothing but space. By this much he is said to "dwell having attained the base of infinite space, by completely transcending perceptions of form, by the disappearance of perceptions of resistance, and by non-attention to perceptions of variety, thinking, 'space is infinite'" (Vibh. 508; D.N. 2.129).

Reading note — Ru-Yi

The insistence that the kasiṇa is "removed" without being touched, rolled up, or lifted away is a carefully precise piece of phenomenology: what actually changes is nowhere in the physical disk at all, but entirely in the direction of the meditator's attention — a reminder that everything about this whole progression through the immaterial attainments takes place purely at the level of what the mind chooses to advert to, never at the level of any external object being manipulated.

The cloth-over-a-jar simile earns its place by capturing something the more abstract "removing the kasiṇa" language could easily leave vague: the space was there all along, underneath or behind the cloth, exactly as unbounded space exists everywhere the kasiṇa disk once seemed to occupy — the disk was never actually blocking anything, and its removal reveals what its own presence had merely been obscuring from attention.

Key vocabulary — for Pāli learners
ugghāṭeti

to remove, to open up, to lift off — the technical verb for this chapter's signature technique: withdrawing attention from the kasiṇa disk so that only the space it touched remains as the new object.

kasiṇugghāṭimākāsa

the space left by the removal of the kasiṇa — kasiṇa + ugghāṭima (removed) + ākāsa (space); one of three equivalent names this section gives for the exact object of the new, immaterial-sphere consciousness.

santavimokkha

the peaceful liberation — santa (peaceful) + vimokkha (liberation); the standard of comparison against which even the accomplished fine-material fourth jhāna is judged "gross," motivating the further ascent into the immaterial attainments.

What "Completely Transcending Perceptions of Form" Actually Means

The chapter now unpacks the canonical formula's opening phrase word by word. "Perceptions of form" turns out to be a compressed reference to two things at once: the fifteen fine-material-sphere jhānas themselves (wholesome, resultant, and functional) and their nine kasiṇa-type objects — both are called "form" elsewhere in the canon, and both must be left behind together, since transcending the perceptions necessarily means transcending their objects as well, even though the Vibhaṅga's own definition speaks only of the perceptions. The chapter is careful to note that reaching the immaterial attainments genuinely requires abandoning one specific object (the kasiṇa) in favor of an entirely new one (space) — unlike the fine-material jhānas, which merely deepen concentration on the very same object throughout.

Pali Source

Rūpāvacarajjhānampi hi rūpanti vuccati "rūpī rūpāni passatī"tiādīsu (dī. ni. 2.129), tassa ārammaṇampi "bahiddhā rūpāni passati suvaṇṇadubbaṇṇānī"tiādīsu (dī. ni. 2.173), tasmā idha rūpe saññā rūpasaññāti evaṃ saññāsīsena vuttarūpāvacarajjhānassetaṃ adhivacanaṃ. Rūpaṃ saññā assāti rūpasaññaṃ. Rūpaṃ assa nāmanti vuttaṃ hoti. Pathavīkasiṇādibhedassa tadārammaṇassa cetaṃ adhivacananti veditabbaṃ. Samatikkamāti virāgā nirodhā ca. Kiṃ vuttaṃ hoti? Ru-Yi Translation

Herein, "completely" means in every way, or the meaning is "of all, without remainder." "Perceptions of form": by the heading of "perception," this refers both to the fine-material-sphere jhānas themselves and to their objects. For the fine-material-sphere jhāna itself is also called "form" in such passages as "possessing form, one sees forms" (D.N. 2.129); its object too is called "form" in such passages as "one sees forms externally, whether beautiful or ugly" (D.N. 2.173). Therefore here, "perception with regard to form" is called "perception of form" — by the heading of "perception," this is a designation for the fine-material-sphere jhāna so referred to. Or: "that for which form is the perceived object" is "perception of form" — the meaning is, "form is its name." This should be understood as a designation for the object as well, distinguished as the earth kasiṇa and the rest. "By completely transcending" means by dispassion toward and cessation of. What is meant is this: by way of wholesome, resultant, and functional consciousness, there are fifteen jhānas reckoned as "perceptions of form," and, by way of the earth kasiṇa and the rest, nine objects reckoned as "perceptions of form" — through the dispassion toward and cessation of these, in every way and without remainder, and by being the cause of that dispassion and that cessation, one dwells having attained the base of infinite space. For it is not possible to dwell having attained this without having completely transcended perception of form.

Herein, since there is no transcendence of perception for one who has not become dispassionate toward the object, and, once the perceptions have been transcended, the object too has thereby been transcended — therefore, without stating the transcendence of the object separately, it is said in the Vibhaṅga: "Herein, what is perception of form? The perceiving, the act of perceiving, the state of having perceived, in one who has attained the fine-material-sphere attainment, or in one reborn there, or in one dwelling happily in the present life — these are called perceptions of form. One has gone beyond, passed beyond, completely transcended these perceptions of form; therefore it is said, 'by completely transcending perceptions of form'" (Vibh. 602) — thus in the Vibhaṅga only the transcendence of the perceptions themselves is stated. But since these attainments must be reached by way of transcending the object, and not by remaining within one single object throughout, as with the first jhāna and the rest — therefore it should be understood that this explanation of the meaning has also been given by way of the transcendence of the object.

Reading note — Ru-Yi

The chapter's careful logical move here — from "there is no transcending perception without transcending its object" to "therefore the Vibhaṅga's silence about the object is not really a silence at all" — is a good example of how this treatise handles an apparent gap in the canonical wording: rather than treating the Abhidhamma's own definition as incomplete, it shows that the definition's narrower focus on perception alone already entails the broader claim about the object, since the two cannot come apart.

The explicit contrast with "the first jhāna and the rest" is a small but important structural marker: it flags that the immaterial attainments work by a genuinely different mechanism than progression through the four fine-material jhānas, where the object (say, the earth kasiṇa) stays fixed throughout while only the accompanying factors change — here, by contrast, each new attainment requires a wholly new object.

rūpasaññā

wholesome, resultant, and functional — the three ethical/functional categories of consciousness (kusala: wholesome/kammically active; vipāka: resultant; kiriya: functional, neither wholesome nor productive of result) across which the fifteen fine-material jhāna-consciousnesses are distributed.

transcendence of the object — ārammaṇa (object) + samatikkama (transcending); the specific technical claim that reaching an immaterial attainment requires abandoning one object for an entirely new one, unlike progress through the fine-material jhānas.

Resistance-Perceptions Gone, Variety-Perceptions Merely Unattended

The formula's remaining two phrases receive an equally careful dissection, and the chapter draws out a genuinely important asymmetry between them: "perceptions of resistance" — the five ordinary sense-perceptions arising from contact between a sense-organ and its object — are said to have wholly "disappeared," since they simply cannot occur once the mind no longer operates through the five sense-doors at all, even as far back as the first jhāna. "Perceptions of variety," by contrast, still technically exist in the plane of existence this jhāna produces, and are merely not attended to — a distinction illustrated by Āḷāra Kālāma, whose absorption was so complete that five hundred carts passed close beside him without his seeing or even hearing them.

Ru-Yi Translation

"By the disappearance of perceptions of resistance": perceptions arising through the impact of the physical bases such as the eye and the rest against their objects such as visible form is called "perception of resistance" — a designation for perception of visible form and the rest. As it is said: "Herein, what is perception of resistance? Perception of visible form, perception of sound, perception of smell, perception of taste, perception of tangible object — these are called perceptions of resistance" (Vibh. 603). Of these, five are wholesome-resultant and five are unwholesome-resultant; "by the disappearance," in every way, of all ten of these perceptions of resistance — that is, by their abandoning, their non-arising, having made them not occur — this is what is meant.

Admittedly, these perceptions do not exist even for one who has attained the first jhāna and the rest, since at that time the mind does not operate by way of the five sense-doors at all. Even so, just as pleasure and pain, already abandoned earlier, are mentioned again in connection with the fourth jhāna, and just as personality-view and the rest, already abandoned earlier, are mentioned again in connection with the third path — so too here, this statement about these perceptions should be understood as made for the sake of generating enthusiasm for this jhāna, by way of praising it.

Or rather: although these perceptions do not exist even for one who has attained the fine-material sphere, still, they are not absent there because they have been abandoned. For fine-material-sphere development does not lead to dispassion toward form — indeed, its very occurrence depends upon form. But this development does lead to dispassion toward form. Therefore it is proper to say that these perceptions are abandoned specifically here. And it is proper not merely to say so, but to hold this as a settled fact. For it was precisely because these had not yet been abandoned at that earlier stage that the Blessed One called sound "a thorn" for one who has attained the first jhāna (A.N. 10.72); whereas here, precisely because they have been abandoned, the immaterial attainments are said to possess "imperturbability" (Vibh. 226) and "the state of the peaceful liberation" (M.N. 1.66). And it is told that Āḷāra Kālāma, having attained an immaterial attainment, neither saw nor even heard about five hundred carts passing close beside him, one after another (D.N. 2.192).

"By non-attention to perceptions of variety": either perceptions occurring in a varied field, or perceptions that are themselves various. For since these are defined in the Vibhaṅga thus: "Herein, what is perception of variety? The perceiving, the act of perceiving, the state of having perceived, in one who has not attained absorption, possessed of the mind-element or the mind-consciousness-element — these are called perceptions of variety" (Vibh. 604) — what is meant here are the perceptions, comprised under the mind-element and mind-consciousness-element, of one who has not attained absorption, occurring in a field that varies and differs in kind as visible form, sound, and so on; and since these number forty-four in all — eight sense-sphere wholesome perceptions, twelve unwholesome perceptions, eleven sense-sphere wholesome-resultant perceptions, two unwholesome-resultant perceptions, and eleven sense-sphere functional perceptions — being various, differing in kind, and unlike one another, they are therefore called "perceptions of variety." "By non-attention," in every way, to these perceptions of variety — that is, by non-adverting, non-consideration, non-review; the meaning is, because he does not advert to them, does not attend to them, does not review them.

And since the former two — perceptions of form and perceptions of resistance — do not exist even in the plane of existence produced by this jhāna, still less at the time of dwelling having attained this jhāna within that same plane — therefore, for these two, their complete absence is expressed by both terms, "transcendence" and "disappearance." But as for perceptions of variety: since twenty-seven of these — eight sense-sphere wholesome perceptions, nine functional perceptions, and ten unwholesome perceptions — do exist within the plane of existence produced by this jhāna, therefore it should be understood that "non-attention" is said with reference to these. For indeed, one dwelling having attained this jhāna dwells having attained it precisely through non-attention to these; whereas one who attends to them is not one who has truly attained it.

In brief, here, "by the transcendence of perceptions of form" expresses the abandoning of all fine-material-sphere states. "By the disappearance of perceptions of resistance, by non-attention to perceptions of variety" expresses, respectively, the abandoning and the mere non-attention of all sense-sphere consciousness and its associated mental factors.

Reading note — Ru-Yi

The three-way distinction drawn here — form-perceptions transcended, resistance-perceptions disappeared, variety-perceptions merely unattended-to — is not stylistic variation in the canonical wording but a precise map of three genuinely different relationships a meditator's mind can have to a category of mental content: full abandonment (the fine-material states, left behind for good upon reaching the immaterial), functional absence going back even further (the five sense-perceptions, already impossible from the first jhāna onward), and mere non-attention to something that technically still exists in that plane of being (the forty-four "variety" perceptions, of which twenty-seven survive but simply go unnoticed).

The Āḷāra Kālāma anecdote does real evidential work rather than merely decorating the point: it is offered as historical testimony, not hypothetical illustration, that an accomplished meditator's absorption in the immaterial attainments can genuinely exclude even five hundred carts' worth of noise and visual disturbance passing directly beside him — grounding an otherwise highly abstract Abhidhamma distinction in a report of what actually, verifiably happened to a specific named teacher.

The chapter closes its treatment of the first immaterial attainment with a precise etymological breakdown of its full canonical name, tracing "infinite" through to "space-infinity" and finally to "the base of infinite space" — a base in exactly the sense that a particular resort or dwelling-place is a base for the beings who inhabit it, here serving as the foundation for the jhāna and all its associated mental factors. This etymological work, characteristic of the treatise's method throughout, closes out the section proper before the chapter moves on to the second immaterial attainment.

Reading note — Ru-Yi

The double location of "infinity" — in the space itself, which has no discernible beginning or end, and in the meditator's own attention, which pervades it without limit — quietly prevents a possible misunderstanding: infinite space is not being described here as some independently existing cosmic entity the meditator merely discovers, but as a meditation object whose infinite character is inseparable from how it is being attended to.

The Base of Infinite Consciousness

The second immaterial attainment is reached by an exactly parallel method to the first, now redirected onto a new object: rather than the infinite space itself, the meditator attends to the very consciousness that occurred while pervading that space, repeating "consciousness, consciousness" rather than "infinite, infinite." Seeing the base of infinite space as still having the fine-material jhāna as a near enemy and as less peaceful than what lies ahead, he lets go of it entirely — a transcendence the chapter is careful to note applies to both the earlier jhāna and its object at once, exactly as before — and the new consciousness becomes absorbed in the prior consciousness itself, now taken as boundless.

Reading note — Ru-Yi

The precise grammatical note on "by consciousness" (viññāṇena) — flagging it as an instrumental case doing the work of an accusative — is a small technical aside, but a telling one: even in a text this focused on meditative attainment, the commentarial tradition's philological rigor never relaxes, and a passage's grammar is checked as carefully as its doctrine, because a misparsed case ending could otherwise suggest that consciousness is somehow the *means* by which space is pervaded, rather than being itself the very thing now taken as the object.

The transition from "infinite space" to "infinite consciousness" as an object is a subtle but significant move: the meditator does not need to manufacture some entirely new object out of nothing, but simply redirects attention from the space that was pervaded to the awareness that was doing the pervading — each immaterial attainment, as this chapter will make explicit later, taking the previous attainment's own consciousness as its next object, one link in a single unbroken chain of refinement.

The Base of Nothingness, and the Empty Meeting-Hall

The third immaterial attainment reverses the direction of the previous two: rather than taking some positive object as infinite, the meditator now attends to the sheer absence of the very consciousness that had pervaded space, repeating "there is not, there is not" or "empty, empty" or "vacant, vacant." The Fourth Attainment, and the King Who Would Never Trade Places

The final and most refined immaterial attainment presents a genuine paradox the chapter addresses head-on: the meditator must attend to the base of nothingness as peaceful in order to move beyond it — yet if he is attending to it as peaceful, how can this count as transcending it rather than simply settling into it? The answer lies not in what is attended to but in the complete absence of any wish to re-enter it. This is illustrated by a king who, passing craftsmen at their skilled work, genuinely admires their skill without ever wishing to trade his throne for their trade — his admiration and his complete lack of desire to change places coexist without contradiction, exactly as the meditator can rightly judge the base of nothingness "peaceful" while having not the slightest inclination to attain it again.

Reading note — Ru-Yi

The king-and-craftsmen simile solves a genuine puzzle rather than merely decorating the text: how can honest appreciation of something's value coexist with a total absence of desire to possess it? The king's admiration for the craftsmen's skill is entirely sincere, not a polite fiction — and precisely because his own position is even better, that sincere admiration generates no pull whatsoever to trade places. The meditator's relationship to the base of nothingness works the same way: it really is peaceful, and he really does recognize that, but the fourth attainment's still-greater refinement leaves no motivational residue drawing him back to it.

"The attainment with a residue of formations" is a carefully hedged name for what is, by any ordinary standard, an extraordinarily subtle state — the phrase concedes that mental formations have not been eliminated altogether (that would be a different attainment entirely, cessation itself) while insisting that what remains is so attenuated that ordinary language runs out, unable to call it simply "perception" or simply its absence.

The chapter now explains exactly why this final attainment carries such a paradoxical name, and — remarkably for a treatise devoted to samatha technique — pauses to note a genuine limit on this attainment's usefulness for insight meditation: its perception has become so attenuated that it can no longer function sharply enough to serve as an object for generating disenchantment, except in the hands of a practitioner of Venerable Sāriputta's caliber, and even he could only grasp it as an undifferentiated mass rather than moment by moment. Two homely similes — a novice's technically-true-but-misleading answers about oil left coating a bowl, and about a trace of water on a path — capture exactly the sense in which something can be truthfully said to be simultaneously "there" and "not there," depending on what function is being asked about.

Ru-Yi Translation

Now, in order to show, in terms of meaning, what is meant by "the base of neither-perception-nor-non-perception" with reference to the perception thus attained, it is said: "'the base of neither-perception-nor-non-perception': the mind and its associated mental factors in one who has attained the base of neither-perception-nor-non-perception, or in one reborn there, or in one dwelling happily in the present life" (Vibh. 620). Of these, what is intended here are the mind and mental factors of one who has attained it. As for the word's meaning: because coarse perception is absent, yet a subtle perception is present, the perception of this jhāna together with its associated states is "neither perception nor non-perception." And that "neither-perception-nor-non-perception," being included under the mind-base and the mental-object-base, is also a "base" — thus, "the base of neither-perception-nor-non-perception." Or alternatively: this perception, because it is incapable of performing the function of sharp perception, is "not perception"; yet, because it exists by way of a subtle residue of formations, it is "not non-perception" either — thus, "neither-perception-nor-non-perception." And that "neither-perception-nor-non-perception," in the sense of being a foundation for the remaining associated states, is also a "base" — thus, "the base of neither-perception-nor-non-perception."

And not only perception here is of this kind — feeling, too, is neither feeling nor non-feeling; consciousness, too, is neither consciousness nor non-consciousness; contact, too, is neither contact nor non-contact. The same holds for the remaining associated mental factors. It should be understood that this teaching has been given under the heading of "perception" alone, as a representative case. This meaning should be illustrated by such similes as the oil smeared on a bowl, and others like it.

It is told that a novice smeared a bowl with oil and set it aside. At the time for drinking rice-gruel, the elder said, "Novice, bring the bowl." He said, "Venerable sir, there is oil in the bowl." Then, when told, "Novice, bring the oil — I shall fill a measure with it," he said, "Venerable sir, there is no oil." Herein: since the oil remains inside, coating it, such that it would render the gruel unallowable if mixed in, it is correctly said, "there is oil"; but by way of being enough to fill a measure and the like, it is correctly said, "there is none." Just so, that perception too, because it is incapable of performing the function of sharp perception, is "not perception"; yet, because it exists by way of a subtle residue of formations, it is "not non-perception" either.

What, then, is the function of perception here? It is the apprehending of an object, and, by becoming the domain of insight, the generation of disenchantment. For just as the fire element in lukewarm water is unable to perform its function of burning sharply, so this perception is unable to perform the function of apprehending sharply. And, unlike the perception in the other attainments, it is also unable, by becoming the domain of insight, to generate disenchantment. For, apart from Venerable Sāriputta, there is indeed no monk who, without having first made a firm grip on the other aggregates, is capable of contemplating the aggregates of the base of neither-perception-nor-non-perception and reaching disenchantment through them. Only one of great wisdom, an ordinary practitioner of insight comparable to Sāriputta, could manage it — and even he could do so only by way of contemplating them as a mass, thinking, "thus indeed, it seems, these states, not having been, come to be; having been, they pass away" (M.N. 3.95) — not by way of insight into each individual state one by one. To such a degree of subtlety has this attainment gone.

And just as by the simile of the oil smeared on the bowl, so too by the simile of the water on the path should this meaning be illustrated. It is told that a novice, walking ahead of an elder traveling along a road, having seen a little water, said, "Venerable sir, there is water — take off your sandals." Then, when the elder said, "If there is water, bring my bathing cloth, we shall bathe," he said, "Venerable sir, there is none." Herein: by way of being merely enough to wet the sandals, it is correctly said, "there is water"; but by way of being sufficient for bathing, it is correctly said, "there is none." Just so too, that perception, because it is incapable of performing the function of sharp perception, is "not perception"; yet, because it exists by way of a subtle residue of formations, it is "not non-perception" either.

And not only by these similes alone — this meaning should be illustrated by other, similarly fitting similes as well. "Dwells having attained" is as already explained.

This is the detailed treatment of the meditation subject of the base of neither-perception-nor-non-perception.

Reading note — Ru-Yi

The novice's two technically-correct-but-opposite answers are chosen with real precision: in both cases, nothing about the actual physical situation changes between the "yes" and the "no" — the same trace of oil, the same trace of water are present throughout. What changes is only the specific function being asked about, exactly paralleling how this jhāna's perception is simultaneously and non-contradictorily "present" (it has not vanished into nothing) and "absent" (it can no longer perform sharp apprehension) depending on which capacity is in question.

The admission that only Sāriputta, and only by contemplating "as a mass" rather than moment by moment, could ever draw insight directly from this specific attainment is a genuinely humbling concession within a text that otherwise projects total systematic confidence — a rare moment where the Visuddhimagga acknowledges a real limit case, an attainment so refined that it becomes, for virtually everyone but the Buddha's foremost disciple in wisdom, functionally unusable as a direct basis for liberating insight.

Miscellaneous Discussion: Same Two Factors, Four Degrees of Refinement

The chapter's closing miscellaneous discussion opens with a precise structural claim: unlike the progression through the four fine-material jhānas, where each step is marked by dropping a specific jhāna factor (first applied and sustained thought, then joy, then pleasure), the four immaterial attainments involve no such transcending of factors at all — all four alike possess only equanimity and one-pointedness of mind. What distinguishes them from one another is purely a transcending of object, one after another: form, then space, then the consciousness that pervaded space, then that consciousness's own departure. Yet each is still genuinely more sublime than the last, illustrated by two similes: the four ascending storeys of a single palace, each stocked with ever more luxurious versions of the same five sensual pleasures, and four cloths of identical size but ever-finer thread, becoming more valuable and more pleasant to the touch without any change in their dimensions.

Ru-Yi Translation

Having thus known the fourfold immaterial state that the Protector, of unequaled form, has taught, a miscellaneous discussion concerning it should also be understood.

For these immaterial attainments —

All four of these come to be by way of transcending an object; the wise do not hold that there is, for these, any transcending of a factor.

Among these, the first comes about by transcending the sign of form; the second, by transcending space; the third, by transcending the consciousness that occurred pervading space; the fourth, by transcending the departure of the consciousness that occurred pervading space. Thus, in every way, by way of transcending an object, all four of these immaterial attainments come to be — this should be so understood. But the wise do not hold that there is, for these, any transcending of a factor. For there is no transcending of factors in these, as there is in the fine-material-sphere attainments. Rather, in all of these alike, there are only these same two jhāna factors: equanimity and one-pointedness of mind.

Even so —

Each successive one here is more sublime than the one before; the similes to be understood here are the storey of a palace and a woven cloth.

For suppose that, in a four-storeyed palace, on the ground floor, sublime versions of the five cords of sensual pleasure were present, by way of divine dance, song, and music, fragrant scents and garlands, food, bedding, and coverings; on the second floor, these would be more sublime than that; on the third floor, still more sublime than that; and on the fourth floor, most sublime of all. Herein, although all four are equally storeys of one and the same palace, with no difference among them simply as storeys, nevertheless, by virtue of the distinctive richness of the five cords of sensual pleasure, each floor above is more sublime than the one below it.

And suppose that one woman were to spin threads of four grades — coarse, fine, finer, and finest — and make from them cloths weighing four palas, three palas, two palas, and one pala respectively, all equal in length and breadth. Herein, although all four cloths are equal in length and breadth, with no difference among them by way of size, nevertheless, by virtue of pleasantness to the touch, fineness, and costliness, each subsequent cloth is more sublime than the one before it. Just so, although in all four of these attainments there are only these same two factors — equanimity and one-pointedness of mind — nevertheless, through the distinction in their development, each successive one here should be understood, by virtue of the increasing sublimity of its factors, to be more and more sublime than the one before it.

Reading note — Ru-Yi

The distinction drawn here — refinement without any change in constituent factors — is a genuinely subtle piece of Abhidhamma analysis, and both similes are chosen to make exactly this specific, otherwise counterintuitive point vivid: two things can be structurally identical by every countable measure (four palace floors, all equally "a storey"; four cloths, all equally sized) while nonetheless differing enormously in quality along some other dimension entirely (luxury of furnishing; fineness of thread) that the structural count alone would never reveal.

This has a real practical implication for how the four immaterial attainments should be understood: a meditator cannot distinguish the base of infinite space from the base of neither-perception-nor-non-perception by inventorying which jhāna factors are present, since the answer would be identical in both cases — the entire difference between them lies in the object being transcended and the degree of subtlety that transcending has produced.

The Shed Built Over Filth: A Simile for All Four Attainments in Sequence

A single extended simile now captures the relationship among all four immaterial attainments at once: a shed built over an unclean place, and four men in succession — one who clings to the shed itself out of disgust for the filth beneath it, a second who relies on that first man, a third who judges both of them insecure and stands apart from both, and a fourth who judges the third man's position the soundest and relies on him instead. Each figure maps onto one attainment's specific relationship to the one before it, tracing a chain in which each new attainment is reached precisely by recognizing the insecurity of its predecessor.

Here is the application of this meaning: it is told that, in an unclean place, there was a shed. One man came, and, disgusted by that filth, grasped the shed with his hands and stood there, clinging to it as if attached to it. Then another came and relied on that man clinging to the shed. Then yet another came and thought, "both this man clinging to the shed and this man relying on him are badly positioned — their fall, together with the shed's own collapse, is certain; well then, I shall stand outside, apart, relying on neither." He, not relying on the one who was relying on the other, stood outside, apart. Then yet another came, and, having considered the insecurity of both the man clinging to the shed and the man relying on him, and having judged, "the one standing outside is well positioned," relied on him and stood there. Herein: the shed in the unclean place should be seen as the space left by the removal of the kasiṇa. The man clinging to the shed out of disgust for the filth should be seen as the base of infinite space, whose object is space, arising from disgust for the sign of form. The man relying on the one clinging to the shed should be seen as the base of infinite consciousness, which occurs by taking as its starting point the base of infinite space, whose object is space. The man who, having considered the insecurity of both of these, stood outside, apart, relying on neither, should be seen as the base of nothingness, which, without taking the base of infinite space as its object, takes the non-existence of that as its object instead. And the man who, having considered the insecurity of both the one clinging to the shed and the one relying on him, and having judged, "the one standing outside is well positioned," relied on him and stood there, should be seen as the base of neither-perception-nor-non-perception, which occurs by taking as its starting point the base of nothingness, standing in that "outside" region reckoned as the absence of consciousness.

Reading note — Ru-Yi

The simile's structure is worth tracing carefully, since its logic is not simply "each stage is an improvement" but something more specific: each new man's position is motivated by an explicit judgment about the insecurity of the position before it — the third man does not merely prefer standing apart, he reasons his way to it by observing that both the shed-clinger and his dependent share the same impending collapse. This mirrors exactly how each immaterial attainment is reached in this treatise: not by arbitrary preference for something more refined, but by the meditator explicitly seeing the specific danger in the attainment just gained.

The final man's choice — relying on the one who himself relies on nothing — captures something genuinely paradoxical about the fourth attainment's own position: neither-perception-nor-non-perception does not itself abandon all reliance the way the third man's "standing apart" did; instead, it is reached by relying on that very state of non-reliance, taking the base of nothingness's own object-less stability as its own foundation, one further, subtler layer removed from anything nameable at all.

Āruppaniddesa Complete

The chapter closes with two final images explaining why the fourth attainment takes the third as its object at all, despite having already seen its fault. A person unable to find any other livelihood will still rely on a king even after recognizing that king's harshness, simply for lack of any alternative — and just so, the base of neither-perception-nor-non-perception, having no other object available to it, takes the base of nothingness as its object regardless of the fault already seen in it. The chapter's very last verse gathers three images of leaning for support — a ladder's rail, a mountain's own summit, one's own knee while climbing — into a single statement of how each of these attainments proceeds only by leaning on the jhāna that came before it. For this base of neither-perception-nor-non-perception, even though it has seen the fault in that base of nothingness — namely, that "this attainment has the base of infinite consciousness as a near enemy" — nevertheless, for lack of any other object, does indeed take that very base of nothingness as its object. Like what? Just as people rely on a king for the sake of their livelihood even though they have seen his faults. For just as people, unable to find a livelihood elsewhere, rely upon and depend upon some sovereign lord of unrestrained, harsh conduct in body, speech, and mind — even though they have seen his fault, thinking, "this one's conduct is harsh" — for the sake of their livelihood, just so this base of neither-perception-nor-non-perception, even though it has seen the fault in that base of nothingness, unable to find any other object, does indeed take this base of nothingness as its object.

And, proceeding in this way —

Just as one who has climbed a tall ladder relies on the ladder's own rail, and just as one who has climbed to a mountain's summit relies on the mountaintop itself, or just as one climbing a mountain leans upon his own knee — just so does each of these proceed, leaning upon the jhāna before it.

Thus, in the Visuddhimagga, composed for the joy of good people,
in the section on the development of concentration,
this is called the Āruppaniddesa —
the tenth chapter.

Reading note — Ru-Yi

The king simile makes a point that could otherwise seem to undercut everything the chapter has built: even the most refined attainment described in this entire text is shown to rest, in the end, on nothing more secure than "there was nothing better available" — a strikingly unromantic admission that even a state as sublime as neither-perception-nor-non-perception remains a conditioned, dependent phenomenon, taking a flawed object because no unflawed one exists at that level, rather than because it has somehow transcended the need for an object altogether.

The three closing images of leaning — a ladder's rail, a mountain's summit, one's own knee — are carefully graduated in how much external support they involve, ending with the most minimal and self-contained of the three: leaning on one's own knee while climbing depends on nothing outside the climber himself. This is a fitting final image for a chapter that has spent its entire length showing each attainment growing more subtle, more self-contained, and less dependent on anything resembling an ordinary external object than the one before it.

Āruppaniddesa Complete

This completes Ru-Yi's translation of the Visuddhimagga's tenth chapter, Āruppaniddesa — the four immaterial attainments of infinite space, infinite consciousness, nothingness, and neither-perception-nor-non-perception, developed by successively transcending one object after another rather than by dropping jhāna factors, and unified by the chapter's closing similes of the palace's four storeys, the four cloths of a single size, and the shed built over an unclean place. The eleventh chapter, Samādhiniddesa — the Description of Concentration — follows next, opening with the perception of repulsiveness in nutriment before turning to a broader general discussion of concentration itself.
