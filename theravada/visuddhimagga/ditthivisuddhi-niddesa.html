Chapter 18 · Diṭṭhivisuddhiniddesa · Complete

Mind-and-Matter, Rightly Seen

This page carries Ru-Yi's translation of the Visuddhimagga's chapter 18, Diṭṭhivisuddhiniddesa ("The Description of Purification of View") — the first of the five remaining purifications that make up wisdom's "body," following directly on from the six-part "ground" completed in chapters fourteen through seventeen. Purification of view is defined at the outset as nothing other than the correct seeing of mind-and-matter (nāmarūpa) as they truly are: the chapter works through eight distinct analytical methods for determining what is mind and what is matter — from the meditator's own direct grasp of the jhāna factors and the heart-basis, through the four elements, the eighteen elements, the twelve bases, and the five aggregates, down to the barest possible summary — before turning to correlate this analysis with the Buddha's own words on the chariot, the dwelling, and suffering alone arising and ceasing. It closes with the treatise's richest cluster of similes for a being's non-existence: the wooden mechanism, the two leaning bundles of reeds, the drum and its sound, and the classic image of the blind man and the cripple who can travel only by combining their separate incapacities.

The Two Root Purifications, and Purification of View Defined

The eighteenth chapter opens with a brief accounting of what has already been accomplished before turning to name what lies ahead. Purification of virtue and purification of mind — the "two root purifications" — were completed back in Part One, in the Description of Virtue and the Description of Concentration respectively, and are simply cross-referenced here rather than repeated. The five purifications that remain, and that this chapter and the next four will treat in turn, are then named in full, with purification of view singled out and defined: nothing other than seeing mind-and-matter as they truly are.

Pali Source

Idāni yā "imesu bhūmibhūtesu dhammesu uggahaparipucchāvasena ñāṇaparicayaṃ katvā 'sīlavisuddhi ceva cittavisuddhi cā'ti dve mūlabhūtā visuddhiyo sampādetabbā"ti vuttā. Tattha sīlavisuddhi nāma suparisuddhaṃ pātimokkhasaṃvarādicatubbidhaṃ sīlaṃ, tañca sīlaniddese vitthāritameva. Cittavisuddhi nāma saupacārā aṭṭha samāpattiyo, tāpi cittasīsena vuttasamādhiniddese sabbākārena vitthāritā eva. Tasmā tā tattha vitthāritanayeneva veditabbā.

Yaṃ pana vuttaṃ "diṭṭhivisuddhi, kaṅkhāvitaraṇavisuddhi, maggāmaggañāṇadassanavisuddhi, paṭipadāñāṇadassanavisuddhi, ñāṇadassanavisuddhī"ti imā pana pañca visuddhiyo sarīranti, tattha nāmarūpānaṃ yāthāvadassanaṃ diṭṭhivisuddhi nāma.

Ru-Yi Translation

Now, it was said that, having made a familiarity of knowledge with respect to these states that constitute the "ground," by way of learning and inquiring into them, the two root purifications — namely, purification of virtue and purification of mind — are to be accomplished. Herein, purification of virtue is the fourfold, thoroughly purified virtue beginning with restraint according to the Pātimokkha; and this has already been treated in full in the Description of Virtue. Purification of mind is the eight attainments together with access; and these too, spoken of under the heading of "mind," have already been treated in every respect in the Description of Concentration. Therefore they should be understood there, in just the way they were treated in detail there.

But as to what was said — that purification of view, purification by overcoming doubt, purification by knowledge and vision of what is path and not-path, purification by knowledge and vision of the way, and purification by knowledge and vision: these five purifications constitute the "body" — herein, the seeing of mind-and-matter as they truly are is what is called purification of view.

Reading note — Ru-Yi

The "root and body" architecture named here was first laid out back in chapter fourteen: virtue and concentration form the two roots, while the five remaining purifications form the body that grows from them. With this chapter, the treatise finally reaches that body's first limb. It is worth noticing how little ceremony attends the transition — no new preamble, no fresh statement of purpose, just a two-sentence recap and a single defining sentence. The chapter's real content, the eight separate methods for actually determining mind-and-matter, is about to do all the work that a more expansive introduction might otherwise have tried to do in the abstract.

upacāra

Determining Mind by the Vehicle of Serenity; the Simile of the Snake in the House

The chapter's first working method belongs to the meditator who has actually attained jhāna (the "vehicle of serenity"). Emerging from absorption, he examines the jhāna factors themselves and determines them as "mind" by their characteristic of bending toward an object. He then works backward from mind to matter, in a simile that will recur throughout the chapter's opening section: tracking mind the way a man tracks a snake he has seen enter a house, until he finds its lair.

Pali Source

Taṃ sampādetukāmena samathayānikena tāva ṭhapetvā nevasaññānāsaññāyatanaṃ avasesarūpārūpāvacarajjhānānaṃ aññatarato vuṭṭhāya vitakkādīni jhānaṅgāni, taṃsampayuttā ca dhammā lakkhaṇarasādivasena pariggahetabbā. Pariggahetvā sabbampetaṃ ārammaṇābhimukhaṃ namanato namanaṭṭhena "nāma"nti vavatthapetabbaṃ.

Tato yathā nāma puriso antogehe sappaṃ disvā taṃ anubandhamāno tassa āsayaṃ passati, evameva ayampi yogāvacaro taṃ nāmaṃ upaparikkhanto "idaṃ nāmaṃ kiṃ nissāya pavattatī"ti pariyesamāno tassa nissayaṃ hadayarūpaṃ passati. Tato hadayarūpassa nissayabhūtāni, bhūtanissitāni ca sesupādāyarūpānīti rūpaṃ pariggaṇhāti. So sabbampetaṃ ruppanato "rūpa"nti vavatthapeti. Tato namanalakkhaṇaṃ nāmaṃ, ruppanalakkhaṇaṃ rūpanti saṅkhepato nāmarūpaṃ vavatthapeti.

Ru-Yi Translation

One desiring to accomplish this should, if he follows the vehicle of serenity, first emerge from any one of the fine-material or immaterial jhānas — setting aside only the base of neither-perception-nor-non-perception — and should then grasp the jhāna factors beginning with applied thought, together with the states associated with them, by way of their characteristic, function, and so on. Having grasped them, he should determine all of this, in the sense of its bending toward the object, as "mind," by reason of its very bending.

Then, just as a man who has seen a snake inside a house, and follows it, sees its lair — in just the same way this meditator too, examining that mind, searching, "in dependence on what does this mind occur?", sees its support, the matter of the heart. Then he grasps, as matter, the great elements that are the support of the heart-matter, and the remaining derived matter that is supported by the great elements. He determines all of this, by reason of its being deformed, as "matter." Then he determines mind-and-matter in brief thus: mind has the characteristic of bending; matter has the characteristic of being deformed.

Reading note — Ru-Yi

The snake-in-the-house simile is doing precise structural work: it explains why the meditator's analysis moves from mind to matter rather than the reverse, even though the finished doctrine treats them as equally real and mutually dependent. Mind is what first becomes evident, because it is what the meditator is directly attending to; matter — specifically the heart-basis, hadayavatthu, the physical seat the Abhidhamma assigns to mind-door consciousness — is discovered only by tracking mind back to what supports it, exactly as the snake's lair is discovered only by following the snake itself.

The closing definitional pair — namana (bending toward an object) for mind, ruppana (being deformed, affected, or molested [by heat, cold, hunger, and the rest]) for matter — is the chapter's single most important pun, since "nāma" and "rūpa" are being defined here by wordplay on their own near-homonyms rather than by an external criterion. This same etymological move will be used again at the very end of the chapter's fourth section, tying the whole eight-method survey back to this one foundational definition.

Key vocabulary — for Pāli learners
samathayānika

one whose vehicle [to insight] is serenity — samatha (calm, serenity) + yāna (vehicle) + ika; a meditator who develops jhāna first and uses it as the basis for insight, contrasted throughout this chapter with the suddhavipassanāyānika, the "bare-insight" practitioner who proceeds without jhāna.

hadayarūpa / hadayavatthu

heart-matter, the heart-basis — the physical support the Abhidhamma assigns to mind-element and mind-consciousness-element states, discovered here as the "lair" to which the snake of mind is tracked back.

namana / ruppana

bending [toward an object] / being deformed — the two defining characteristics from which "nāma" and "rūpa" take their very names: mind bends toward (or inclines toward) its object; matter is deformed, altered, or afflicted by contrary conditions such as cold, heat, and hunger.

The Detailed Method: Mind-and-Matter via the Four Elements

The second method belongs to the practitioner of bare insight, or equally to the serenity-vehicle practitioner working without jhāna — the "determining of the four elements" already introduced in chapter eleven's treatment of catudhātuvavatthāna. Here the four elements are traced through the thirty-two parts of the body, then through ten further aspects (making forty-two in all: the same detailed scheme used for the body's earth- and water-constituents in chapter eleven, extended now to matter-counting proper), tallying the exact kalāpas — the smallest indivisible clusters of material phenomena — that become evident in each part, before the immaterial states that arise dependent on all of this matter are likewise tallied and determined as "mind."

Pali Source

Suddhavipassanāyāniko pana ayameva vā samathayāniko catudhātuvavatthāne vuttānaṃ tesaṃ tesaṃ dhātupariggahamukhānaṃ aññataramukhavasena saṅkhepato vā vitthārato vā catasso dhātuyo pariggaṇhāti. Athassa yāthāvasarasalakkhaṇato āvibhūtāsu dhātūsu kammasamuṭṭhānamhi tāva kese "catasso dhātuyo, vaṇṇo, gandho, raso, ojā, jīvitaṃ, kāyappasādo"ti evaṃ kāyadasakavasena dasa rūpāni, tattheva bhāvassa atthitāya bhāvadasakavasena dasa, tattheva āhārasamuṭṭhānaṃ ojaṭṭhamakaṃ, utusamuṭṭhānaṃ, cittasamuṭṭhānanti aparānipi catuvīsatīti evaṃ catusamuṭṭhānesu catuvīsatikoṭṭhāsesu catucattālīsa catucattālīsa rūpāni, sedo, assu, kheḷo, siṅghāṇikāti imesu pana catūsu utucittasamuṭṭhānesu dvinnaṃ ojaṭṭhamakānaṃ vasena soḷasa soḷasa rūpāni, udariyaṃ, karīsaṃ, pubbo, muttanti imesu catūsu utusamuṭṭhānesu utusamuṭṭhānasseva ojaṭṭhamakassa vasena aṭṭha aṭṭha rūpāni pākaṭāni hontīti. Esa tāva dvattiṃsākāre nayo.

Ye pana imasmiṃ dvattiṃsākāre āvibhūte apare dasa ākārā āvibhavanti. Tattha asitādiparipācake tāva kammaje tejokoṭṭhāsamhi ojaṭṭhamakañceva jīvitañcāti nava rūpāni, tathā cittaje assāsapassāsakoṭṭhāsepi ojaṭṭhamakañceva saddo cāti nava, sesesu catusamuṭṭhānesu aṭṭhasu jīvitanavakañceva tīṇi ca ojaṭṭhamakānīti tettiṃsa rūpāni pākaṭāni honti.

Tassevaṃ vitthārato dvācattālīsākāravasena imesu bhūtupādāyarūpesu pākaṭesu jātesu vatthudvāravasena pañca cakkhudasakādayo, hadayavatthudasakañcāti aparānipi saṭṭhirūpāni pākaṭāni honti. So sabbānipi tāni ruppanalakkhaṇena ekato katvā "etaṃ rūpa"nti passati.

Tassevaṃ pariggahitarūpassa dvāravasena arūpadhammā pākaṭā honti. Seyyathidaṃ – dvepañcaviññāṇāni, tisso manodhātuyo, aṭṭhasaṭṭhi manoviññāṇadhātuyoti ekāsīti lokiyacittāni, avisesena ca tehi cittehi sahajāto phasso, vedanā, saññā, cetanā, jīvitaṃ, cittaṭṭhiti, manasikāroti ime satta satta cetasikāti. Lokuttaracittāni pana neva suddhavipassakassa, na samathayānikassa pariggahaṃ gacchanti anadhigatattāti. So sabbepi te arūpadhamme namanalakkhaṇena ekato katvā "etaṃ nāma"nti passati. Evameko catudhātuvavatthānamukhena vitthārato nāmarūpaṃ vavatthapeti.

Ru-Yi Translation

The practitioner of bare insight, or this same practitioner following the vehicle of serenity, grasps the four elements — by whichever of the "doors" of elemental analysis already stated in the determining of the four elements, either in brief or in detail. Then, as the elements become evident to him according to their own true characteristic: first, in the kamma-originated hairs, [he sees] — "the four elements, colour, odour, taste, nutritive essence, life, body-sensitivity" — thus ten material phenomena by way of the body-decad; in that same [hair], because sex is also present, ten more by way of the sex-decad; there too, the nutriment-originated octad-with-nutritive-essence-as-eighth, the temperature-originated [octad], and the mind-originated [octad] — twenty-four more; thus, across the four originations, in the twenty-four portions, forty-four material phenomena each become evident. In the case of the four [parts] originated from temperature-and-mind [only] — sweat, tears, saliva, nasal mucus — by way of two nutritive-essence-octads, sixteen material phenomena each become evident. In the case of the four [parts] originated from temperature only — gastric fluid, feces, pus, urine — by way of the nutritive-essence-octad belonging to temperature-origination alone, eight material phenomena each become evident. This, for now, is the method as applied to the thirty-two aspects.

But when these thirty-two aspects have become evident, a further ten aspects become evident besides. Herein: first, in the kamma-originated portion of fire that digests what is eaten and so on, there are nine material phenomena — the nutritive-essence-octad together with life; likewise, in the mind-originated portion that is the in-breath-and-out-breath, there are also nine — the nutritive-essence-octad together with sound; in the remaining eight aspects, which have all four originations, thirty-three material phenomena become evident — the life-nonad together with three nutritive-essence-octads.

When, in this way, this great-elements-and-derived-matter has become evident in detail, by way of the forty-two aspects, then, by way of the [sense-]bases and doors, a further sixty material phenomena become evident as well — the five [decads] beginning with the eye-decad, and the heart-basis-decad. He then takes all of these together, by the characteristic of being deformed, and sees: "this is matter."

For one who has thus grasped matter, the immaterial states become evident by way of the [sense-]doors. That is: the two sets of five [sense-]consciousnesses, the three mind-elements, and the sixty-eight mind-consciousness-elements — eighty-one mundane states of consciousness in all — and, without distinction, arisen together with those states of consciousness, the seven mental factors of contact, feeling, perception, volition, life, stability of consciousness, and attention, in each and every case. Supramundane states of consciousness, however, come within the grasp neither of the practitioner of pure insight nor of the practitioner of the vehicle of serenity, since they have not yet been attained. He then takes all of these immaterial states together, by the characteristic of bending, and sees: "this is mind." This is how one determines mind-and-matter in detail, by way of the method of determining the four elements.

Reading note — Ru-Yi

This section leans directly on the elaborate thirty-two-parts-of-the-body machinery already built in chapter eleven's catudhātuvavatthāna, but puts it to a new use: there, the thirty-two parts served to dissolve the perception of "a body" into earth and water elements for the sake of calm; here, the same parts, now extended to forty-two aspects by adding the four fire-portions and six wind-portions (the digestive fire, the in-and-out breath, and the rest), serve to tally exact kalāpas — the smallest indivisible units of matter the Abhidhamma recognizes, each one a fixed bundle such as the "nutritive-essence-octad" (four elements plus colour, odour, taste, and nutritive essence) or the "body-decad" (that same octad plus life and body-sensitivity). The arithmetic is exacting almost to the point of severity, and deliberately so: this is what it looks like to dismantle "the body" into its most granular physical constituents, leaving no residue for a self to hide in.

The final two paragraphs then perform the mirror-image operation for mind: once matter has been tallied in full, the eighty-one mundane states of consciousness (every kammically active and resultant mind-moment short of the supramundane paths and fruits, which by definition this ordinary meditator has not yet attained) and the seven "universal" mental factors that accompany all of them become evident in turn, and are gathered under the single heading "mind." The passage thus completes, in exhaustive Abhidhammic detail, exactly what the previous section's snake-and-house simile did more intuitively.

The Method of the Eighteen Elements

A third method redetermines the same territory through the eighteen-element scheme familiar from chapter fifteen: the eye-element is defined not as the visible lump of flesh the world calls "the eye," but strictly as eye-sensitivity, with every other material phenomenon co-arising alongside it — the supporting elements, the accompanying colour and odour and taste and nutritive essence, the kamma-born decads, the not-clung-to octads — explicitly excluded from that designation. The same procedure runs across all eighteen elements before the resulting tally is sorted into matter and mind.

Pali Source

Aparo aṭṭhārasadhātuvasena. Kathaṃ? Idha bhikkhu atthi imasmiṃ attabhāve cakkhudhātu…pe… manoviññāṇadhātūti dhātuyo āvajjitvā yaṃ loko setakaṇhamaṇḍalavicittaṃ āyatavitthataṃ akkhikūpake nhārusuttakena ābaddhaṃ maṃsapiṇḍaṃ "cakkhū"ti sañjānāti, taṃ aggahetvā khandhaniddese upādārūpesu vuttappakāraṃ cakkhupasādaṃ "cakkhudhātū"ti vavatthapeti.

Yāni panassa nissayabhūtā catasso dhātuyo, parivārakāni cattāri vaṇṇa-gandha-rasa-ojā-rūpāni, anupālakaṃ jīvitindriyanti nava sahajātarūpāni, tattheva ṭhitāni kāyadasakabhāvadasakavasena vīsati kammajarūpāni, āhārasamuṭṭhānādīnaṃ tiṇṇaṃ ojaṭṭhamakānaṃ vasena catuvīsati anupādinnarūpānīti evaṃ sesāni tepaṇṇāsa rūpāni honti, na tāni ca "cakkhudhātū"ti vavatthapeti. Esa nayo sotadhātuādīsupi. Kāyadhātuyaṃ pana avasesāni tecattālīsa rūpāni honti. Keci pana utucittasamuṭṭhānāni saddena saha nava nava katvā pañcacattālīsāti vadanti.

Iti ime pañca pasādā, tesañca visayā rūpasaddagandharasaphoṭṭhabbā pañcāti dasa rūpāni dasa dhātuyo honti. Avasesarūpāni dhammadhātuyeva honti. Cakkhuṃ pana nissāya rūpaṃ ārabbha pavattaṃ cittaṃ cakkhuviññāṇadhātu nāmāti evaṃ dvepañcaviññāṇāni pañca viññāṇadhātuyo honti. Tīṇi manodhātucittāni ekā manodhātu, aṭṭhasaṭṭhi manoviññāṇadhātucittāni manoviññāṇadhātūti sabbānipi ekāsīti lokiyacittāni satta viññāṇadhātuyo. Taṃsampayuttā phassādayo dhammadhātūti evamettha aḍḍhekādasa dhātuyo rūpaṃ, aḍḍhaṭṭhamā dhātuyo nāmanti evameko aṭṭhārasadhātuvasena nāmarūpaṃ vavatthapeti.

Ru-Yi Translation

Another determines it by way of the eighteen elements. How? Here, a monk, having adverted to the elements — "there is, in this very person, the eye-element … and so on … the mind-consciousness-element" — without taking hold of what the world, seeing a lump of flesh bound by a sinew-cord in the eye-socket, variegated with white and black circles, long and broad, recognizes as "the eye," determines instead, as "the eye-element," the eye-sensitivity of the kind already described among the derived material phenomena in the Description of the Aggregates.

As for the four elements that are its support, the four accompanying phenomena of colour, odour, taste, and nutritive essence, and the life faculty that maintains it — these nine co-arisen material phenomena; and, standing there as well, the twenty kamma-originated material phenomena by way of the body-decad and the sex-decad; and the twenty-four not-clung-to material phenomena by way of the three nutritive-essence-octads beginning with the nutriment-originated one — these remaining fifty-three material phenomena there are, and he does not determine them as "the eye-element." This same method applies also to the ear-element and the rest. In the case of the body-element, however, the remaining material phenomena number forty-three. Some, however, say forty-five, counting the temperature-and-mind-originated [octads] together with sound as nine each.

Thus these five sensitivities, and their five objects — visible form, sound, odour, taste, and the tangible — are ten material phenomena, constituting ten elements. The remaining material phenomena belong solely to the mind-object-element. Now, the state of consciousness that occurs in dependence on the eye, taking visible form as its object, is called the eye-consciousness-element; thus the two sets of five sense-consciousnesses are the five consciousness-elements. The three states of consciousness of the mind-element are the one mind-element; the sixty-eight states of consciousness of the mind-consciousness-element are the mind-consciousness-element; so all eighty-one mundane states of consciousness together are the seven consciousness-elements. The states associated with them, beginning with contact, are the mind-object-element. Thus, herein, ten-and-a-half elements are matter, and seven-and-a-half elements are mind. This is how one determines mind-and-matter by way of the eighteen elements.

Reading note — Ru-Yi

The opening move here — refusing the "lump of flesh bound by a sinew-cord" that ordinary people call "the eye," in favor of the invisible sensitivity (pasāda) that alone counts as eye-element — is one of the sharpest small moments in the whole chapter. It is a direct, almost polemical restatement of the chapter's governing theme in miniature: what convention names as a single, solid thing dissolves, on analysis, into a much narrower technical reality surrounded by dozens of other material phenomena that happen to co-occur with it but do not belong to its definition. The same move will be generalized, a few sections later, into the chariot and house similes.

cakkhupasāda

eye-sensitivity — cakkhu (eye) + pasāda (clarity, sensitivity); the subtle, translucent material phenomenon the Abhidhamma identifies as the actual "eye-element," sharply distinguished here from the visible eyeball that ordinary convention calls "the eye."

anupādinnarūpa

not-clung-to matter — an+upādinna (not grasped/appropriated) + rūpa; material phenomena not produced by kamma (and so not "clung to" as a karmic result), here referring to the octads originating from nutriment, temperature, and mind.

aḍḍhekādasa / aḍḍhaṭṭhamā

"ten-and-a-half" and "seven-and-a-half" — Pāli's idiomatic way of expressing a half short of the next whole number (literally "half [short of] eleven" and "half [short of] eight"); used here because the mind-object-element (dhammadhātu) itself splits between matter (its subtle-matter portion) and mind (its mental-factor portion).

The Methods of the Twelve Bases and the Five Aggregates

Two further methods compress the same analysis into progressively simpler frameworks: the twelve sense bases, familiar from chapter fifteen, and then — "more briefly still" — the five aggregates, with their own internal distinction between the seventeen kinds of "real, actual" matter directly susceptible to insight's comprehension and the ten kinds of matter that are merely the modes, alterations, and boundaries of that real matter.

Pali Source

Ru-Yi Translation

Another determines it by way of the twelve bases. How? Setting aside, exactly as stated for the eye-element, the fifty-three other material phenomena, he determines the mere eye-sensitivity as "the eye-base." Herein, by exactly the method already stated, he determines the ear-, nose-, tongue-, and body-elements as "the ear-, nose-, tongue-, and body-bases"; the five phenomena that are their objects as "the visible-form-, sound-, odour-, taste-, and tangible-bases"; the seven mundane consciousness-elements as "the mind-base"; and the states associated with them, beginning with contact, together with the remaining material phenomena, as "the mind-object-base." Thus, herein, ten-and-a-half bases are matter, and one-and-a-half bases are mind. This is how one determines mind-and-matter by way of the twelve bases.

Another determines it more briefly still, by way of the aggregates. How? Here, a monk reflects: in this body there are the four elements, having four origins; dependent on them, colour, odour, taste, and nutritive essence; the five sensitivities beginning with eye-sensitivity; the heart-basis-matter; sex; the life faculty; and sound, which has two origins — these seventeen material phenomena are susceptible to comprehension and are actual, real matter. But the following ten material phenomena — bodily intimation, verbal intimation, the space-element, the lightness, malleability, and wieldiness of matter, growth, continuity, decay, and impermanence of matter — are not susceptible to comprehension in that same way; they are merely the modes, alterations, and boundaries of other matter, not actual matter, not real matter. Nevertheless, since they are merely the modes, alterations, and boundaries of matter, they are reckoned as "matter." Thus all these twenty-seven material phenomena together are the matter-aggregate; the feeling that arises together with the eighty-one mundane states of consciousness is the feeling-aggregate; the perception associated with it is the perception-aggregate; the formations are the formations-aggregate; consciousness is the consciousness-aggregate. Thus the matter-aggregate is matter, and the four immaterial aggregates are mind. This is how one determines mind-and-matter by way of the five aggregates.

Reading note — Ru-Yi

The five-aggregates version introduces a distinction not made explicit in the earlier methods: within matter itself, only seventeen of the twenty-seven kinds counted here are nipphannarūpa, "actual" or "produced" matter directly graspable by insight, while the remaining ten — intimation, space, and the various "alteration" phenomena like growth and impermanence — are real only in a secondary sense, as ways of describing changes in the genuinely real matter. This is the same distinction the Abhidhamma elsewhere draws between the twenty-eight kinds of matter proper and its finer subdivisions, surfacing here because the aggregate-scheme, unlike the element- or base-schemes, forces every single kind of matter into one basket (rūpakkhandha) regardless of this difference in ontological weight.

Key vocabulary — for Pāli learners
nipphannarūpa / rūparūpa

"produced matter" / "matter that is [really] matter" — two near-synonymous terms for the seventeen kinds of directly real, causally produced matter, contrasted with the ten kinds that are merely modes or alterations of that real matter.

viññatti

intimation — kāyaviññatti (bodily intimation) and vacīviññatti (verbal intimation), the subtle material phenomena by which bodily posture and speech convey meaning; classed among the ten "non-produced" material phenomena because they are alterations of primary matter rather than a distinct substance.

khandha

aggregate — one of the five basic groupings (matter, feeling, perception, formations, consciousness) into which all conditioned phenomena are sorted; the coarsest and most familiar of the frameworks surveyed in this chapter, and the one from which the chapter's very topic, "mind-and-matter," is itself a summary restatement (matter-aggregate = matter, the remaining four = mind).

The Concise Method, and Persevering When the Immaterial Does Not Appear

A sixth method compresses everything into a single canonical formula — "whatever is matter, all such matter is the four great elements and matter derived from them" — before the text turns to a practical difficulty every one of these methods can run into: matter is coarse and shows itself readily, but the immaterial, being subtle, can refuse to become evident at all. The instruction is simply to keep working on matter rather than giving up.

Pali Source

Aparo "yaṃkiñci rūpaṃ sabbaṃ rūpaṃ cattāri mahābhūtāni catunnañca mahābhūtānaṃ upādāyarūpa"nti evaṃ saṃkhitteneva imasmiṃ attabhāve rūpaṃ pariggahetvā, tathā manāyatanañceva dhammāyatanekadesañca nāmanti pariggahetvā "iti idañca nāmaṃ idañca rūpaṃ, idaṃ vuccati nāmarūpa"nti saṅkhepato nāmarūpaṃ vavatthapeti.

Yathā yathā hissa rūpaṃ suvikkhālitaṃ hoti nijjaṭaṃ suparisuddhaṃ, tathā tathā tadārammaṇā arūpadhammā sayameva pākaṭā honti.

Ru-Yi Translation

Another, having grasped matter in this very person quite briefly, thus — "whatever is matter, all such matter is the four great elements, and matter derived from the four great elements" — and having likewise grasped, as mind, the mind-base together with a portion of the mind-object-base, determines mind-and-matter in brief: "such is mind, and such is matter; this is called 'mind-and-matter.'"

But if, having grasped matter by this or that method, and then grasping the immaterial, the immaterial does not become evident to him on account of its subtlety, then, without giving up the task, he should again and again comprehend, attend to, grasp, and determine matter itself. For in whatever measure his grasp of matter becomes well rinsed clean, free of tangle, and thoroughly purified, in that same measure the immaterial states that have it as their object become evident of themselves.

Reading note — Ru-Yi

This concise sixth method quotes almost verbatim a stock canonical definition of matter found across the Nikāyas (the same formula appears, for instance, at Majjhima Nikāya 28 and Aṅguttara Nikāya 11.17) — a reminder that even this treatise's most technical Abhidhammic elaborations are ultimately in service of, and answerable to, the Buddha's own recorded words. The practical instruction that follows then pivots from taxonomy to troubleshooting: having given six increasingly economical ways to determine mind-and-matter, Buddhaghosa anticipates that a real meditator may still get stuck, with matter showing itself readily while mind stays stubbornly obscure, and gives the single remedy that will be illustrated three times over in the similes immediately following — do not abandon the object; keep purifying the coarser thing, and the subtler thing will follow.

Key vocabulary — for Pāli learners
upādāyarūpa

derived matter — upādāya (derived from, based on) + rūpa; the twenty-four (or so) kinds of matter that depend on the four great elements for their existence, as distinct from the great elements (mahābhūta) themselves.

dhuranikkhepa

giving up the task — dhura (burden, responsibility) + nikkhepa (laying down, abandoning); the failure this passage explicitly warns against — the instruction is to keep working the object rather than set it aside when progress stalls.

suvikkhālita

well rinsed clean — su (well) + vikkhālita (rinsed, washed out); one of a small cluster of near-synonyms (with nijjaṭa, "untangled," and suparisuddha, "thoroughly purified") describing the quality matter must reach before the immaterial states dependent on it become evident of their own accord.

Three Similes for Persevering: The Mirror, the Sesame Oil, and the Clearing-Nut

The instruction to persevere is now illustrated three times over, each simile making the same point from a slightly different angle: a man does not throw away a dirty mirror because his reflection is momentarily unclear, an oil-presser does not discard his sesame flour because oil has not yet come, and a water-clarifier does not throw away his clearing-nut because the water has not yet settled. In every case, repeated, patient effort on the coarse material is what allows the subtler result to appear of itself.

Ru-Yi Translation

For just as, when a sighted man, looking at the reflection of his own face in an impure mirror, cannot make out that reflection, he does not, on that account, throw the mirror away, thinking "the reflection cannot be made out" — rather, he polishes it again and again; and then, once the mirror is pure, the reflection becomes evident of itself. And just as one who seeks oil, having poured sesame flour into a trough and sprinkled it with water, does not, when the oil fails to come out after pressing it once or twice, throw the sesame flour away; rather, he sprinkles it with hot water again and again, kneads it, and presses it — and as he does so, clear sesame oil comes out. And just as one wishing to clarify water, having taken a clearing-nut and lowered his hand inside the water-jar, does not, when the water fails to become clear after rubbing it once or twice, throw the clearing-nut away; rather, he rubs it again and again — and as he does so, the mud and silt settle down, and the water becomes clean and clear — in just the same way, that monk, without giving up the task, should again and again comprehend, attend to, grasp, and determine matter itself.

For in whatever measure his grasp of matter becomes well rinsed clean, free of tangle, and thoroughly purified, in that same measure the defilements opposed to it settle down, and the mind becomes clear, like water above the settled silt. And the immaterial states that have it as their object become evident of themselves. This same meaning should be illustrated in the same way by other similes as well — of sugarcane, a thief, an ox, curds, a fish, and so on.

Reading note — Ru-Yi

Buddhaghosa closes this passage by naming five further similes — sugarcane, a thief, an ox, curds, a fish — without actually spelling any of them out; this is the source text's own abbreviation, not an omission on our part. The pattern is recognizable enough from context and from parallel commentarial usage that a reader can reconstruct the gist of each: sugarcane yields its juice only under repeated pressing; a thief caught fleeing must be pursued rather than let go at the first failed attempt; an ox is broken to the yoke through patient, repeated handling rather than a single effort; curds are churned from milk only by sustained agitation; a fish in muddied water is caught only by one who keeps working the net rather than giving up when the water first looks too murky to see through. All are variations on the same lesson as the mirror, the sesame oil, and the clearing-nut: coarse, repeated effort on what is already present yields, of itself, what is not yet apparent.

The Three Modes in Which the Immaterial Becomes Evident: Contact, Feeling, Consciousness

With the second subhead, the chapter turns from determining matter to explaining precisely how, once matter is thoroughly purified, the immaterial states actually present themselves. Three modes are distinguished — by way of contact, by way of feeling, and by way of consciousness — depending on which mental factor happens to stand out most vividly to a given meditator as he examines the very same object, the earth-element's characteristic of hardness, worked through the entire forty-two-aspect body scheme.

Pali Source

Ekassa tāva "pathavīdhātu kakkhaḷalakkhaṇā"tiādinā nayena dhātuyo pariggaṇhantassa paṭhamābhinipāto phasso, taṃsampayuttā vedanā vedanākkhandho, saññā saññākkhandho, saddhiṃ phassena cetanā saṅkhārakkhandho, cittaṃ viññāṇakkhandhoti upaṭṭhāti. Ru-Yi Translation

Now, for one whose grasp of matter has thus become thoroughly purified, the immaterial states become evident in three ways: by way of contact, by way of feeling, or by way of consciousness. How? First, for one grasping the elements by the method beginning "the earth-element has the characteristic of hardness," the first impingement appears as contact; the feeling associated with it, as the feeling-aggregate; perception, as the perception-aggregate; volition, together with contact, as the formations-aggregate; and the state of consciousness, as the consciousness-aggregate. Likewise, for "in the hairs, the earth-element has the characteristic of hardness … and so on … in the in-breath-and-out-breath, the earth-element has the characteristic of hardness," the first impingement appears as contact; the feeling associated with it, as the feeling-aggregate … and so on … the state of consciousness, as the consciousness-aggregate. In this way the immaterial states become evident by way of contact.

For another, in the case of "the earth-element has the characteristic of hardness," the feeling that experiences the flavour of that same object appears as the feeling-aggregate; the perception associated with it, as the perception-aggregate; the contact and volition associated with it, as the formations-aggregate; and the state of consciousness associated with it, as the consciousness-aggregate. Likewise for "in the hairs, the earth-element has the characteristic of hardness … and so on … in the in-breath-and-out-breath, the earth-element has the characteristic of hardness," the feeling that experiences the flavour of that same object appears as the feeling-aggregate … and so on … the state of consciousness associated with it appears as the consciousness-aggregate. In this way the immaterial states become evident by way of feeling.

For yet another, in the case of "the earth-element has the characteristic of hardness," the consciousness that cognizes the object appears as the consciousness-aggregate; the feeling associated with it, as the feeling-aggregate; perception, as the perception-aggregate; contact and volition, as the formations-aggregate. Likewise for "in the hairs, the earth-element has the characteristic of hardness … and so on … in the in-breath-and-out-breath, the earth-element has the characteristic of hardness," the consciousness that cognizes the object appears as the consciousness-aggregate; the feeling associated with it, as the feeling-aggregate; perception, as the perception-aggregate; contact and volition, as the formations-aggregate. In this way the immaterial states become evident by way of consciousness.

By this same method, the application should be made, following through every variation of method, by way of the four elements taken one by one, across the forty-two elemental portions, beginning with "in the kamma-originated hairs, the earth-element has the characteristic of hardness" — and likewise across the remaining approaches to the grasping of matter, beginning with the eye-element and the rest.

Reading note — Ru-Yi

What varies across the three modes is not the underlying reality — the same five-factor cluster of contact, feeling, perception, volition, and consciousness is present in every case — but which single factor happens to stand out to a particular meditator's attention first, with the rest then being noticed as its associates. This is a psychologically realistic touch: Buddhaghosa is not claiming three different meditative experiences exist, but rather that meditators differ in which member of an inseparable cluster catches their attention most vividly, and structuring the instruction so that whichever one it is, the meditator has a template for identifying the rest.

The repeated "…pe…" abbreviation running through this section is the source text's own shorthand for cycling the same formula through the entire forty-two-aspect body scheme built up two sections earlier — hair, body-hair, nails, and so on down to the breath — rather than writing out all forty-two applications in full four times over (once for contact-mode, once for feeling-mode, once for consciousness-mode, and once for the general instruction). It is preserved here as the source itself presents it.

Why Matter Must Be Purified First; No Being Beyond Mind-and-Matter

The subhead closes by explaining why the entire opening section insisted on thoroughly purifying matter before attempting the immaterial: skip ahead too soon, on the strength of only one or two material phenomena becoming evident, and the meditation subject collapses — exactly like the mountain-bred cow from chapter eleven's kasiṇa instructions, who wanders off before she has learned her own pasture. Properly grounded, the eight methods now converge on a single conclusion: beyond this bare mind-and-matter, there is no being, person, god, or Brahmā to be found anywhere.

Sace hi ekasmiṃ vā rūpadhamme upaṭṭhite dvīsu vā rūpaṃ pahāya arūpapariggahaṃ ārabhati kammaṭṭhānato parihāyati, pathavīkasiṇabhāvanāya vuttappakārā pabbateyyā gāvī viya. So evaṃ phassādīnaṃ vasena upaṭṭhite cattāro arūpino khandhe nāmanti, tesaṃ ārammaṇabhūtāni cattāri mahābhūtāni, catunnañca mahābhūtānaṃ upādāyarūpaṃ rūpanti vavatthapeti. Ru-Yi Translation

And since it is only for one whose grasp of matter has thus become thoroughly purified that the immaterial states become evident in these three ways, the effort to grasp the immaterial should be made only on the basis of a thoroughly purified grasp of matter, and not otherwise. For if, when one or two material phenomena have become evident, one abandons matter and undertakes the grasping of the immaterial, one falls away from one's meditation subject — like the mountain-bred cow described in the development of the earth kasiṇa. But for one who makes the effort to grasp the immaterial only on the basis of a thoroughly purified grasp of matter, the meditation subject reaches growth, increase, and fullness.

Having thus made evident, by way of contact and the rest, the four immaterial aggregates, he determines them as "mind"; and he determines, as "matter," the four great elements that are their object, together with the matter derived from the four great elements. Thus, taking all states of the three planes — the eighteen elements, the twelve bases, the five aggregates — he determines them, twofold, as mind and matter, just as one might open a casket with a sword, or split a double palmyra shoot down the middle. He arrives at the conclusion: apart from this mere mind-and-matter, there is no other being, or person, or god, or Brahmā.

Reading note — Ru-Yi

The mountain-bred cow is a direct callback to chapter eleven's own earth-kasiṇa instructions, where a cow raised on steep, unfamiliar terrain wanders off a cliff because she never properly learned her own pasture before roaming further afield — Buddhaghosa reuses her here as a warning against methodological impatience specifically in this new context: jumping to the immaterial before matter is thoroughly known does not merely produce a weaker result, it collapses the practice entirely. The sword-and-casket and split-palmyra-shoot images that follow then supply the section's real payoff: this whole apparatus of eight overlapping methods — four elements, eighteen elements, twelve bases, five aggregates, and the rest — exists to perform one single, clean cut, dividing everything that exists within the three planes into exactly two things, and thereby leaving no room, and no need, for a third thing called "a being."

Key vocabulary — for Pāli learners
pabbateyyā gāvī

the mountain-bred cow — pabbateyya (of the mountain) + gāvī (cow); the simile from chapter eleven's earth-kasiṇa instructions for a meditator who advances to a subtler stage before mastering the coarser one, reused here for exactly the same danger in the rūpa-to-arūpa transition.

tebhūmaka dhammā

"states of the three planes" — tebhūmaka (belonging to the three bhūmi, or planes of existence: sense-sphere, fine-material, and immaterial) + dhammā (states, phenomena); a standard term for all mundane, worldly phenomena, contrasted with the supramundane (lokuttara) states that lie outside its scope.

yamakatālakanda

a double palmyra shoot — yamaka (twin, paired) + tāla (the palmyra palm) + kanda (bulb, shoot); two palmyra shoots grown fused at the root, used here as an image for a single clean division made along a natural seam, just as mind-and-matter split cleanly out of every state without remainder.

Correlating with the Suttas: The Chariot and the Dwelling

Having determined mind-and-matter analytically by eight separate methods, the meditator now checks this conclusion against the Buddha's own words, drawing on a great many discourses to confirm that "being" and "person" are mere worldly conventions rather than realities in their own right — a step Buddhaghosa presents as necessary not for logical proof, but so that the mind can settle, free of confusion, on ground it has now verified for itself against the canon.

Pali Source

So evaṃ yāthāvasarasato nāmarūpaṃ vavatthapetvā suṭṭhutaraṃ "satto puggalo"ti imissā lokasamaññāya pahānatthāya sattasammohassa samatikkamatthāya asammohabhūmiyaṃ cittaṃ ṭhapanatthāya sambahulasuttantavasena "nāmarūpamattamevidaṃ, na satto, na puggalo atthī"ti etamatthaṃ saṃsandetvā vavatthapeti. Vuttañhetaṃ –

"Yathāpi aṅgasambhārā, hoti saddo ratho iti; Evaṃ khandhesu santesu, hoti sattoti sammutī"ti. (saṃ. ni. 1.171);

Aparampi vuttaṃ, "seyyathāpi, āvuso, kaṭṭhañca paṭicca valliñca paṭicca mattikañca paṭicca tiṇañca paṭicca ākāso parivārito agārantveva saṅkhaṃ gacchati, evameva kho, āvuso, aṭṭhiñca paṭicca nhāruñca paṭicca maṃsañca paṭicca cammañca paṭicca ākāso parivārito rūpantveva saṅkhaṃ gacchatī"ti. (ma. ni. 1.306).

Aparampi vuttaṃ –

"Dukkhameva hi sambhoti, dukkhaṃ tiṭṭhati veti ca; Nāññatra dukkhā sambhoti, nāññaṃ dukkhā nirujjhatī"ti. (saṃ. ni. 1.171);

Ru-Yi Translation

Having in this way determined mind-and-matter according to their own true nature, he correlates and confirms this same conclusion — "this is mind-and-matter alone; there is no being, no person" — by way of a great many discourses, so as to more thoroughly abandon this worldly convention of "a being, a person," so as to overcome confusion about beings, and so as to establish the mind on the ground free of confusion. For this was said:

"Just as, when the parts are assembled, there occurs the term 'chariot' — so, when the aggregates are present, there occurs the convention 'a being.'"

It was also said elsewhere: "Just as, friend, when space is enclosed by means of timber, and creepers, and clay, and grass, it comes to be reckoned as 'a dwelling' — just so, friend, when space is enclosed by means of bone, and sinew, and flesh, and skin, it comes to be reckoned as 'matter.'"

It was also said:

"For only suffering arises, only suffering stands and passes away; nothing other than suffering arises, nothing other than suffering ceases."

Reading note — Ru-Yi

This short subhead marks a deliberate shift in method from everything before it: the eight methods of the first subhead were analytical, breaking mind-and-matter down piece by piece; here, instead, Buddhaghosa correlates the resulting conclusion against direct canonical authority, citing the Vajirā Sutta's famous chariot verse (also spoken, in the Saṃyutta Nikāya, by the nun Vajirā to Māra), a parallel building-simile from the Mahāhatthipadopama Sutta that extends the same "conventional label for an assembly of parts" logic from beings to matter itself, and a stark, compressed verse reducing the entire process to suffering alone arising and ceasing — with no experiencer standing behind either the arising or the ceasing. The three citations move outward from the person, to matter itself, and finally to the bare process of arising-and-passing-away, each one stripping away one further foothold for a self to hide in.

Key vocabulary — for Pāli learners
lokasamaññā

worldly convention — loka (world) + samaññā (designation, convention); the everyday manner of speaking that uses terms like "being" and "person," which this whole passage aims to see through without necessarily needing to abandon in ordinary speech.

asammohabhūmi

"the ground free from confusion" — a (not) + sammoha (confusion, delusion) + bhūmi (ground, stage); the settled mental state this correlation-with-the-suttas exercise is meant to establish, distinct from the analytical work of determination itself.

Clarifying Mind-and-Matter by Similes: Chariot, Dwelling, Fist, and Tree

The chapter's fourth and final subhead now gathers together a whole cluster of similes to drive the same point home from every possible angle, beginning with the classic set already introduced by the canonical chariot verse in the previous subhead: a chariot, a dwelling, a fist, a lute, an army, a city, and a tree are all, in the ultimate sense, nothing over and above their assembled parts — and neither, Buddhaghosa now states outright, is a being anything over and above the five aggregates.

Pali Source

Evaṃ anekasatehi suttantehi nāmarūpameva dīpitaṃ, na satto na puggalo. Tasmā yathā akkhacakkapañjaraīsādīsu aṅgasambhāresu ekenākārena saṇṭhitesu "ratho"ti vohāramattaṃ hoti, paramatthato ekekasmiṃ aṅge upaparikkhiyamāne ratho nāma natthi. Yathā ca kaṭṭhādīsu gehasambhāresu ekenākārena ākāsaṃ parivāretvā ṭhitesu "geha"nti vohāramattaṃ hoti, paramatthato gehaṃ nāma natthi. Yathā ca aṅguliaṅguṭṭhādīsu ekenākārena ṭhitesu "muṭṭhī"ti vohāramattaṃ hoti. Doṇitantiādīsu "vīṇā"ti. Hatthiassādīsu "senā"ti. Pākāragehagopurādīsu "nagara"nti. Khandhasākhāpalāsādīsu ekenākārena ṭhitesu "rukkho"ti vohāramattaṃ hoti, paramatthato ekekasmiṃ avayave upaparikkhiyamāne rukkho nāma natthi. Evamevaṃ pañcasu upādānakkhandhesu sati "satto, puggalo"ti vohāramattaṃ hoti, paramatthato ekekasmiṃ dhamme upaparikkhiyamāne "asmīti vā ahanti vā"ti gāhassa vatthubhūto satto nāma natthi. Paramatthato pana nāmarūpamattameva atthīti. Evaṃ passato hi dassanaṃ yathābhūtadassanaṃ nāma hoti.

Ru-Yi Translation

In this way, mind-and-matter alone, and not a being, not a person, is illuminated through many hundreds of discourses. Therefore: just as, when the parts — axle, wheel, frame, pole, and the rest — are arranged in a certain fashion, there is a mere manner of speaking, "a chariot," while in the ultimate sense, when each part is examined, there is no such thing as "a chariot" — and just as, when timber and the rest, forming the parts of a dwelling, stand enclosing space in a certain fashion, there is a mere manner of speaking, "a dwelling," while in the ultimate sense there is no such thing as "a dwelling" — and just as, when the fingers and thumb stand in a certain fashion, there is a mere manner of speaking, "a fist"; when strings are stretched over a body of wood, "a lute"; when elephants and horses are assembled, "an army"; when ramparts, houses, and gates stand together, "a city" — and just as, when the trunk, branches, and foliage stand in a certain fashion, there is a mere manner of speaking, "a tree," while in the ultimate sense, when each constituent part is examined, there is no such thing as "a tree" — in just the same way, when the five aggregates subject to clinging are present, there is a mere manner of speaking, "a being," "a person," while in the ultimate sense, when each single state is examined, there is no such thing as a being that could serve as the basis for the grasping "I am" or "I." In the ultimate sense, however, there exists only mind-and-matter. And seeing in this way is called seeing things as they truly are.

Reading note — Ru-Yi

Seven similes in a single breath — chariot, dwelling, fist, lute, army, city, tree — is a deliberate rhetorical strategy, not mere repetition: the sheer accumulation of everyday, unremarkable examples is itself the argument, since not one of these is a controversial or exotic case. Everyone already agrees that "a fist" or "an army" is nothing more than a convenient label for an arrangement of fingers or of soldiers, with no separate "fist-substance" or "army-substance" lurking behind the label. The entire persuasive weight of the passage rests on transferring that already-obvious insight, by sheer accumulated analogy, onto the one case — "a being," "a person" — where ordinary intuition resists it most strongly.

The closing formula, yathābhūtadassana, "seeing things as they truly are," is the chapter's own name for what purification of view actually consists in, and will recur, essentially unchanged, in the chapter's final definitional sentence. It is worth noting that Buddhaghosa does not claim conventional speech itself is false or must be abandoned — a monk still says "I am going," "this is my bowl" — only that clinging to what such speech seems to presuppose, an underlying self to which the terms refer, is the confusion purification of view removes.

Key vocabulary — for Pāli learners
vohāramatta

"a mere manner of speaking" — vohāra (usage, convention, transaction) + matta (mere, only); the key qualifier running through every simile in this passage, marking each conventional term (chariot, dwelling, fist, being) as real only as a manner of speaking, not as an ultimate reality.

upādānakkhandha

the aggregates subject to clinging — upādāna (clinging, fuel) + khandha (aggregate); the five aggregates considered specifically as objects that can be, and ordinarily are, clung to as "I" or "mine" — the exact basis on which the false convention "a being, a person" gets constructed.

yathābhūtadassana

"seeing things as they truly are" — yathā (as, according to) + bhūta (become, actual) + dassana (seeing); the chapter's own technical name for the correct seeing that constitutes purification of view, distinguished from mere conceptual agreement by being an actual, direct meditative seeing.

Sinking Back and Overshooting: The Two Wrong Views, and Those with Eyes

Abandoning right seeing for the belief that "a being exists" is shown to force a choice between only two possible positions — that this being persists forever, or that it is annihilated — and both, Buddhaghosa argues, are wrong for structurally identical reasons. A well-known passage from the Itivuttaka is then quoted in full, contrasting those who "sink back" into craving for continued existence, those who "overshoot" into craving for annihilation, and "those with eyes" who see what has come to be simply as what has come to be.

Pali Source

Yo panetaṃ yathābhūtadassanaṃ pahāya "satto atthī"ti gaṇhāti. So tassa vināsaṃ anujāneyya avināsaṃ vā. Avināsaṃ anujānanto sassate patati. Vināsaṃ anujānanto ucchede patati. Kasmā? Khīranvayassa dadhino viya tadanvayassa aññassa abhāvato. So "sassato satto"ti gaṇhanto olīyati nāma. "Ucchijjatī"ti gaṇhanto atidhāvati nāma. Tenāha bhagavā –

"Dvīhi, bhikkhave, diṭṭhigatehi pariyuṭṭhitā devamanussā olīyanti eke, atidhāvanti eke, cakkhumanto ca passanti.

"Kathañca, bhikkhave, olīyanti eke? Bhavārāmā, bhikkhave, devamanussā bhavaratā bhavasamuditā. Tesaṃ bhavanirodhāya dhamme desiyamāne cittaṃ na pakkhandati nappasīdati na santiṭṭhati nādhimuccati. Evaṃ kho, bhikkhave, olīyanti eke.

"Kathañca, bhikkhave, atidhāvanti eke? Bhaveneva kho paneke aṭṭīyamānā harāyamānā jigucchamānā vibhavaṃ abhinandanti, yato kira bho ayaṃ attā kāyassa bhedā ucchijjati vinassati, na hoti paraṃmaraṇā, etaṃ santaṃ, etaṃ paṇītaṃ, etaṃ yāthāvanti. Evaṃ kho, bhikkhave, atidhāvanti eke.

"Kathañca, bhikkhave, cakkhumanto passanti? Idha, bhikkhave, bhikkhu bhūtaṃ bhūtato passati, bhūtaṃ bhūtato disvā bhūtassa nibbidāya virāgāya nirodhāya paṭipanno hoti. Evaṃ kho, bhikkhave, cakkhumanto passantī"ti. (itivu. 49).

Ru-Yi Translation

But one who, abandoning this seeing of things as they truly are, grasps "a being exists" — such a person must then hold either that it perishes, or that it does not perish. Holding that it does not perish, he falls into eternalism. Holding that it perishes, he falls into annihilationism. Why? Because, on that view, there would be the coming-to-be of something else in succession, without the original persisting — like curds coming after milk. One who grasps "a being is eternal" is said to "sink back." One who grasps "it is annihilated" is said to "overshoot." Hence the Blessed One said:

"Bhikkhus, gods and humans possessed by two kinds of views sink back, some overshoot, and those with eyes see.

"And how, bhikkhus, do some sink back? Gods and humans, bhikkhus, delight in existence, take pleasure in existence, rejoice in existence. When the Dhamma is being taught to them for the cessation of existence, their minds do not leap forward into it, do not gain confidence in it, do not become settled in it, do not become resolved upon it. It is in this way, bhikkhus, that some sink back.

"And how, bhikkhus, do some overshoot? Some, on the other hand, oppressed, humiliated, and disgusted by existence itself, delight in non-existence, thinking: 'Since, sirs, this self is cut off and destroyed at the breaking-up of the body, and does not exist after death — this is peaceful, this is excellent, this is just as it should be.' It is in this way, bhikkhus, that some overshoot.

"And how, bhikkhus, do those with eyes see? Here, bhikkhus, a bhikkhu sees what has come to be as what has come to be; having seen what has come to be as what has come to be, he practices for disenchantment with it, for dispassion toward it, for its cessation. It is in this way, bhikkhus, that those with eyes see."

Reading note — Ru-Yi

The curds-and-milk logic behind the eternalism/annihilationism trap is worth unpacking: once "a being" is posited as something over and above the five aggregates, its relationship to those aggregates over time becomes unanswerable except in one of two broken ways. Either the being is identical with, and persists through, a succession of momentarily different states — which is eternalism, denying the aggregates' own manifest impermanence — or the being genuinely ends when the aggregates it was "in" end, in which case something is destroyed that was never, on this view, identical with any impermanent aggregate in the first place — which is annihilationism, smuggling in exactly the fixed self it then claims to destroy. Both errors share the same root: taking "being" as a name for something rather than as a convenient label over a process.

The Itivuttaka quotation's third category, cakkhumanto — "those with eyes" — is the chapter's real point of arrival: rather than offering a third theory to debate against eternalism and annihilationism, it dissolves the whole opposition by refusing the shared premise both wrong views depend on. "Seeing what has come to be as what has come to be" (bhūtaṃ bhūtato passati) is simply yathābhūtadassana under another name — and note that its stated consequence is not a further metaphysical claim, but a practical one: dispassion, disenchantment, and progress toward cessation.

Key vocabulary — for Pāli learners
olīyati / atidhāvati

"sinks back" / "overshoots" — ava+lī (to stick, cling, shrink back) and ati+dhāv (to run past, run beyond); the Itivuttaka's own pair of terms for the eternalist's clinging to continued existence and the annihilationist's eager rush toward non-existence, translated here to preserve their vivid, almost physical sense of motion.

bhavārāma / bhavarata

"delighting in existence" / "taking pleasure in existence" — bhava (existence, becoming) + ārāma (delight, park) and rata (attached to, fond of); the psychological description the Buddha gives of ordinary gods and humans, whose comfort with existence itself is what makes the teaching on its cessation so hard to accept.

bhūtaṃ bhūtato passati

"sees what has come to be as what has come to be" — bhūta (that which has become, a formed thing) repeated in subject and adverbial form; the Itivuttaka's own phrase for right seeing, functionally identical to yathābhūtadassana and quoted here as the scriptural anchor for that very term.

The Wooden Mechanism and the Two Bundles of Reeds

Two further similes, each closed by a verse of "the ancients" (porāṇā — earlier commentators Buddhaghosa quotes throughout the Visuddhimagga without naming individually), now capture mind-and-matter's peculiar combination of total emptiness with apparent purposeful activity: a puppet-like wooden mechanism that is lifeless yet moves as if alive, and two bundles of reeds that stand only by leaning on each other and collapse together the instant either one falls.

Pali Source

Tasmā yathā dāruyantaṃ suññaṃ nijjīvaṃ nirīhakaṃ, atha ca pana dārurajjukasamāyogavasena gacchatipi tiṭṭhatipi. Saīhakaṃ sabyāpāraṃ viya khāyati, evamidaṃ nāmarūpampi suññaṃ nijjīvaṃ nirīhakaṃ, atha ca pana aññamaññasamāyogavasena gacchatipi tiṭṭhatipi. Saīhakaṃ sabyāpāraṃ viya khāyatīti daṭṭhabbaṃ. Tenāhu porāṇā –

"Nāmañca rūpañca idhatthi saccato, Na hettha satto manujo ca vijjati; Suññaṃ idaṃ yantamivābhisaṅkhataṃ, Dukkhassa puñjo tiṇakaṭṭhasādiso"ti.

Na kevalañcetaṃ dāruyantupamāya, aññāhipi naḷakalāpīādīhi upamāhi vibhāvetabbaṃ – yathā hi dvīsu naḷakalāpīsu aññamaññaṃ nissāya ṭhapitāsu ekā ekissā upatthambho hoti, ekissā patamānāya itarāpi patati, evamevaṃ pañcavokārabhave nāmarūpaṃ aññamaññaṃ nissāya pavattati, ekaṃ ekassa upatthambho hoti. Maraṇavasena ekasmiṃ patamāne itarampi patati. Tenāhu porāṇā –

"Yamakaṃ nāmarūpañca, ubho aññoññanissitā; Ekasmiṃ bhijjamānasmiṃ, ubho bhijjanti paccayā"ti.

Ru-Yi Translation

Therefore it should be seen thus: just as a wooden mechanism is empty, lifeless, without any striving of its own, and yet, by the conjunction of wood and cords, it moves and it stands still, and appears as though it were striving, as though it had purpose — in just the same way this mind-and-matter, too, is empty, lifeless, without any striving of its own, and yet, by the conjunction of one part with another, it moves and it stands still, and appears as though it were striving, as though it had purpose. Hence the ancients said:

"Mind and matter truly exist here; but no being, no person, is found herein. Empty is this, constructed like a mechanism — a mere heap of suffering, like grass and sticks."

Nor should this meaning be made clear only by the simile of the wooden mechanism — it should also be made clear by other similes, beginning with that of two bundles of reeds: just as, when two bundles of reeds are set standing, leaning one upon the other, each is a support for the other, and when one falls, the other falls too — in just the same way, in a mode of existence with five constituents, mind-and-matter occurs in dependence on one another, each being a support for the other; and when one falls, through death, the other falls as well. Hence the ancients said:

"Twin are mind and matter, each dependent on the other; when one breaks apart, both break apart, since each is a condition for the other."

Reading note — Ru-Yi

The wooden mechanism (dāruyanta) — essentially a marionette or automaton worked by hidden cords — is a striking choice of image precisely because it moves convincingly enough to seem alive to an untrained observer, which is exactly the point: mind-and-matter's combination of total emptiness of self with genuinely functional, purposeful-looking activity is not a paradox to be explained away but the actual nature of conditioned existence, correctly seen. The verse's closing image, "a mere heap of suffering, like grass and sticks," deliberately deflates any residual grandeur the mechanism simile might suggest — this is not a marvel to admire but dukkha to be seen through.

The two-bundles-of-reeds simile makes a subtly different point from the mechanism: where the mechanism illustrates emptiness-with-apparent-agency, the reed bundles illustrate mutual, symmetrical dependence — neither mind nor matter is primary or supports the other unilaterally, and neither can survive the other's collapse. This directly sets up the drum-and-sound and blind-man-and-cripple similes still to come, which will develop this same mutual-dependence theme from two further angles.

dāruyanta

a wooden mechanism — dāru (wood, timber) + yanta (a mechanical contrivance, device); a puppet or automaton worked by strings, cited here as the classic image for something that moves purposefully while being, in itself, entirely lifeless and empty.

porāṇā

"the ancients" — a standing term throughout the Visuddhimagga for earlier generations of commentators whose verses Buddhaghosa quotes approvingly without naming individually, as here and at several further points in this same chapter.

naḷakalāpī

a bundle of reeds — naḷa (reed) + kalāpī (bundle, sheaf); two such bundles leaned together so that each supports the other is a well-known canonical image (paralleling the Nidānasaṃyutta's own reed-bundles simile for consciousness and name-and-form) for strictly mutual, symmetrical dependence.

The Drum and Its Sound: Distinct, Yet Arisen in Dependence

A further simile addresses a subtlety the reed-bundles image leaves open: mind and matter are not merely mutually dependent, they are also entirely distinct from one another, neither mixed nor identical, exactly as a drum and the sound it produces are two clearly different things even though the sound could never occur without the drum being struck. Six verses — one for each sense door, the first and last given in full and the middle four abbreviated by the source's own peyyāla convention — apply this same logic to every mode of experience.

Pali Source

Yathā ca daṇḍābhihataṃ bheriṃ nissāya sadde pavattamāne aññā bherī, añño saddo, bherisaddā asammissā, bherī saddena suññā, saddo bheriyā suñño, evamevaṃ vatthudvārārammaṇasaṅkhātaṃ rūpaṃ nissāya nāme pavattamāne aññaṃ rūpaṃ, aññaṃ nāmaṃ, nāmarūpā asammissā, nāmaṃ rūpena suññaṃ, rūpaṃ nāmena suññaṃ, apica kho bheriṃ paṭicca saddo viya rūpaṃ paṭicca nāmaṃ pavattati. Tenāhu porāṇā –

"Na cakkhuto jāyare phassapañcamā, Na rūpato no ca ubhinnamantarā; Hetuṃ paṭiccappabhavanti saṅkhatā, Yathāpi saddo pahaṭāya bheriyā.

"Na sotato jāyare phassapañcamā, Na saddato no ca ubhinnamantarā…pe….

"Na ghānato jāyare phassapañcamā, Na gandhato no ca ubhinnamantarā…pe….

"Na jivhāto jāyare phassapañcamā, Na rasato no ca ubhinnamantarā…pe….

"Na kāyato jāyare phassapañcamā, Na phassato no ca ubhinnamantarā…pe….

"Na vatthurūpā pabhavanti saṅkhatā, Na cāpi dhammāyatanehi niggatā; Hetuṃ paṭiccappabhavanti saṅkhatā, Yathāpi saddo pahaṭāya bheriyā"ti.

Ru-Yi Translation

And just as, when sound occurs in dependence on a drum struck with a stick, the drum is one thing and the sound another — drum and sound are not mixed together, the drum is empty of the sound, the sound is empty of the drum — in just the same way, when mind occurs in dependence on matter reckoned as basis, door, and object, matter is one thing and mind another; mind and matter are not mixed together, mind is empty of matter, matter is empty of mind; and yet, just as sound occurs dependent on the drum, mind occurs dependent on matter. Hence the ancients said:

"Not from the eye alone are the five, headed by contact, born; nor from visible form alone, nor yet from between the two: conditioned things arise dependent on a cause — just as sound arises from a drum that is struck.

"Not from the ear alone are the five, headed by contact, born; nor from sound alone, nor yet from between the two … [and so on, as before] …

"Not from the nose alone are the five, headed by contact, born; nor from odour alone, nor yet from between the two … [and so on, as before] …

"Not from the tongue alone are the five, headed by contact, born; nor from taste alone, nor yet from between the two … [and so on, as before] …

"Not from the body alone are the five, headed by contact, born; nor from the tangible alone, nor yet from between the two … [and so on, as before] …

"Not from the basis-matter alone do conditioned things arise, nor have they issued forth from mind-objects alone: conditioned things arise dependent on a cause — just as sound arises from a drum that is struck."

Reading note — Ru-Yi

The drum simile completes a logical structure the reed bundles left half-finished: reeds explained mutual dependence, but a reader might wrongly conclude from mutual dependence that mind and matter simply are each other, or blend into some third hybrid thing. The drum and its sound block exactly that misreading — dependence does not entail mixture. Every school of Buddhist commentary reaches for some version of this drum-and-sound (or lute-and-sound, or fire-and-smoke) pairing precisely because it isolates dependent arising from identity so cleanly: no one is tempted to think the sound of a drum simply is the drum, even while agreeing the sound could not exist without it.

The six-verse structure, running through eye, ear, nose, tongue, body, and finally the mind-door itself, methodically closes off every possible sense-channel as a potential hiding place for a self: whichever door experience arrives through, the same threefold refutation applies — not from the sense faculty alone, not from the object alone, and not from some third thing "between" the two, but only from conditions, dependently. The abbreviated middle four verses are the source text's own peyyāla shorthand, reproduced here as given rather than expanded, since expanding them would misrepresent what the Pāli itself actually writes out in full.

The Blind Man and the Cripple: Purification of View Complete

The chapter's final simile is its most memorable: neither mind nor matter has any power of its own to act, yet together, like a man blind from birth carrying a cripple on his shoulders and being steered by him, they accomplish everything a living being does. Five closing verses restate the point once more, comparing mind-and-matter to people and the boat that carries them across the ocean — dependent on each other, and on nothing else. With this, Buddhaghosa gives purification of view its final definition and closes the eighteenth chapter.

Pali Source

Apicettha nāmaṃ nittejaṃ na sakena tejena pavattituṃ sakkoti, na khādati, na pivati, na byāharati, na iriyāpathaṃ kappeti. Rūpampi nittejaṃ na sakena tejena pavattituṃ sakkoti. Na hi tassā khāditukāmatā, nāpi pivitukāmatā, na byāharitukāmatā, na iriyāpathaṃ kappetukāmatā, atha kho nāmaṃ nissāya rūpaṃ pavattati, rūpaṃ nissāya nāmaṃ pavattati, nāmassa khāditukāmatāya pivitukāmatāya byāharitukāmatāya iriyāpathaṃ kappetukāmatāya sati rūpaṃ khādati, pivati, byāharati, iriyāpathaṃ kappeti.

Imassa panatthassa vibhāvanatthāya imaṃ upamaṃ udāharanti – yathā jaccandho ca pīṭhasappī ca disāpakkamitukāmā assu, jaccandho pīṭhasappiṃ evamāha "ahaṃ kho bhaṇe, sakkomi pādehi pādakaraṇīyaṃ kātuṃ, natthi ca me cakkhūni yehi samavisamaṃ passeyya"nti. So tuṭṭhahaṭṭho jaccandho pīṭhasappiṃ aṃsakūṭaṃ āropesi. Pīṭhasappī jaccandhassa aṃsakūṭe nisīditvā evamāha "vāmaṃ muñca dakkhiṇaṃ gaṇha, dakkhiṇaṃ muñca vāmaṃ gaṇhā"ti. Ru-Yi Translation

And here, moreover, mind is without power of its own; it cannot occur by its own power; it does not eat, does not drink, does not speak, does not perform any bodily posture. Matter, too, is without power of its own; it cannot occur by its own power. For matter has no desire to eat, no desire to drink, no desire to speak, no desire to perform any posture; rather, matter occurs in dependence on mind, mind occurs in dependence on matter; and it is when mind has the desire to eat, the desire to drink, the desire to speak, the desire to perform a posture, that matter eats, drinks, speaks, and performs a posture.

To make this meaning clear, they cite this simile: suppose a man blind from birth and a cripple wished to set out in some direction. The man blind from birth said to the cripple: "See here, friend — I am capable of doing what is to be done with the feet, but I have no eyes with which to see what is even and what is uneven." The cripple, in turn, said to the man blind from birth: "See here, friend — I am capable of doing what is to be done with the eyes, but I have no feet with which to go forward or go back." Delighted, the man blind from birth lifted the cripple onto his shoulders. Seated on the shoulders of the man blind from birth, the cripple said: "Let go the left, take the right; let go the right, take the left." Herein: the man blind from birth, being without power, being weak, cannot go by his own power, by his own strength; the cripple, too, being without power, being weak, cannot go by his own power, by his own strength; and yet, in dependence on one another, their going does not fail to occur. In just the same way, mind, too, is without power; it does not arise by its own power; it does not occur in this or that activity. Matter, too, is without power; it does not arise by its own power; it does not occur in this or that activity; and yet, in dependence on one another, their arising and occurrence does not fail to happen. Hence it is said:

"Not by their own strength are they born, nor by their own strength do they stand; following the power of things other than themselves, conditioned things are born, weak in themselves.

"From another's condition they are born, arisen from another's object; by conditions that are objects, and by things other than themselves, are these produced.

"Just as, in dependence on a boat, people travel upon the ocean — just so, in dependence on matter, the mind-body occurs.

"And just as, in dependence on people, a boat travels upon the ocean — just so, in dependence on mind, the matter-body occurs.

"Dependent on each other do they travel — people and boat, upon the ocean; just so do mind and matter, both dependent on each other."

Thus, for one who determines mind-and-matter by these various methods, overcoming the perception of a being, the seeing of mind-and-matter as they truly are, established on the ground free from confusion, should be understood as purification of view. "The determination of mind-and-matter" and "the delimitation of formations" are likewise designations for this same thing.

Reading note — Ru-Yi

The blind-man-and-cripple simile is one of the most quoted images in the entire Buddhist commentarial tradition (it reappears, with variations, in discussions of consciousness and name-and-form throughout Abhidhamma literature), and it earns that reputation by solving a problem the earlier similes in this chapter leave slightly open: the wooden mechanism explained empty-yet-purposeful movement, the reed bundles and the boat-and-passengers verses explained mutual dependence, but neither quite captured the specific, almost comic image of two incapacities combining into a working competence. Neither the blind man nor the cripple can travel a single step alone; joined, they navigate perfectly — which is exactly the relationship between a mind that can direct action but cannot itself move a body, and a body that can move but has no capacity to want or intend anything.

The chapter's closing sentence gives purification of view two further names — nāmarūpavavatthāna ("the determination of mind-and-matter") and saṅkhāraparicchedo ("the delimitation of formations") — both of which will recur as this same accomplishment gets referenced from later chapters, particularly once the treatise turns to tracing conditions between mind-and-matter and its causes in the purification that follows. With this eighteenth chapter's close, the "body" of wisdom's five remaining purifications has begun: mind-and-matter has now been seen, by eight analytical methods and a full gallery of similes, exactly as it is — leaving purification by overcoming doubt, which extends this same seeing to mind-and-matter's causes and conditions across time, as the very next task.

Key vocabulary — for Pāli learners
jaccandha / pīṭhasappī

one blind from birth / a cripple — jacca (by birth) + andha (blind), and pīṭha (a stool or bench, hence one who must move by sliding along the ground) + sappin (creeping, crawling); the two figures of this chapter's most famous simile, each incapable alone, fully capable combined.

nāmakāya / rūpakāya

the mind-body / the matter-body — nāma or rūpa + kāya (body, collection); a pairing of terms used in the closing verses to name mind and matter each as its own "body" or collection, precisely parallel to the people and the boat that depend on one another to cross the ocean.

saṅkhāraparicchedo

"the delimitation of formations" — saṅkhāra (formations, conditioned things) + pariccheda (delimitation, precise boundary-marking); one of two alternate names given, in the chapter's final sentence, for purification of view itself, alongside nāmarūpavavatthāna, "the determination of mind-and-matter."
