Chapter 13 · Abhiññāniddesa · Complete

The Direct Knowledges

This page carries Ru-Yi's translation of the Visuddhimagga's chapter 13, Abhiññāniddesa ("The Discussion of the Direct Knowledges") — the second and final chapter treating the fruits of mastered concentration, before the treatise turns to wisdom (paññā) in earnest. It develops the divine ear, the reading of others' minds, and the recollection of past lives — the last opening into a complete narrative account of the universe's own destruction and re-formation by fire, water, and wind — before closing with the divine eye and a technical survey mapping all seven direct knowledges onto the Abhidhamma's own object-classification scheme.

The Divine Ear: Why "Divine," and What It Actually Hears

The thirteenth chapter opens by taking up, in order, the five direct knowledges left over from the last — beginning with the divine ear-element. Rather than re-explaining what the last chapter's commentary on jhāna and the direct-knowledge formula already covered, Buddhaghosa signals that only what is genuinely new to each knowledge will be discussed from here on. The divine ear earns its name three ways at once: it resembles the deva's own faculty (unclouded by bile, phlegm, or blood, and so able to register even a distant object); it arises from the same power of developed effort that produces every other direct knowledge; and it is "divine" simply because it is acquired by, and grounded in, the divine abidings of jhāna itself. Its actual range, the canonical formula makes clear, is total in two directions at once — sound of any kind, divine or human, and sound at any distance, from another world-system to the smallest creature clinging to one's own body.

Ru-Yi Translation

Now the order of exposition has arrived at the divine ear-element. Herein, among the three remaining direct knowledges beyond this one, the meaning of "with his mind thus concentrated" and so on should be understood in the way already stated. But everywhere we shall describe only what is distinctive. There, "with the divine ear-element": it is divine because it resembles the divine. For devas possess a divine, faculty-based ear-element, produced by good conduct, unobstructed by bile, phlegm, blood, and the like, and, through its freedom from such impurities, able to receive an object even at a distance. And this monk's own knowledge-based ear-element, produced by the power of developed effort, is exactly of that same kind — hence it is divine, because it resembles the divine. Moreover, it is divine because it is acquired by means of a divine abiding, and because it is itself grounded in a divine abiding. It is an "element" (dhātu) in the sense of hearing, and in the sense of being lifeless; and it is called an "element" also because it performs the function of an ear-element.

"Purified": completely pure, free of defilement. "Surpassing the human": having gone beyond the range of the human, surpassing, having transcended, the fleshly ear-element which is human in its hearing of sound. "He hears both kinds of sound": he hears two kinds of sound. Which two? Divine and human — that is, the sounds of both devas and human beings. By this, the exhaustive range in terms of kind should be understood. "Both far and near": he hears sounds that are far, even in another world-system, and sounds that are near, down to the sound of a creature clinging to his own body. By this, the exhaustive range in terms of location should be understood.

Reading note — Ru-Yi

The three-fold etymology of "divine" — resembling a deva's faculty, arising from the same effort-based power behind every direct knowledge, and grounded in a divine abiding — is a pattern the chapter will now repeat almost verbatim for the divine eye later on, making clear that "divine" in this technical vocabulary never means literally borrowed from a god, but rather structurally parallel to what a god's own faculties can do.

The example given for "near" sound — a creature clinging to one's own body — is a deliberately extreme case, chosen precisely because it is the least glamorous possible instance of this power: the divine ear's usefulness is not confined to hearing something impressively far away, but extends symmetrically down to the smallest, most mundane sound imaginable.

the state of resembling the divine — dibba (divine) + sadisa (resembling) + -tta (abstract-noun suffix); the recurring etymological basis for calling any of these developed faculties "divine," here and again for the divine eye.

atikkantamānusika

surpassing the human — ati (beyond) + kkanta (gone, past) + mānusika (human); describing a faculty that has gone past the ordinary range of the fleshly sense-organ it is paired with.

nippadesapariyādāna

exhaustive range without remainder in terms of location — ni- (without) + padesa (place, location) + pariyādāna (exhausting, using up completely); the technical term for the divine ear's total spatial coverage, from another world-system down to one's own skin.

Developing the Divine Ear: From the Roar of a Lion to the Rustle of an Ant

The method for developing the divine ear works by systematic contrast rather than direct effort: the meditator first attends, in order from grossest to subtlest, to an entire catalogue of ordinary sounds — a lion's roar in the forest, a monastery gong, drums, conch-shells, novices reciting at full volume, ordinary conversation, birdsong, wind, footsteps, boiling water, palm-leaves crackling in the heat, down to the faintest sound of small insects — deliberately working through every one of the ten directions in turn. Once this contrast-based attention has been cultivated to full clarity, a single mind-door adverting toward any one of these sounds triggers the actual arising of the divine ear itself, in the same javana-sequence structure already explained for the power of resolve. From that point on, the meditator can hear at will, without needing to re-enter the basis-jhāna each time, and can even pick out one specific sound — a conch here, a drum there — from within an otherwise indistinguishable roar of celebration reaching all the way to the Brahmā-world.

Ru-Yi Translation

But how should this be developed? Having entered the basis-jhāna for direct knowledge and emerged, that monk, with the preliminary-work consciousness of concentration, should first advert to the grosser sound, farther off in the ordinary path of hearing — the sound of lions and the like in the forest. Then, in the monastery: the sound of the gong, the sound of the drum, the sound of the conch, the sound of novices and young monks reciting with all their strength, the sound of those engaged in ordinary conversation — "what is it, venerable sir," "what is it, friend," and so on — the sound of birds, the sound of wind, the sound of footsteps, the sizzling sound of boiling water, the sound of palm-leaves drying and crackling in the heat, the sound of small ants and the like — thus, beginning from the grossest of all, subtler and subtler sounds should be adverted to in turn. By this, the sign of sounds in the eastern direction should be attended to. Likewise the sign of sounds in the western, northern, southern, lower, and upper directions, and in the south-eastern, south-western, north-western, and north-eastern intermediate directions. The sign of both gross and subtle sounds should be attended to. To him, those sounds become clear even to his ordinary consciousness — but to the consciousness of preliminary-work concentration, they become exceedingly clear.

To one attending to the sign of sounds in this way, thinking, "now the divine ear-element is about to arise," taking any one of those sounds as object, a mind-door adverting arises. When that has ceased, four or five javana-moments run their course, of which the first three or four — called preliminary work, access, conformity, and change-of-lineage — are of the sense sphere, while the fourth or fifth is the absorption-consciousness itself, of the fine-material sphere, pertaining to the fourth jhāna. Herein, the knowledge that arises together with that absorption-consciousness should be understood as this very divine ear-element. From then on, he has fallen, as it were, into that stream of hearing. Making it firmly established, thinking, "within this range I hear sound," he should delimit a space of just one finger-breadth and then expand it. From there, delimiting again and again by way of two finger-breadths, four finger-breadths, eight finger-breadths, a span, a cubit, the interior of a room, the front of a mansion, a monastery hall, a monastic compound, an alms-resort village, a country, and so on, up to a whole world-system, or even further still, he should expand it.

Having thus attained this direct knowledge, this monk, hearing sounds that have reached within the range already touched by the basis-jhāna's object, hears them by the power of direct knowledge alone, even without entering the basis-jhāna again. And hearing in this way, even if there is a single uproar reaching all the way to the Brahmā-world, made up of the sounds of conches, drums, and kettledrums together, if he wishes to distinguish them separately, he is indeed able to determine, "this is the sound of a conch, this is the sound of a drum."

The discussion of the divine ear-element is finished.

Reading note — Ru-Yi

The method's opening move — starting with the grossest, most distant sound (a lion's roar in the forest) rather than the nearest and quietest — inverts what might seem the intuitive teaching order, and the reason becomes clear as the list continues: only a genuinely loud, unmistakable sound gives the beginner's still-untrained attention something to firmly grasp before it is asked to discriminate progressively subtler and subtler stimuli, ending at the threshold of audibility itself (the rustle of ants).

The closing image — picking out one specific sound, a conch here, a drum there, from within a single indistinguishable roar reaching the Brahmā-world — mirrors exactly the discriminating skill practiced during training itself, just performed now at a vastly larger scale: the whole method has been building, from its very first step, toward the ability to resolve one sound out of many, whether that many is a village's evening noise or an entire cosmos celebrating at once.

saddanimitta

the sign of sound — sadda (sound) + nimitta (sign, mark); the mental image or trace of a sound taken as the meditation object, developed through directional and gross-to-subtle attention exactly as a kasiṇa's visual sign is developed.

thāmajāta

become firm, firmly established — thāma (firmness, strength) + jāta (become, arisen); describing the divine ear once it has stabilized enough to be deliberately expanded outward by measured stages.

ekakolāhala

a single uproar, one great commotion — eka (one) + kolāhala (uproar, tumult); the test case for a fully matured divine ear — the ability to discriminate individual sounds within what would otherwise be pure undifferentiated noise.

Reading Another's Mind: The Color of the Heart's Blood

The chapter turns next to the knowledge of penetrating others' minds. Its etymology is deliberately compact — "pariya" simply means a delimiting or determining, so "cetopariyañāṇa" is knowledge that delimits the heart, that is, another's mind. Its actual method of development is genuinely striking: it is developed by way of the divine eye, using the divine eye as its own preliminary work. The meditator expands his light, then looks with the divine eye at the physical basis of the heart and observes the literal color of the blood flowing there — reddish like a banyan fruit for a joyful mind, blackish like a rose-apple for a sad mind, clear like refined sesame oil for an equanimous mind. Only once this color-based method has been made thoroughly firm does the ability generalize, so that a mind can eventually be read directly, mind meeting mind, without any further need to look at the heart's blood at all — a shift the commentary is careful to qualify with an old exchange from the Aṭṭhakathā about how, exactly, this same knowledge can possibly operate for beings in the formless realm, who have no heart-basis to look at in the first place.

Ru-Yi Translation

In the discussion of the knowledge of penetrating minds: herein, "for the sake of the knowledge of penetrating minds" — "pariyāti" means "pariyaṃ," in the sense of "delimits." The delimiting of the heart (ceto) is "cetopariyaṃ." That which is both a delimiting of the heart and knowledge is "cetopariyañāṇaṃ." "For its sake" is what is meant. "Of other beings": of the remaining beings, setting oneself aside. "Of other persons": this too is one in meaning with the preceding — the variation in wording is simply for the sake of those to be guided, and for the sake of elegance in teaching. "With his own heart, their heart": with his own mind, their mind. "He understands, having delimited": having delimited it, he knows it in its various forms, by way of being affected by passion and so on.

But how should this knowledge be developed? This is accomplished by means of the divine eye — that is its preliminary work. Therefore, that monk, having expanded his light, and having seen with the divine eye the color of the blood occurring in dependence on another's heart-basis, should search out the mind. For when a joyful mind is occurring, that blood is red, like a banyan fruit. When a sad mind is occurring, it is blackish, like a rose-apple fruit. When an equanimous mind is occurring, it is clear, like refined sesame oil. Therefore, by seeing the color of another's heart-blood and thereby searching out the mind, thinking, "this form arises from the joy faculty, this from the sadness faculty, this from the equanimity faculty" — in this way the knowledge of penetrating minds should be made firm. For when it has been made firm in this way, he comes, in due course, to understand all sense-sphere consciousness, and fine-material and immaterial-sphere consciousness as well, simply by passing from mind to mind, even without seeing the heart's physical form at all. This too has been said in the commentary: "In the formless realm, one wishing to know another's mind — whose heart-basis does he see, whose alteration of faculty does he observe? None at all. This is the domain of one possessing power: adverting to a mind, wherever it may be, he knows that mind in its sixteen-fold classification. This account, however, is given from the standpoint of one who has not yet made this attainment firm."

Reading note — Ru-Yi

The heart's-blood-color method is a striking instance of this treatise's consistent pattern: even a power as seemingly abstract as reading another's mind is given an entirely concrete, physiologically specific starting point, treated no differently from learning to recognize a kasiṇa's visual sign. Only once that concrete method is mastered does the power become genuinely abstract, generalizing to a mind-to-mind reading that requires no physical basis at all.

The Aṭṭhakathā exchange about beings in the formless realm is a small but important piece of intellectual honesty on the commentary's part: rather than silently ignoring the case that its own physiological method cannot handle (a formless being has no heart at all), it explicitly raises the objection and answers it by relocating the power's true basis from the concrete method to the trained faculty itself — the color-reading was only ever training wheels, not the power's actual mechanism.

Key vocabulary — for Pāli learners
cetopariyañāṇa

the knowledge of penetrating (others') minds — ceto (mind, heart) + pariya (delimiting, from pari + i, "to go around, encompass") + ñāṇa (knowledge); knowledge that determines and delimits the state of another's mind.

hadayarūpa

the heart's physical form, the heart-basis — hadaya (heart) + rūpa (form, matter); in Abhidhamma physiology, the material support on which mind-consciousness is said to depend, and the object this method's preliminary divine-eye work actually observes.

soḷasappabheda

sixteen-fold classification — soḷasa (sixteen) + pabheda (division, classification); referring to the eight paired categories of mind (with-lust/without-lust, and so on) about to be detailed in the chapter's next section.

The Sixteen Kinds of Mind, Paired Two by Two

Having explained how the knowledge of penetrating minds is actually developed, the commentary now works through the full canonical list of sixteen mental states this knowledge is meant to discern, arranged as eight opposed pairs, matching each classification to its precise Abhidhamma boundary. Lust and its absence, hatred and its absence, delusion and its absence — each pair is drawn along strict technical lines rather than loose ordinary usage, and the commentary is careful to flag two places where different elder-lineages actually disagreed about exactly which borderline mental states belong on which side of the divide. The remaining five pairs — contracted/distracted, exalted/unexalted, surpassed/unsurpassed, concentrated/unconcentrated, and liberated/unliberated — extend the classification from momentary affective coloring to a mind's overall structural condition, closing with liberation itself, understood here through all five of its own classical types.

Ru-Yi Translation

As for "a mind with lust" and so on: the eightfold consciousness accompanied by greed should be understood as "a mind with lust." The remaining wholesome and indeterminate consciousness of the four planes is "a mind without lust." But these four states of mind — the two sad-minded consciousnesses and the two doubt-and-restlessness-accompanied consciousnesses — do not fall into this particular pair's classification. Some elders, however, do include them here as well.

As for "with delusion, without delusion": here, by the method proper to the individual pairing, only the two states accompanied by doubt and restlessness are "with delusion" — but since delusion is present in all unwholesome states, all twelve kinds of unwholesome consciousness should also be understood as "a mind with delusion." Fine-material and immaterial-sphere consciousness is "exalted." The remainder is "unexalted." All consciousness pertaining to the three planes is "surpassable." The supramundane is "unsurpassable." Consciousness that has reached access or absorption is "concentrated." Consciousness that has attained neither is "unconcentrated." Consciousness that has reached liberation by substitution of opposites, by suppression, by cutting off, by tranquilizing, or by escape is "liberated." Thus, the monk who has gained the knowledge of penetrating minds understands, in every one of these ways, whether it be a mind with lust ... or a mind unliberated, that it is a mind unliberated.

The discussion of the knowledge of penetrating minds is finished.

Reading note — Ru-Yi

The commentary's twice-repeated aside — "some elders, however, do include them here as well" — is a small but genuine window into how this tradition handled unresolved technical disagreement: rather than silently picking a side or artificially forcing consensus, Buddhaghosa records the majority classification as the working position while explicitly preserving the dissenting lineage's view, leaving the boundary case (where exactly do sadness, doubt, and restlessness belong in a lust/non-lust or hatred/non-hatred binary) visibly unsettled rather than falsely resolved.

The five kinds of liberation closing this list — by substitution of opposites, suppression, cutting off, tranquilizing, and escape — form a graduated sequence moving from the weakest and most temporary (a single wholesome thought briefly displacing an unwholesome one) to the most permanent (full liberation itself), and their appearance here as the final classification a mind-reader must discern suggests that this power's deepest use is not gossip or surveillance but a genuinely diagnostic tool for gauging exactly how far another person's practice has actually progressed.

Six Kinds of Rememberers, and How Far Back Each One Reaches

The chapter's third direct knowledge, recollection of past lives, opens with a careful etymology — "pubbenivāsa" names the aggregates that were once "dwelt in" during former existences, whether known through one's own consciousness or, in rarer cases, through another's report, a capacity the commentary notes is available only to Buddhas. It then ranks, with striking precision, exactly six kinds of beings capable of this recollection, each bounded by a fixed maximum reach measured in world-cycles: non-Buddhist ascetics can reach back only forty aeons, on account of their comparatively weak wisdom (lacking the analytical grasp of name-and-form); ordinary disciples can reach back a hundred or a thousand aeons; the eighty great disciples, a hundred thousand aeons; the two chief disciples, one incalculable period plus a hundred thousand aeons; Paccekabuddhas, two incalculable periods plus a hundred thousand aeons; and Buddhas alone have no limit whatsoever.

Firefly-Light to Sunlight: How Each Rememberer's Vision Differs

Having ranked the six kinds of rememberers by reach, the commentary now distinguishes them by method and by the very quality of the seeing itself. Non-Buddhist ascetics can only follow the aggregates forward in strict sequence, one life after another without skipping — like a blind man who cannot leave the stick he is following — while ordinary and great disciples can also jump directly by way of death and rebirth-linking. The two chief disciples and Paccekabuddhas dispense with sequence entirely, seeing one life's death and the very next life's rebirth directly, arrow-like, never missing or fumbling; and Buddhas need no procedure of any kind, any point in incalculable aeons being equally available to them at will. The commentary closes this comparison with two parallel sets of similes — the quality of light by which each sees, from firefly-glow up to a thousand-rayed autumn sun, and the quality of the path each walks, from a blind man's cane-tip journey up to travel along a great high road.

Working Backward: From the Last Meal to the Moment of Conception

Paragraph 403 lays out the actual step-by-step method a beginner uses to recollect past lives — and it is, quite deliberately, a method of working strictly backward. Starting from the very last sitting posture just taken, the meditator reverses through the day's events in scrupulous reverse order: preparing the seat, entering the lodging, putting away bowl and robe, the meal itself, the walk back from the village, the almsround, leaving the monastery that morning, paying respects at the shrine and Bodhi tree, washing and receiving the bowl — all the way back through the previous night in its three watches. The same backward method is then repeated across ever-larger units of time — a second day, then several days, then ten days, a fortnight, a month, a year — until the meditator reaches back to the very moment of conception in this present life, and beyond it, to the moment of death in the life immediately prior. The commentary is candid that this final crossing is genuinely difficult, since the name-and-form of the previous existence has ceased without remainder, leaving that boundary point cloaked in what it calls "impenetrable darkness" — met not with discouragement, but with patient, repeated re-entry into the basis-jhāna, illustrated by the memorable simile of a strong man doggedly felling a great tree with a dulling axe, returning again and again to the forge to have it resharpened rather than ever abandoning the task.

The insistence on strict reverse order is not incidental stylistic preference but the method's actual engine: each step backward is anchored to the step immediately following it in ordinary forward time, so that nothing can be skipped without breaking the chain of association the meditator is deliberately building — a structure that makes the entire method, in effect, an extremely long and rigorous exercise in autobiographical memory before it is ever extended past the boundary of the present life.

The blunted-axe simile earns its place among this text's most quietly instructive images precisely because of what it does not valorize: not raw effort applied harder to the same failing tool, but the humility to repeatedly stop, walk away to the smithy, and re-equip — patience about method, not mere doggedness, is what the simile is actually recommending to a meditator stuck at the darkness of the death-moment boundary.

Naming the Knowledge, and the Three Kinds of Buddha-Field

Having described the whole backward-tracing procedure, the commentary pauses to draw a precise technical line: everything from the last sitting posture up to the moment of rebirth-linking is not yet called "recollection of past lives" at all — it is merely preliminary-work knowledge, since it takes only this present existence as its object. Only once the meditator has broken through rebirth-linking and taken as object the name-and-form of the moment of death in the previous life does the mind-door process fire in the standard four-or-five-javana pattern already familiar from the divine ear, its culminating fourth-jhāna consciousness alone earning the name "knowledge of recollecting past lives" proper. With that technical point settled, paragraph 404 opens the chapter's long digression into cosmology proper — defining a single "birth" precisely, then introducing the two great categories of world-cycle, the contracting aeon and the expanding aeon, and the three-fold classification of "Buddha-field" (the birth-field, the command-field, and the boundless resort-field) needed to understand exactly how much of the universe is actually destroyed and reformed at each cycle's turn.

Reading note — Ru-Yi

The careful technical distinction between "preliminary-work knowledge" and genuine "recollection of past lives" mirrors exactly the same structural move made throughout chapter twelve for the power of resolve: an elaborate, effortful preparatory phase is deliberately not credited with the power's actual name, which is reserved strictly for the single moment the fourth-jhāna consciousness itself arises — a recurring insistence throughout this treatise that a power's name tracks a precise causal event, not the surrounding effort that makes that event possible.

The three-fold Buddha-field classification quietly resolves what could otherwise look like a scale contradiction in Buddhist cosmology: the "boundless resort-field" affirms a Buddha's knowledge is genuinely unlimited, while the much smaller "command-field" (still an almost incomprehensibly vast hundred thousand million world-systems) is what actually gets destroyed and reformed at each cosmic cycle — an unlimited knowing capacity coexisting perfectly well with a finite, cyclically renewed physical domain.

The Long Drought and the Weeping Messenger-Gods

Paragraph 405 opens the treatise's full narrative account of a world destroyed by fire, beginning long before any actual burning starts. The first sign is agricultural rather than cosmic: an initially promising great rainfall, followed by a drought so complete that not a single further drop falls for what the Buddha himself describes, in the Sevens Discourse, as many hundreds of thousands of years, until every water-dependent being and deity has died and been reborn in the Brahmā-world purely by virtue of jhāna attained there. The commentary pauses to address an obvious objection — how can beings crippled by famine or naturally incapable of jhāna possibly be reborn among the Brahmās at all? — and answers that the necessary jhāna is itself attained only after death, in the deva-world, through kasiṇa practice on the very wind-element responsible for keeping the world's collapsing sun-scorched water aloft. Once the drought's warning has fully run its course, a class of dishevelled, weeping messenger-deities appears across the human world, urging universal loving-kindness and filial devotion as the only fitting response to the coming end, before the account turns to the successive appearance of a second, third, fourth, fifth, sixth, and finally seventh sun, each one drying up progressively larger bodies of water — rivers, then the seven great Himalayan lakes, then the ocean itself — until, with the seventh sun's appearance, every world-system alike is engulfed in flame reaching all the way up to the first-jhāna Brahmā-worlds.

The commentary's answer to its own objection — how can famine-crippled or jhāna-incapable beings reach the Brahmā-world when rebirth there strictly requires jhāna — is a small masterclass in this tradition's careful causal bookkeeping: rather than softening the strict rule, it simply relocates the necessary jhāna's attainment to an intermediate deva-world existence, reached first by ordinary merit or by ripening long-dormant kamma, so that the Brahmā-world's own entry requirement remains completely intact for absolutely everyone who arrives there.

The weeping Lokabyūhā deities are a genuinely unusual figure in this literature: messengers of catastrophe who appear not triumphant or threatening but visibly grieving, disheveled, and in tears — their own emotional state modeling exactly the response (saṃvega, a shaken sense of urgency) they are trying to provoke in their human audience, and their closing advice is strikingly ordinary and domestic (attend to your parents, honor your elders) rather than esoteric, grounding the coming end of a universe in the most basic, everyday ethical relationships.

The World Reforms: Savor-Earth, and the Loss of Self-Luminescence

Paragraph 406 turns from destruction to re-formation. After the long darkness, a new great cloud rises and begins to rain, at first in the finest possible drops, then in ever-thicker streams — compared to a bamboo stalk, a pestle, a palm-trunk — steadily filling every scorched world-system before finally vanishing, at which point wind, working both from below and from the sides, compresses the accumulated water the way it might round a single drop into a ball on a lotus leaf, letting it gradually subside until Brahmā-worlds and deva-worlds alike reappear at their proper heights within it. As the water recedes past the level of the former earth, a fragrant "savor-earth" (rasapathavī) forms on its surface, resembling the skin that forms on cooling rice-milk — and it is precisely this savor-earth that draws down the first inhabitants of the newly re-formed world: self-luminous, sky-flying beings from the Ābhassara Brahmā-world, whose lifespan or merit there has simply run out. Tasting the savor-earth out of craving, exactly as the Aggañña Sutta describes, these beings lose their own self-luminescence the very moment greed takes hold, plunging the new world into darkness and terror — until the sun and moon themselves are called into being, quite literally named "sun" and "moon" by the very beings whose fear and longing conjured them into appearing.

Reading note — Ru-Yi

The naming of the sun and moon is a small etiological myth folded directly into the cosmological account, and its logic is worth noticing: both names are acts of grateful naming by frightened beings responding to relief from fear, not descriptions of any inherent property of the object itself — the sun is "sun" because it dispelled fear and produced courage, exactly as a person might be named for a quality first noticed at their birth, making language itself, in this account, born out of an emotional response to darkness and its remedy.

The self-luminous Ābhassara beings' loss of their own light at the exact moment they act on craving draws a quietly severe moral equation: greed does not merely fail to obtain what it wants, it actively extinguishes a prior good already possessed — the very same beings who could light their own way through the sky lose that capacity the instant they grasp at something else instead.

Bubbling Porridge: How Mountains, Oceans, and Continents Take Shape

The narrative continues with a homely but exact simile for how the world's basic geography first takes form: exactly as bubbles rise unevenly across the surface of millet porridge as it cooks, some patches heaping up, others sinking, others staying level, so too the newly settled earth takes shape unevenly wherever it happens to sit — the heaped-up patches becoming mountains, the sunken patches becoming oceans, the level patches becoming continents. And this geographic settling, the text specifies, occurs on one single day above all others: the full-moon day of the month of Phagguṇa, never earlier and never later, so that Sineru, the surrounding world-ring, and the Himalayas all spring into being on that same appointed date, every single time a world reforms.

Reading note — Ru-Yi

The precision of naming an exact calendar date — the full-moon day of Phagguṇa — for a cosmogonic event of this scale is a distinctive feature of this cosmology's style throughout: even at the grandest possible scale of world-formation, the text insists on the same specificity of date, measurement, and named location it uses for the most ordinary monastic events, refusing to let scale become an excuse for vagueness.

The porridge-bubble simile is disarmingly domestic for the subject it illustrates: a phenomenon of literally cosmic proportions (why is the earth's surface uneven at all, forming mountains here and ocean basins there) is explained by pointing to something any listener could have watched happen in their own cooking pot that very morning, exemplifying this text's recurring commitment to grounding even its most extraordinary claims in observable, everyday experience.

Pride, Loss, and Hoarding: The Descent Into Ordinary Human Life

The narrative now traces a repeating pattern of pride and loss across four successive food-sources, each one coarser and less immediately edible than the last. The beings feeding on the fragrant savor-earth develop different degrees of complexion depending on how much they eat; those with better complexion come to despise the others, and precisely this contempt causes the savor-earth itself to vanish, replaced by a coarser "earth-crust." The same cycle of pride and loss repeats with the earth-crust, replaced by a spreading creeper, and again with the creeper, finally giving way to a wild, self-propagating rice needing no cultivation at all — husked, fragrant, and pure. Only with this rice does the narrative turn from diet to anatomy and society in quick succession: cooking pots that need no fire, the eventual appearance of distinguishing male and female organs, obsessive mutual staring, sexual desire, intercourse condemned by onlookers, houses built to hide the act, hoarding by a lazy individual that causes rice to stop regenerating on its own, boundary-marking of fields, theft, denial, and finally physical punishment — until society itself, exhausted by escalating conflict, invents government by mutual agreement, electing a "Great Elect" (mahāsammata) who is none other than the future Buddha in a former life, the very first bodhisatta among the newly formed khattiya caste from which the brahmin and other castes subsequently branch off.

Reading note — Ru-Yi

The recurring three-beat pattern — a food source appears, pride or greed toward it develops, that very pride causes it to vanish and be replaced by something coarser — repeats four full times (savor-earth, earth-crust, creeper, wild rice's own eventual husking) before the narrative even reaches sexuality or property, making unmistakably clear that this text locates the root cause of humanity's entire physical and social condition in a single recurring moral failure, not four separate unrelated declines.

The account of government's origin is remarkable for grounding political authority in nothing more exalted than a practical, mutually agreed solution to an escalating cycle of theft and violence — the Mahāsammata is elected, not divinely appointed, "sammata" itself meaning simply "agreed upon" — and the three etymologies given for his resulting titles (elected, lord of the fields, one who delights others fairly) each trace legitimate kingship back to service performed for the community's benefit, not to inherited status or conquest.

The Four Measureless Periods, and When Water and Wind Take Their Turn

With the full fire-cycle narrated end to end, the commentary now names its four constituent phases precisely: from the appearance of the aeon-destroying cloud until the flame finally goes out is one incalculable period, called "contraction"; from that point until the great cloud that fills the hundred thousand million world-systems with water is a second incalculable period, "the state of standing-contracted"; from that filling until the sun and moon appear is a third, "expansion"; and from the sun and moon's appearance until the next aeon-destroying cloud arises is a fourth, "the state of standing-expanded" — the four together making up one complete great aeon. Paragraphs 407 and 408 then narrate the two remaining modes of destruction with matching structure but distinct mechanics: an aeon destroyed by water begins the same way, but its destroying cloud rains caustic water that dissolves earth and mountains outright rather than burning them, reaching this time all the way up to the second-jhāna Brahmā-worlds before coming to rest at the Subhakiṇha realm; while an aeon destroyed by wind dispenses with cloud and rain altogether, a violent windstorm instead lifting dust, sand, gravel, boulders, and finally entire mountain ranges — Sineru and the world-ring themselves — into the sky to grind each other to powder, reaching furthest of all, up to the third-jhāna Brahmā-worlds, and coming to rest only at the Vehapphala realm.

Reading note — Ru-Yi

The parallel structure across all three destruction-modes — fire, water, wind — is nearly total, right down to the four-incalculable-period accounting scheme, but the one deliberate variable is exactly how high each mode reaches (first jhāna for fire, second for water, third for wind) and correspondingly how far the newly re-formed world must descend before life can begin again: a strict, escalating hierarchy in which wind is established as the single most far-reaching of the three destructive forces, precisely because delusion — its associated root cause, given in the next section — is treated as harder to eradicate than either greed or hatred.

The description of the wind-destruction's mechanics — mountains, and even Sineru itself, ground to powder by mutual collision in mid-air rather than dissolved or burned — is the most violently kinetic of the three accounts, matching a destructive force whose defining property (unlike fire's heat or water's dissolving) is sheer uncontrolled motion.

Seven Fires, Then a Flood: The Rhythm of Cosmic Destruction

Paragraph 409 asks the obvious question directly — why does the world get destroyed at all? — and answers it by tracing destruction back to the three unwholesome roots. When greed predominates, the world is destroyed by fire; when hatred predominates, by water (though some elders reverse this pairing); and when delusion predominates, by wind. But these three do not simply alternate freely: fire destroys the world seven times running, and only on the eighth occasion is it destroyed by water instead; this pattern of seven-fire-then-one-water repeats a further seven times, so that the world is burned forty-nine times and flooded seven times before the sixty-third destruction is reached — at which point, with the water-destruction's "turn" now used up, wind is finally given its opportunity, destroying a Subhakiṇha realm that has, uniquely, endured for a full sixty-four aeons without ever previously being reached by fire or water at all. The section closes by confirming that a monk recollecting past lives genuinely does perceive all of this directly, contracting and expanding aeons alike, by means of the formula given in the canonical text itself: "I was in such a place..."

"I Was There": Unpacking the Canonical Recollection Formula

The recollection-of-past-lives section closes with a careful, phrase-by-phrase commentary on the canonical formula itself — the very words a meditator's recollection actually takes the shape of. "I was there" can mean any of six things: a particular existence, birth-realm, destiny, station of consciousness, abode of beings, or class of beings once occupied. "Such was my name, such my clan" is explained straightforwardly as remembering one's own former name and family line, while "such was my appearance, such my food, such my experience of pleasure and pain, such the limit of my life" extend the same recollection to physical complexion, diet, feeling, and lifespan, should the meditator wish to recall them. The formula's final movement — passing away from there and arising here, and here again — the commentary explains as ordinary forward-moving review after the meditator has first climbed as far back as he wishes, distinguishing the initial "ascent" from the subsequent "descent" back toward the present, and closing with a precise grammatical point: name and clan are what is properly "designated," while appearance, food, feeling, and lifespan are merely the accompanying "modes" that flesh out that designation.

Reading note — Ru-Yi

The distinction drawn between "designation" (name and clan) and mere "modes" (complexion, diet, feeling, lifespan) is a small but precise piece of Abhidhamma-style linguistic analysis embedded within an otherwise narrative-driven formula: a being is properly identified, referred to, picked out by name and lineage, while everything else recollected about a past life — however vivid or significant — is classified as descriptive coloring around that core identification, never the identification itself.

The Divine Eye: Knowledge of Passing Away and Rebirth

The chapter's fourth direct knowledge, knowledge of the passing away and rebirth of beings, is named for its purpose rather than its mechanism — it exists for the sake of the divine eye's own knowledge, since it is by the divine eye that beings' death and rebirth become known. The formula's opening phrase, "he directs and inclines his mind," names the ordinary preliminary-work consciousness that any meditator brings before actually developing this power, and its etymology of "divine" follows the identical three-fold pattern already given for the divine ear: the eye resembles a deva's own faculty (unclouded by bile, phlegm, and blood, and so able to receive even a distant object), it arises from the very same knowledge-based power that produces every direct knowledge, and it is divine both because it is acquired through, and grounded in, the divine abidings. Two further qualities round out the etymology — its exceptional brightness, gained through mastery of light, and its exceptional reach, extending to forms hidden behind walls and other obstructions — both of which, the commentary notes, are best pursued through the general grammatical treatises rather than repeated here.

Reading note — Ru-Yi

The two wrong views the commentary pairs against seeing only half of the cuti-upapāta pattern are precisely matched to the two classical extremes this entire tradition defines itself against: seeing only death without any corresponding rebirth naturally suggests annihilationism (nothing continues), while seeing only rebirth without any corresponding prior death suggests something like spontaneous creation ex nihilo — and the divine eye's actual defining achievement, seeing both together in a single continuous act, is presented as literally identical with the "purification of view" that steers a middle course between them.

The phrase "the Buddha's own children" (buddhaputtā) for those who see this middle path directly is a warm, familial way of naming the noble disciples, and its placement here — immediately after defining the very knowledge that avoids both eternalism and annihilationism — quietly identifies correct view on this exact question as something like a family trait shared by everyone genuinely descended from the Buddha's own teaching.

Base and Excellent, Fair and Ugly: Reading a Being's Kamma at a Glance

The commentary now works through the formula's remaining technical terms one by one. "Passing away, being reborn" is carefully restricted to beings on the very verge of death or freshly arrived in a new existence — the actual instants of death and rebirth-linking themselves are too fleeting even for the divine eye to catch directly. The paired terms "base" and "excellent," "fair" and "ugly," "fortunate" and "unfortunate" are each traced to one of the three wholesome or unwholesome roots: delusion's residue produces low birth and looked-down-upon status, hatred's residue produces unattractive appearance, and greed's residue (or its absence) produces wealth or poverty. The final term, "faring according to their deeds," introduces a distinct but closely related knowledge — one that does not merely see a being's present circumstances but actually identifies which past action produced them, arising when the meditator, having first seen hell-beings undergoing suffering or heaven-beings enjoying bliss purely through the divine eye, then wonders what deed could account for what he sees, and a further knowledge arises spontaneously to answer that very question.

Reviling the Noble Ones: A Grave Deed, and How to Undo It

The commentary's word-list continues into the formula's catalogue of unwholesome conduct, singling out one item — reviling the noble ones — for extended, careful treatment because of its exceptional gravity. Whether spoken knowingly or unknowingly, reviling a noble one by denying their attainment or disparaging their virtue is classed as a grave deed, obstructive to both heaven and the path, comparable in weight to the five deeds of immediate retribution, yet crucially still remediable through confession — unlike those five, which are not. A story from an unnamed village illustrates the danger vividly: an elderly monk, suffering intestinal wind, drinks hot gruel while seated on a piece of firewood rather than waiting for it to cool, prompting a junior companion to reproach him with contempt as a greedy old man behaving shamefully — only for the elder, once safely back at the monastery, to quietly reveal that he is in fact a stream-enterer, at which point the junior monk immediately begs forgiveness. Generalizing from this cautionary tale, the commentary lays out a full graduated procedure for making amends after unknowingly reviling a noble one — apologizing in person according to relative seniority, sending a messenger if travel is impossible, appealing to a wise monk as intermediary if the offended party's whereabouts are unknown, and, if that person has already attained final Nibbāna, traveling all the way to the cremation ground itself to complete the apology there.

Wrong View, and the Four Names for a Bad Rebirth

The commentary flags wrong view as the gravest of all the listed unwholesome states, more blameworthy than any other single thing named in the entire canon, and quotes the Buddha's own unequivocal ranking of it as the worst of all faults. It then works through the formula's closing cluster of destination-terms with careful etymological precision: "apāya" (a woeful state) is so called because it is devoid of the auspicious "coming" that leads to heaven and liberation; "duggati" (a bad destiny) because it is a resort born of ill conduct; "vinipāta" (a fall) because beings collapse there against their will, limbs breaking as they fall; and "niraya" (hell proper) because it is devoid of the happiness ordinary beings imagine must exist even there. The commentary further clarifies that these four terms are not simply synonyms piled up for rhetorical effect, but pick out four genuinely distinct realms — the animal kingdom (a woeful state, yet not truly a "bad destiny" given the eminence of beings like great nāga kings), the ghost realm (both woeful and a bad destiny, yet not a "fall" in the full sense), the asura host (all three), and hell itself, in all its many varieties, uniquely earning all four names at once.

Placing the Buddha's own unqualified ranking of wrong view as the single most blameworthy thing in existence directly after the extended treatment of reviling the noble ones — itself already ranked as gravely as the five deeds of immediate retribution — establishes a clear hierarchy of fault running through this whole passage: wrong action toward a noble person is extremely serious, but wrong view about reality itself outranks even that, since wrong view is what generates wrong action in the first place, including reviling.

The four-way discrimination among woeful state, bad destiny, fall, and hell proper is a genuinely careful piece of doctrinal fine-tuning: rather than treating "bad rebirth" as one undifferentiated category, the commentary insists on real, textured distinctions even among unfortunate destinies — the animal kingdom's inclusion of "highly eminent beings such as great nāga kings" is a small but telling admission that even a woeful state admits of its own internal hierarchy and dignity.

Developing the Divine Eye: Choosing the Fire, White, or Light Kasiṇa

With the formula's every clause now explained, the commentary closes the word-commentary with a brief summarizing statement, then turns, in paragraph 412, to the actual method a beginner uses to develop the divine eye. Three kasiṇas alone are suitable as its foundation — fire, white, and light — and among these three, the light kasiṇa is explicitly ranked best. Crucially, the meditator must stop short of full absorption when developing any of these three for this particular purpose: only access concentration should be cultivated and then deliberately held there, never allowed to deepen into full jhāna, since full absorption would make the resulting light dependent on the basis-jhāna itself rather than on the flexible, freely-directed preliminary-work concentration the divine eye actually requires.

The Grass-Torch and the Sunrise: Expanding Light Into Sight

Having chosen a kasiṇa, the meditator must actually see physical forms within the space his expanded light now fills — and this initial phase, the commentary notes, is fragile: the moment the preliminary-work period lapses, the light itself vanishes, and the forms disappear along with it, requiring the meditator to re-enter the basis-jhāna and re-project the light all over again. Only gradual, repeated practice makes the light genuinely firm. The whole process is illustrated by an unforgettable extended simile: a man walking a road at night by the light of a bundle of burning grass, whose flame keeps guttering out, forcing him to rub it against the ground and relight it again and again, each relighting producing a slightly larger flame than the last — until, eventually, the sun itself rises, and the torch, now entirely unnecessary, is simply discarded, its bearer walking on through the day by sunlight alone. Every element of the simile maps directly onto the meditator's own experience: the guttering torch is the light of preliminary work; the relighting is repeated re-entry into the basis-jhāna; and the sunrise is the light of the divine eye itself, finally made permanently firm and no longer needing renewal at all.

When the Divine Eye Actually Arises — and Its Danger for the Unenlightened

The commentary now states precisely the moment the divine eye can be said to have genuinely arisen: when forms otherwise entirely outside the range of the fleshly eye — hidden inside the belly, resting on the heart-basis, beneath the earth's surface, behind walls, mountains, and ramparts, or in another world-system entirely — come within range of the meditator's knowledge-eye exactly as if they were being seen with ordinary vision. It is this actual capacity for seeing form, the commentary stresses, and not any of the preliminary mind-moments leading up to it, that constitutes the power itself. But the very same passage carries a sobering warning: for an unawakened worldling, this power is a genuine hazard rather than an unqualified achievement, since determining "let there be light" anywhere he wishes can suddenly illuminate terrifying sights — yakkhas, rakkhasas, and other fearsome forms — capable of provoking a fright so severe that his mind becomes deranged and his jhāna itself collapses entirely, making vigilant care in the exercise of this particular power absolutely essential. The section then closes with the same standard arising-process already given for the divine ear and past-life recollection, and its own formal colophon.

Miscellaneous Discussion: Seven Knowledges, Four Kinds of Object

With all five remaining direct knowledges now explained, the chapter's closing "miscellaneous discussion" opens with a pair of summarizing verses: the knower of the five aggregates, the Protector, spoke of five direct knowledges — the divine ear, mind-reading, past-life recollection, the divine eye, and the power of resolve already covered in chapter twelve — and having understood them, this miscellaneous discussion should also be known. The discussion's actual purpose is to prevent confusion about exactly what kind of object each of these knowledges takes, since the single power of the divine eye (renamed here "knowledge of passing away and rebirth") actually yields two further subsidiary knowledges — the knowledge of the future and the knowledge of faring according to deeds — bringing the total under discussion to seven direct knowledges in all. The Great Sage himself, the verses continue, spoke of four "triads of object" in the Abhidhamma matrices — objects that are limited, objects belonging to the path, objects that are past, and objects that are internal — and the discussion now sets out to show precisely how each of the seven knowledges' own activity maps onto this fourfold scheme, beginning with the power of resolve itself.

This closing discussion performs a genuinely different kind of analysis than everything preceding it in the chapter: rather than explaining how each power is developed or what it accomplishes, it retroactively classifies every power already explained according to a wholly separate Abhidhamma framework (the object-triads), demonstrating that these seven direct knowledges, however exotic their effects, remain fully governed by the same technical apparatus that describes ordinary consciousness — nothing about supernormal power exempts it from standard categorization.

Mahākassapa's relic-preservation determination and Assagutta's daily curd-essence both function as worked examples of "future object" — a category easy to state abstractly but genuinely difficult to picture concretely, and the choice of two such vivid, well-attested stories to anchor it shows the commentary's consistent habit of pairing every abstract classification with a memorable narrative instance.

The Divine Ear's Four Objects, and the Sixteen-Fold Mind Reconsidered

The object-classification survey continues with the divine ear, whose range turns out to be considerably narrower than the power of resolve's: since sound itself is always a "limited" object, and since sound can only ever be heard as it is actually occurring, the divine ear-knowledge is confined to just four of the seven possible categories — limited, present, internal (when hearing a sound within one's own body), and external (when hearing another's). The commentary then turns to mind-reading, whose range is considerably wider precisely because a mind, unlike a sound, can belong to any of the three planes of existence or even be supramundane — sense-sphere minds are limited objects, fine-material and immaterial minds are exalted objects, and path-and-fruit minds are measureless objects, giving this single knowledge access to fully eight of the framework's object-categories at once.

The strict one-directional rule — a higher stage of attainment can read a lower mind, but never the reverse — quietly encodes a whole theory of spiritual hierarchy into a single technical remark about mind-reading: understanding is treated as something that scales with attainment itself, so that an Arahant's comprehensive access to every mind is not merely a stronger version of an ordinary worldling's total blindness to a stream-enterer's mind, but a difference in kind tracking the actual structure of the path.

The divine ear's comparatively narrow four-object range, set right next to mind-reading's eight, is a useful corrective to any assumption that "more exotic-sounding power" automatically means "wider technical scope" — sound's own inherent nature (always limited, always occurring only in the present) constrains what can be known about it no matter how refined the perceiving faculty becomes, while a mind's much greater possible variety (spanning three planes plus the supramundane) simply gives mind-reading more genuine categories to range across.

Three Kinds of "Present": A Flower-Bunch Thrown Into the Air

Before mind-reading's object-classification can be finished, the commentary must settle exactly what "present" means for a mind-moment that, by nature, lasts only an instant — and it distinguishes three technical grades: momentary-present (a state actually undergoing arising, persisting, and dissolving right now), continuity-present (anything falling within one or two or three sequences of mental process), and duration-present (anything bounded within a single existence, the sense used in the Bhaddekaratta Sutta). Some teachers had argued that mind-reading takes even the razor-thin momentary-present as its object, illustrated by the striking image of a handful of flowers thrown into the air, where each flower's stem inevitably meets another flower's stem at the very same instant they scatter — implying that the meditator's own cognizing mind and the target's cognized mind must likewise coincide at a single identical moment. But the commentaries firmly reject this account as technically impossible, since the mind that adverts and the mind that actually knows cannot occupy the same location in the mental process, and the text instead settles on continuity-present and duration-present as the only genuinely correct answer — illustrated by two further similes from a differing group of reciter-elders: a footprint left in water that has not yet faded, warmth in the body not yet cooled after a journey, and the still-flickering afterimage in the eyes when first stepping from sunlight into a darkened room.

The choice to preserve two rival monastic reciting-traditions' differing accounts of "continuity-present" side by side — the Majjhima reciters' three ordinary-experience examples (adjusting to light, adjusting to sound) against the Saṃyutta reciters' more precise material/immaterial split — continues this treatise's consistent practice of showing its work: rather than silently synthesizing a single official position, it lets the reader see exactly how two respected lineages independently converged on compatible, if differently framed, answers.

Past-Life Recollection's Eight Objects, the Divine Eye's Four

The survey turns next to recollection of past lives, whose object-range matches mind-reading's eight categories almost exactly but for one telling substitution: in place of an external object, past-life recollection instead admits an "inexpressible" object, needed to accommodate name, clan, and similar designations that are conventionally real but not, strictly speaking, any of the standard ultimate categories. The commentary pauses here to draw a further fine distinction against mind-reading and knowledge of faring according to deeds, both of which likewise touch the past: mind-reading is confined to another's mind occurring within the preceding seven days, and knowledge of faring according to deeds touches only past volition, whereas past-life recollection alone ranges freely across the aggregates of any past existence whatsoever, with no comparable restriction — a scope the commentary backs with a direct citation from the Paṭṭhāna itself. The divine eye, treated next, returns to a narrower four-object scope closely paralleling the divine ear's own: visible form is always a limited object, always present, and either internal or external depending on whose body is being observed.

partaking of the range of omniscient knowledge — sabbaññu (all-knowing) + ñāṇa (knowledge) + gatika (having the range or course of); the striking phrase used to describe past-life recollection's exceptionally unrestricted scope across past aggregates, likened to the Buddha's own omniscience in this one narrow respect.

niruttipaṭisambhidā

analytical knowledge of language, etymological discernment — nirutti (etymology, language) + paṭisambhidā (analytical knowledge); cited here from elsewhere in the canon to support the claim that a mere linguistic term, as opposed to its conventional sense, is always a limited object.

Foreknowledge's Eight Objects, and the Deed-Knowledge's Five

The survey closes with its final two knowledges. Knowledge of the future mirrors past-life recollection's eight-category range almost exactly, substituting "future" for "past" throughout — sense-sphere rebirths as limited objects, fine-material and immaterial rebirths as exalted objects, future path-and-fruit as measureless, future path alone as path-related — while carrying over the very same restriction already noted for mind-reading: it too is confined to what will occur within a bounded seven-day window, and to consciousness alone rather than the full range of past or future aggregates that past-life recollection alone can access. Knowledge of faring according to deeds, treated last, has the narrowest range of all the direct knowledges discussed in this survey, occurring by way of just five objects — limited, exalted, past, internal, and external — corresponding neatly to actions belonging to the sense sphere, actions belonging to the fine-material and immaterial spheres, the strictly past nature of any deed already performed, and one's own deeds as against another's. With this final classification complete, the chapter's technical work is finished, and Buddhaghosa closes with the traditional colophon marking its completion.

Ru-Yi Translation

The knowledge of the future occurs by way of eight objects: limited, exalted, measureless, path-related, future, internal, external, and inexpressible. How? At the time of knowing, "this one will be reborn, in the future, in the sense sphere," it is a limited object. At the time of knowing, "he will be reborn in the fine-material or immaterial sphere," it is an exalted object. At the time of knowing, "he will develop the path, he will realize the fruit," it is a measureless object. At the time of knowing simply, "he will develop the path," it is a path-object. This knowledge, however, is by rule always a future object as well.

Herein, although the knowledge of penetrating minds also takes a future object, still, its object is only a mind that is future within the following seven days — for it does not know any other aggregate, or anything bound up with the aggregates. For the knowledge of the future, in the manner already stated for the knowledge of recollecting past lives, there is nothing in the future that is not its object. At the time of knowing, "I shall be reborn in such a place," it is an internal object. At the time of knowing, "such-and-such a person will be reborn in such a place," it is an external object. And by the method of, "in the future, the Blessed One Metteyya will arise; a brahmin named Subrahmā will be his father; a brahmin woman named Brahmavatī will be his mother," and so on, at the time of knowing name and clan, it is, in exactly the manner already stated for the knowledge of recollecting past lives, an inexpressible object. Thus should the occurrence of the knowledge of the future among its eight objects be understood.

The knowledge of faring according to deeds occurs by way of five objects: limited, exalted, past, internal, and external. How? At the time of knowing a sense-sphere deed, it is a limited object. At the time of knowing a fine-material or immaterial-sphere deed, it is an exalted object. Since it knows only what is past, it is a past object. At the time of knowing one's own deed, it is an internal object. At the time of knowing another's deed, it is an external object. Thus should the occurrence of the knowledge of faring according to deeds among its five objects be understood. And where it is said here to be both an internal object and an external object, this means that, at times knowing internally and at times knowing externally, it does indeed also become an internal-and-external object.

Reading note — Ru-Yi

The near-perfect mirror-symmetry between the knowledge of the future and the knowledge of recollecting past lives — same eight object-categories, same seven-day restriction shared with mind-reading, same inexpressible category needed for names not yet borne by anyone — suggests these two knowledges are conceived as genuinely twin faculties, one looking backward and one forward along the identical axis of time, sharing every structural feature except the direction of their gaze.

Ending this long, technically demanding chapter on the narrowest-ranging knowledge of all seven — faring according to deeds, confined to just five object-categories, entirely past-bound and lacking even the seven-day forward window granted to mind-reading and future-knowledge — is a fitting close: after the chapter's vast excursions into world-destroying fire and multi-aeon cosmology, the final knowledge discussed brings the reader firmly back to the plainest and most immediately practical of all these powers, simply seeing which specific past deed produced which specific present result.

Abhiññāniddesa Complete

With the knowledge of faring according to deeds classified last, the thirteenth chapter of the Visuddhimagga comes to its close, and with it, the treatise's full two-chapter treatment of the direct knowledges available to one who has mastered concentration. Beginning where chapter twelve left off — the divine ear developed by systematic gross-to-subtle sound discrimination, mind-reading developed through the literal color of another's heart-blood, and recollection of past lives developed by the long, disciplined backward journey through one's own present life and beyond — the chapter then opened into its longest and most ambitious excursion: a complete narrative account of the universe's own destruction and re-formation by fire, water, and wind, tracing every stage from the first warning drought through the seven suns, the settling of the primordial waters, the loss of self-luminescence, the naming of the sun and moon, and the slow, cumulative descent from mind-made radiant beings to ordinary human society, property, kingship, and caste. It closed by completing the divine eye's own development, its genuine dangers for the unenlightened, and a rigorous final survey mapping all seven direct knowledges onto the Abhidhamma's own object-classification scheme.

The fourteenth chapter, the Khandhaniddesa ("Discussion of the Aggregates"), verified from the Visuddhimagga's own table of contents, now opens the treatise's second great division in earnest — paññā, wisdom — turning from the fruits of mastered concentration to the direct analytical understanding of mind and matter itself, beginning with the five aggregates that constitute the entirety of what a being actually is.
