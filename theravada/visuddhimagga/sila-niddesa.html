What Is Virtue?

This page carries Ru-Yi's translation of the Visuddhimagga's chapter 1, Sīlaniddesa ("The Description of Virtue") — the seven-question matrix that structures the whole chapter, the complete answers to its first four questions, the fifth question's onefold through fourfold classification schemes, and all four "purifications of virtue" fully unpacked word by word: restraint by the Pātimokkha, restraint of the faculties, purification of livelihood, and virtue connected with the requisites. The rest is translated in the sessions ahead and will be added to this same page.

Translation Method

The Pāli source is the Chaṭṭha Saṅgāyana (Sixth Council) romanized edition maintained by the Vipassana Research Institute (VRI, tipitaka.org), cross-checked against the Pali Text Society's Rhys Davids edition (1920–21) as digitized on GRETIL — used here only to verify readings, not copied, since that particular digital file carries its own CC BY-SA licence from PTS and the Dhammakaya Foundation. The VRI text itself is used under its stated terms: freely available for non-commercial use, with attribution to the Vipassana Research Institute.

The English translation is Ru-Yi's own, made directly from the Pāli. No existing English translation of the Visuddhimagga — including Bhikkhu Ñāṇamoli's copyrighted The Path of Purification — was consulted, paraphrased, or used as a structural guide for this wording. Pāli technical terms that are standard scholarly convention (sīla, saṅgāhaka, sīlaniddesa, and the like) are rendered or left untranslated following ordinary usage; this does not draw on any single translator's specific choices.

Reading notes are grounded only in the passage's own internal citations — to the Paṭisambhidāmagga, the Vibhaṅga, the Dīgha Nikāya, the Majjhima Nikāya, the Aṅguttara Nikāya, and the Suttanipāta — and in the general, uncontested convention of the Pāli commentarial (aṭṭhakathā) genre. They do not draw on Ñāṇamoli's or any other modern scholar's introduction, footnotes, or interpretive framing. Each translated passage also carries a short vocabulary list for readers learning Pāli alongside the text.

The Seven Questions

The chapter opens by admitting that virtue, concentration, and understanding — however comprehensive as a threefold outline — have so far been announced only in the barest summary. It then poses the seven questions that the rest of the chapter answers in turn, taking up virtue first.

Pali Source

Evaṃ anekaguṇasaṅgāhakena sīlasamādhipaññāmukhena desitopi panesa visuddhimaggo atisaṅkhepadesitoyeva hoti. Tasmā nālaṃ sabbesaṃ upakārāyāti vitthāramassa dassetuṃ sīlaṃ tāva ārabbha idaṃ pañhākammaṃ hoti.

Kiṃ sīlaṃ, kenaṭṭhena sīlaṃ, kānassa lakkhaṇarasapaccupaṭṭhānapadaṭṭhānāni, kimānisaṃsaṃ sīlaṃ, katividhaṃ cetaṃ sīlaṃ, ko cassa saṃkileso, kiṃ vodānanti.

Ru-Yi Translation

Thus, although this Path of Purification has been taught by way of virtue, concentration, and understanding — a division that gathers in many good qualities — it has still been taught only in the barest outline. Therefore, thinking, "this will not suffice to help everyone," in order to show its elaboration, this question-and-answer treatment now begins, taking up virtue first.

What is virtue? In what sense is it virtue? What are its characteristic, function, manifestation, and proximate cause? What are the benefits of virtue? How many kinds does this virtue have? What is its defilement? And what is its cleansing?

Key vocabulary — for Pāli learners
sīlant.

virtue, moral conduct — the whole chapter's subject. Same word can mean "nature, habit" more generally; "virtue" is what one's habitual conduct has become.

lakkhaṇa · rasa · paccupaṭṭhāna · padaṭṭhāna

"characteristic, function, manifestation, proximate cause" — a fixed quartet of questions Abhidhamma asks about almost every phenomenon it defines. Worth learning as a set now; it recurs constantly.

ānisaṃsam.

benefit, advantage, reward.

saṅkilesa / vodāna

a paired opposite you'll see often: "defilement" vs. "cleansing, purification."

-ādi

not a standalone word but a very common suffix meaning "and so on, etc." — attached to the last item of an implied list (as in pāṇātipātādīhi, "from killing living beings, etc.").

What Is Virtue? — Volition and the Mental Factor

The chapter answers only the first question here, citing the Paṭisambhidāmagga's own fourfold analysis of virtue, then glossing each of its first two terms in turn.

Pali Source

Tatridaṃ vissajjanaṃ. Kiṃ sīlanti pāṇātipātādīhi vā viramantassa vattapaṭipattiṃ vā pūrentassa cetanādayo dhammā. Vuttañhetaṃ paṭisambhidāyaṃ "kiṃ sīlanti cetanā sīlaṃ, cetasikaṃ sīlaṃ, saṃvaro sīlaṃ, avītikkamo sīla"nti (Paṭis I.39).

Tattha cetanā sīlaṃ nāma pāṇātipātādīhi vā viramantassa vattapaṭipattiṃ vā pūrentassa cetanā. Cetasikaṃ sīlaṃ nāma pāṇātipātādīhi viramantassa virati. Apica cetanā sīlaṃ nāma pāṇātipātādīni pajahantassa satta kammapathacetanā. Cetasikaṃ sīlaṃ nāma "abhijjhaṃ pahāya vigatābhijjhena cetasā viharatī"ti (DN I.217) ādinā nayena vuttā anabhijjhābyāpādasammādiṭṭhidhammā.

Ru-Yi Translation

Here now is the answer. As to "what is virtue" — it is the states beginning with volition, in one who abstains from killing living beings and so on, or who fulfills the observance of duties. For this was said in the Paṭisambhidā: "What is virtue? Volition is virtue, the mental factor is virtue, restraint is virtue, non-transgression is virtue" (Paṭis I.39).

Herein, "volition is virtue" means the volition in one who abstains from killing living beings and so on, or who fulfills the observance of duties. "The mental factor is virtue" means the abstinence in one who abstains from killing living beings and so on. And further, "volition is virtue" also means the seven volitions of the courses of action, in one who abandons killing living beings and the rest. "The mental factor is virtue" also means the states of non-covetousness, non-ill-will, and right view, spoken of by the method beginning, "having abandoned covetousness, he dwells with a mind free from covetousness" (DN I.217).

Key vocabulary — for Pāli learners
cetanāf.

volition, intention — from ceteti, "to intend." One of Abhidhamma's most load-bearing terms: it is cetanā that later theory treats as what actually makes an act count as kamma.

cetasikant./adj.

"belonging to citta (mind)" — a mental factor that arises together with a moment of consciousness.

kammapatham.

"course of action" — the classic set of bodily and verbal deeds (here, the seven unwholesome ones being abandoned).

avītikkamam.

non-transgression — a- (not) + vīti-kkama ("stepping across/beyond"). The fourth of the chapter's fourfold definition of virtue.

What Is Virtue? — The Fivefold Restraint, and Non-transgression

The chapter turns to the third term, restraint, dividing it into five kinds and supporting each with a separate canonical citation, before closing with the fourth term and a summary line.

Pali Source

Saṃvaro sīlanti ettha pañcavidhena saṃvaro veditabbo — pātimokkhasaṃvaro, satisaṃvaro, ñāṇasaṃvaro, khantisaṃvaro, vīriyasaṃvaroti. Tattha "iminā pātimokkhasaṃvarena upeto hoti samupeto"ti (Vibh. 511) ayaṃ pātimokkhasaṃvaro. "Rakkhati cakkhundriyaṃ, cakkhundriye saṃvaraṃ āpajjatī"ti (DN I.213) ayaṃ satisaṃvaro.

Yāni sotāni lokasmiṃ, (ajitāti bhagavā;)
sati tesaṃ nivāraṇaṃ;
sotānaṃ saṃvaraṃ brūmi, paññāyete pidhiyyareti. (Sn 1041)

Ayaṃ ñāṇasaṃvaro. Paccayapaṭisevanampi ettheva samodhānaṃ gacchati. Yo panāyaṃ khamo hoti sītassa uṇhassātiādinā (MN I.24; AN 6.58) nayena āgato, ayaṃ khantisaṃvaro nāma. Yo cāyaṃ uppannaṃ kāmavitakkaṃ nādhivāsetītiādinā (MN I.26; AN 6.58) nayena āgato, ayaṃ vīriyasaṃvaro nāma. Ājīvapārisuddhipi ettheva samodhānaṃ gacchati.

Iti ayaṃ pañcavidhopi saṃvaro, yā ca pāpabhīrukānaṃ kulaputtānaṃ sampattavatthuto virati, sabbampetaṃ saṃvarasīlanti veditabbaṃ. Avītikkamo sīlanti samādinnasīlassa kāyikavācasiko anatikkamo. Idaṃ tāva kiṃ sīlanti pañhassa vissajjanaṃ.

Ru-Yi Translation

As to "restraint is virtue" — here, restraint should be understood as fivefold: restraint by the Pātimokkha, restraint by mindfulness, restraint by knowledge, restraint by patience, and restraint by energy. Herein, "he is possessed, fully possessed, of this restraint by the Pātimokkha" (Vibh. 511) — this is restraint by the Pātimokkha. "He guards the eye faculty; he enters upon restraint of the eye faculty" (DN I.213) — this is restraint by mindfulness.

"Whatever streams there are in the world" — Ajita, said the Blessed One — "mindfulness is their restraining; the restraint of the streams I declare; by understanding these are obstructed." (Sn 1041)

This is restraint by knowledge. The proper use of the requisites is included here as well. And this — one who is patient, who has come by the method beginning, "enduring cold, enduring heat..." (MN I.24; AN 6.58) — this is called restraint by patience. And this — one who does not tolerate an arisen sensual thought, who has come by the same method (MN I.26; AN 6.58) — this is called restraint by energy. Purity of livelihood is included here as well.

Thus this fivefold restraint, together with whatever abstinence there is — on the part of clansmen who fear wrongdoing — from an occasion for transgression that has presented itself: all this should be understood as restraint-virtue. As to "non-transgression is virtue" — it is the bodily and verbal non-transgression on the part of one who has undertaken the precepts of virtue. This, for now, is the answer to the question "what is virtue."

Reading note — Ru-Yi

Notice the method, not just the content: rather than argue for his own definition of "virtue," Buddhaghosa builds it almost entirely out of citations. The fourfold analysis itself — volition, mental factor, restraint, non-transgression — is quoted verbatim from the Paṭisambhidāmagga, a source the chapter expects its reader to already recognize as settled canonical authority. Each of the five kinds of restraint is then anchored to a separate scriptural passage: the Vibhaṅga for restraint by the code of monastic rules, the Dīgha Nikāya for restraint by mindfulness at the sense doors, the Suttanipāta's verse to the wanderer Ajita for restraint by knowledge, and stock formulas shared by the Majjhima and Aṅguttara Nikāyas for restraint by patience and by energy. None of these five sources originally presents itself as part of a fivefold scheme — each simply describes one kind of restraint on its own. The fivefold saṃvara taught here is Buddhaghosa's synthesis of five independent canonical passages under a single interpretive heading, which is the recurring method of the Pāli commentarial genre generally: authority accumulates through the density and precision of citation, not through claims to originality.

Key vocabulary — for Pāli learners
saṃvaram.

restraint, control — saṃ- (together/thoroughly) + vṛ ("to cover, hold back"). This whole section is Buddhaghosa dividing this one word into five kinds.

pātimokkhant.

the code of monastic training rules recited fortnightly by the Saṅgha.

khantif.

patience, forbearance, endurance.

vīriyant.

energy, effort, exertion — one of the five spiritual faculties (indriya) elsewhere in the text.

ājīvam.

livelihood — as in sammā-ājīva, "right livelihood," the fifth factor of the Noble Eightfold Path.

In What Sense Is It Virtue?

The chapter turns to the second question, tracing "sīla" to a verbal root meaning "to compose, to conduct oneself," and noting that grammarians recognize two related senses of that root.

Pali Source

Avasesesu kenaṭṭhena sīlanti sīlanaṭṭhena sīlaṃ. Kimidaṃ sīlanaṃ nāma. Samādhānaṃ vā, kāyakammādīnaṃ susīlyavasena avippakiṇṇatāti attho. Upadhāraṇaṃ vā, kusalānaṃ dhammānaṃ patiṭṭhānavasena ādhārabhāvoti attho. Etadeva hettha atthadvayaṃ saddalakkhaṇavidū anujānanti. Aññe pana siraṭṭho sīlattho, sītalaṭṭho sīlatthoti evamādināpi nayenettha atthaṃ vaṇṇayanti.

Ru-Yi Translation

As to "in what sense is it virtue" — among the remaining questions — it is virtue in the sense of "composing" (sīlana). What, then, is this "composing"? Either it is a coordinating, meaning the non-scatteredness of bodily action and the rest, by way of their being well-disciplined; or it is a supporting, meaning the state of being a foundation, by way of grounding wholesome states. This twofold meaning alone is what those skilled in grammar accept here. Others, however, explain the meaning by yet other methods — such as, the meaning of sīla is "head" (foremost), or the meaning of sīla is "coolness."

Key vocabulary — for Pāli learners
sīlanant.

"composing, conducting oneself" — the abstract noun the chapter derives sīla from. A classic example of Pāli commentarial etymology: explaining a word by tracing it to a verbal root, not by citing a dictionary.

samādhānant.

coordinating, composing together. Note for learners: this looks like samādhi (concentration) but is a different derivative — a common false-friend trap in Pāli compounds.

patiṭṭhā / patiṭṭhāna

foundation, footing, support — the same root behind sīle patiṭṭhāya, "established in virtue," from the chapter's opening verse.

Characteristic, Function, Manifestation, Proximate Cause

The chapter now delivers on the third question from its opening matrix, taking each of the four terms in turn — each introduced by a verse, then unpacked in prose.

Pali Source

Idāni kānassa lakkhaṇarasapaccupaṭṭhānapadaṭṭhānānīti ettha –

Sīlanaṃ lakkhaṇaṃ tassa, bhinnassāpi anekadhā;
sanidassanattaṃ rūpassa, yathā bhinnassanekadhā.

Yathā hi nīlapītādibhedena anekadhā bhinnassāpi rūpāyatanassa sanidassanattaṃ lakkhaṇaṃ, nīlādibhedena bhinnassāpi sanidassanabhāvānatikkamanato. Tathā sīlassa cetanādibhedena anekadhā bhinnassāpi yadetaṃ kāyakammādīnaṃ samādhānavasena kusalānañca dhammānaṃ patiṭṭhānavasena vuttaṃ sīlanaṃ, tadeva lakkhaṇaṃ, cetanādibhedena bhinnassāpi samādhānapatiṭṭhānabhāvānatikkamanato. Evaṃ lakkhaṇassa panassa –

Dussīlyaviddhaṃsanatā, anavajjaguṇo tathā;
kiccasampattiatthena, raso nāma pavuccati.

Tasmā idaṃ sīlaṃ nāma kiccaṭṭhena rasena dussīlyaviddhaṃsanarasaṃ, sampattiatthena rasena anavajjarasanti veditabbaṃ. Lakkhaṇādīsu hi kiccameva sampatti vā rasoti vuccati.

Soceyyapaccupaṭṭhānaṃ, tayidaṃ tassa viññuhi;
ottappañca hirī ceva, padaṭṭhānanti vaṇṇitaṃ.

Tayidaṃ sīlaṃ kāyasoceyyaṃ vacīsoceyyaṃ manosoceyyanti (AN 3.121) evaṃ vuttasoceyyapaccupaṭṭhānaṃ, soceyyabhāvena paccupaṭṭhāti gahaṇabhāvaṃ gacchati. Hirottappañca panassa viññūhi padaṭṭhānanti vaṇṇitaṃ, āsannakāraṇanti attho. Hirottappe hi sati sīlaṃ uppajjati ceva tiṭṭhati ca. Asati neva uppajjati, na tiṭṭhatīti. Evaṃ sīlassa lakkhaṇarasapaccupaṭṭhānapadaṭṭhānāni veditabbāni.

Ru-Yi Translation

Now, as to "what are its characteristic, function, manifestation, and proximate cause" —

Composing is its characteristic, even though divided in many ways — just as visibility is the characteristic of visible form, though that too is divided in many ways.

For just as visibility is the characteristic of the visible-form base, even though that base is divided in many ways by the distinction of blue, yellow, and the rest — because, however divided, it never goes beyond the state of being visible — so too with virtue: even though divided in many ways by the distinction of volition and the rest, that very "composing," spoken of as the coordinating of bodily action and the rest and as the grounding of wholesome states, is its characteristic — because, however divided, it never goes beyond the state of coordinating and grounding. Its characteristic having been explained thus —

The destruction of bad conduct, and likewise its flawless quality, in the sense of function and of achievement — this is called its function.

Therefore this virtue should be understood: in the sense of function, its function is the destruction of bad conduct; in the sense of achievement, its function is flawlessness. For among characteristic and the rest, it is precisely the function, or the achievement, that is called the "function."

Purity is declared by the wise to be its manifestation; and shame and moral dread are said to be its proximate cause.

This virtue has as its manifestation the purity spoken of thus: "bodily purity, verbal purity, mental purity" (AN 3.121) — it comes to be apprehended in the state of purity, as its manifestation. And shame and moral dread are declared by the wise to be its proximate cause, meaning its immediate cause. For when shame and moral dread are present, virtue arises and endures; when they are absent, it neither arises nor endures. Thus should the characteristic, function, manifestation, and proximate cause of virtue be understood.

Reading note — Ru-Yi

This is the chapter making good on a promise from its own opening matrix, and it does so with the standard Abhidhamma move of anchoring an abstract "characteristic" to a concrete, familiar case: visible form (rūpāyatana) stays visible-form no matter how many colors it divides into, so a characteristic can be single even while its bearer multiplies — the same reasoning is then applied, unchanged, to virtue. Watch for this exact pattern of "characteristic, function, manifestation, proximate cause," each introduced by verse and unpacked in prose, to recur for essentially every phenomenon the rest of this text goes on to define.

Naming shame (hirī) and moral dread (ottappa) as virtue's proximate cause is not incidental. Elsewhere in the canon this pair is called the "two guardians of the world" (lokapāladhamma) — the passage is quietly asserting that virtue does not sustain itself; it depends on these two more basic qualities to arise and to endure at all, exactly as the closing line states outright.

Key vocabulary — for Pāli learners
rūpāyatanant.

"the visible-form base" — one of the twelve āyatanas (sense bases) in Abhidhamma; used here purely as a familiar comparison case, not as this passage's real subject.

hirī / ottappa

a fixed pair: moral shame (recoiling from wrongdoing out of self-respect) and moral dread (fearing wrongdoing's consequences). Almost always cited together, never one without the other.

soceyyant.

purity, cleanliness — from suci, "clean, pure."

The Benefits of Virtue

The fourth question turns from analysis to inspiration: a canonical prose passage on the five benefits a virtuous householder enjoys, followed by ten verses in praise of virtue unlike anything so far in this chapter.

Pali Source

Kimānisaṃsaṃ sīlanti avippaṭisārādianekaguṇapaṭilābhānisaṃsaṃ. Vuttañhetaṃ — "avippaṭisāratthāni kho, ānanda, kusalāni sīlāni avippaṭisārānisaṃsānī"ti (AN 11.1).

Aparampi vuttaṃ "pañcime gahapatayo ānisaṃsā sīlavato sīlasampadāya. Katame pañca? Idha gahapatayo sīlavā sīlasampanno appamādādhikaraṇaṃ mahantaṃ bhogakkhandhaṃ adhigacchati, ayaṃ paṭhamo ānisaṃso sīlavato sīlasampadāya. Puna caparaṃ gahapatayo sīlavato sīlasampannassa kalyāṇo kittisaddo abbhuggacchati, ayaṃ dutiyo ānisaṃso sīlavato sīlasampadāya. Puna caparaṃ gahapatayo sīlavā sīlasampanno yaññadeva parisaṃ upasaṅkamati yadi khattiyaparisaṃ yadi brāhmaṇaparisaṃ yadi gahapatiparisaṃ yadi samaṇaparisaṃ, visārado upasaṅkamati amaṅkubhūto, ayaṃ tatiyo ānisaṃso sīlavato sīlasampadāya. Puna caparaṃ gahapatayo sīlavā sīlasampanno asammūḷho kālaṃ karoti, ayaṃ catuttho ānisaṃso sīlavato sīlasampadāya. Puna caparaṃ gahapatayo sīlavā sīlasampanno kāyassa bhedā paraṃ maraṇā sugatiṃ saggaṃ lokaṃ upapajjati, ayaṃ pañcamo ānisaṃso sīlavato sīlasampadāyā"ti (DN II.150; AN 5.213; Mahāvagga 285).

Aparepi "ākaṅkheyya ce, bhikkhave, bhikkhu sabrahmacārīnaṃ piyo ca assaṃ manāpo ca garu ca bhāvanīyo cāti, sīlesvevassa paripūrakārī"tiādinā (MN I.65) nayena piyamanāpatādayo āsavakkhayapariyosānā anekā sīlānisaṃsā vuttā. Evaṃ avippaṭisārādianekaguṇānisaṃsaṃ sīlaṃ. Apica —

Sāsane kulaputtānaṃ, patiṭṭhā natthi yaṃ vinā;
ānisaṃsaparicchedaṃ, tassa sīlassa ko vade.

Na gaṅgā yamunā cāpi, sarabhū vā sarasvatī;
ninnagā vāciravatī, mahī vāpi mahānadī.
Sakkuṇanti visodhetuṃ, taṃ malaṃ idha pāṇinaṃ;
visodhayati sattānaṃ, yaṃ ve sīlajalaṃ malaṃ.

Na taṃ sajaladā vātā, na cāpi haricandanaṃ;
neva hārā na maṇayo, na candakiraṇaṅkurā.
Samayantīdha sattānaṃ, pariḷāhaṃ surakkhitaṃ;
yaṃ sameti idaṃ ariyaṃ, sīlaṃ accantasītalaṃ.

Sīlagandhasamo gandho, kuto nāma bhavissati;
yo samaṃ anuvāte ca, paṭivāte ca vāyati.

Saggārohaṇasopānaṃ, aññaṃ sīlasamaṃ kuto;
dvāraṃ vā pana nibbāna-nagarassa pavesane.

Sobhantevaṃ na rājāno, muttāmaṇivibhūsitā;
yathā sobhanti yatino, sīlabhūsanabhūsitā.

Attānuvādādibhayaṃ, viddhaṃsayati sabbaso;
janeti kittihāsañca, sīlaṃ sīlavataṃ sadā.

Guṇānaṃ mūlabhūtassa, dosānaṃ balaghātino;
iti sīlassa viññeyyaṃ, ānisaṃsakathāmukhanti.

Ru-Yi Translation

As to "what are the benefits of virtue" — its benefit is the attainment of many good qualities, beginning with freedom from remorse. For this was said: "Wholesome virtues, Ānanda, have freedom from remorse as their purpose, freedom from remorse as their benefit" (AN 11.1).

It was also further said: "Householders, there are these five benefits for one who is virtuous, through the accomplishment of virtue. What five? Here, householders, one who is virtuous, accomplished in virtue, gains a great mass of wealth through diligence — this is the first benefit for one who is virtuous, through the accomplishment of virtue. Further, a good reputation spreads about one who is virtuous, accomplished in virtue — this is the second benefit. Further, one who is virtuous, accomplished in virtue, approaches any assembly whatsoever — whether of nobles, of brahmins, of householders, or of ascetics — confidently and without embarrassment — this is the third benefit. Further, one who is virtuous, accomplished in virtue, dies unconfused — this is the fourth benefit. Further, one who is virtuous, accomplished in virtue, on the breakup of the body, after death, is reborn in a good destination, in a heavenly world — this is the fifth benefit for one who is virtuous, through the accomplishment of virtue" (DN II.150; AN 5.213; Mahāvagga 285).

By the method beginning, "If, monks, a monk should wish: 'May I be dear and pleasing to my companions in the holy life, respected and esteemed by them' — let him then be one who fulfills the precepts of virtue" (MN I.65), many more benefits of virtue are stated — being dear and pleasing, and so on, culminating in the destruction of the taints. Thus virtue has as its benefit the attainment of many good qualities, beginning with freedom from remorse. Moreover —

Without it there is no foundation at all for clansmen in this Dispensation — who, then, could declare the full extent of that virtue's benefits?

Not the Ganges, nor the Yamunā, not the Sarabhū, nor the Sarasvatī, not the river Aciravatī, nor the Mahī, nor any great river, can cleanse that stain here in living beings — it is the water of virtue alone that truly cleanses beings of that stain.

Not those moisture-laden breezes, nor sandalwood, nor garlands, nor gems, nor the young rays of the moon, can allay here the deep-seated fever of beings — it is this noble virtue, utterly cool, that allays it.

Where indeed could there be a fragrance to equal the fragrance of virtue — which blows equally with the wind, and against it?

Where else is there another staircase for the ascent to heaven equal to virtue — or a gateway for entering the city of nibbāna?

Kings adorned with pearls and gems do not shine as the self-controlled shine, adorned with the ornament of virtue.

Virtue always destroys entirely the fear that begins with self-reproach, for the virtuous, and always gives rise to renown and joy.

Thus should be known this introductory account of the benefits of virtue — the root of all good qualities, and the destroyer of the very strength of faults.

Reading note — Ru-Yi

Notice the shift in register. Every question so far has been answered in flat analytical prose, but "benefits" pulls in a canonical citation repeated nearly word for word across the Dīgha, Aṅguttara, and the Vinaya's Mahāvagga alike — evidence of how standardized this five-item list already was, long before Buddhaghosa assembled this chapter — and then opens into ten couplets of devotional verse unlike anything preceding it. Pāli commentarial prose does this often: definitional questions get flat analysis, but a question about a thing's worth gets verse, as though the genre itself is marking that this part is meant to be felt, not only parsed.

The image of virtue's fragrance traveling "equally with the wind, and against it" trades on an implicit contrast with ordinary scent, which only carries downwind — the verse never spells this out, assuming the reader already knows the comparison it is quietly overturning.

How Many Kinds of Virtue? — The Full List

The Twofold Schemes, Explained

Ru-Yi Translation

Herein, in the case of the onefold division, the meaning should be understood by the method already stated. In the case of the twofold division: whatever fulfillment of a training precept laid down by the Blessed One as "this should be done" — that is performance. Whatever non-doing of what has been prohibited as "this should not be done" — that is avoidance. Here is the verbal meaning of these terms: "performance" is so called because through it people conduct themselves, proceeding by way of fulfilling the training precepts; "avoidance" is so called because by means of it people protect and guard what has been forbidden. Performance is accomplished by both faith and energy; avoidance is accomplished by faith alone. Thus it is twofold by way of performance and avoidance.

In the second pair: "good conduct" means the highest proper conduct. Good conduct itself, as an adjective, is "pertaining to good conduct"; or, what is laid down with reference to good conduct is "pertaining to good conduct" — a designation for the remaining virtue apart from the eightfold livelihood-precept set. "Being the starting point of the path that constitutes the holy life" is "fundamentals of the holy life" — a designation for the eightfold livelihood-precept virtue itself, since that virtue is the starting point of the path and must be purified at the very outset. Hence it is said: "for his bodily action, verbal action, and livelihood are already, beforehand, thoroughly purified" (MN III.431). Or again: whatever training precepts are called "minor and lesser-minor" is good-conduct virtue; the remainder is fundamentals-of-the-holy-life virtue. Or: what is included in the twofold monastic-code analysis is fundamentals-of-the-holy-life virtue, while what is included in the Khandhaka observances is good-conduct virtue — and through the accomplishment of the latter, the former succeeds. Hence it is said: "monks, that a monk should fulfill the fundamentals-of-the-holy-life state without having fulfilled the good-conduct state — this is not possible" (AN 5.21). Thus it is twofold by way of good-conduct virtue and fundamentals-of-the-holy-life virtue.

In the third pair: mere abstinence from killing living beings and the rest is abstinence-virtue. The remainder — volition and so on — is non-abstinence-virtue. Thus it is twofold by way of abstinence and non-abstinence.

In the fourth pair: "dependence" means there are two dependences — dependence on craving and dependence on views. Whatever is engaged in by one wishing for a favorable rebirth, thinking, "by this virtue may I become a god, or one among the gods" (DN III.320; MN I.186; AN 5.206, 7.50) — this is dependent on craving. Whatever is engaged in through the view that "purity comes by virtue alone" — this is dependent on views. But whatever is supramundane, together with the mundane virtue that serves as its requisite, is independent. Thus it is twofold by way of dependent and independent.

In the fifth pair: virtue undertaken after fixing a determination of time is time-limited. That undertaken and thereafter maintained for life is life-long. Thus it is twofold by way of time-limited and life-long.

In the sixth pair: virtue whose limit is determined by gain, fame, relatives, limb, or life is called "limited"; the opposite is "unlimited." And this was said in the Paṭisambhidā: "What is that virtue which is limited? There is virtue limited by gain, virtue limited by fame, virtue limited by relatives, virtue limited by limb, virtue limited by life. What is that virtue limited by gain? Here someone, for the sake of gain, because of gain, for the reason of gain, transgresses a training precept as undertaken — this is that virtue limited by gain" (Paṭis I.38). By this same method the others should be elaborated. And in the answer concerning the unlimited, too, it was said: "What is that virtue not limited by gain? Here someone, for the sake of gain, because of gain, for the reason of gain, does not even give rise to a thought of transgressing a training precept as undertaken — how much less would he transgress it! This is that virtue not limited by gain" (Paṭis I.38). By this same method the others should be elaborated. Thus it is twofold by way of limited and unlimited.

In the seventh pair: all virtue that is subject to the taints is mundane; that free of the taints is supramundane. Herein, the mundane brings about distinction of existence, and also serves as a requisite for escape from existence. As it is said: "The discipline is for the purpose of restraint; restraint is for the purpose of freedom from remorse; freedom from remorse is for the purpose of gladness; gladness is for the purpose of joy; joy is for the purpose of tranquility; tranquility is for the purpose of pleasure; pleasure is for the purpose of concentration; concentration is for the purpose of the knowledge and vision of things as they really are; the knowledge and vision of things as they really are is for the purpose of disenchantment; disenchantment is for the purpose of dispassion; dispassion is for the purpose of liberation; liberation is for the purpose of the knowledge and vision of liberation; the knowledge and vision of liberation is for the purpose of final nibbāna through non-clinging. For this purpose is the talk, for this purpose is the counsel, for this purpose is the support, for this purpose is the lending of ear — namely, the liberation of the mind through non-clinging" (Paṭis II.366). The supramundane brings about escape from existence, and is also the ground for reviewing knowledge. Thus it is twofold by way of mundane and supramundane.

Reading note — Ru-Yi

The seventh pair's chain — discipline to restraint to freedom from remorse to gladness to joy to tranquility to pleasure to concentration to knowledge-and-vision to disenchantment to dispassion to liberation — is worth reading slowly: each link is stated as existing for the sake of the next, a single argument that virtue is not an end in itself but the first domino in a causal sequence ending in liberation. The chapter is answering "why bother with the mundane kind of virtue at all?" by embedding sīla in a chain it does not control the end of.

The Threefold Schemes, Explained

Six more threefold schemes follow the same pattern — each restarts from scratch with its own basis of division, even where it reuses labels (like "inferior, medium, superior") from a scheme already given.

Ru-Yi Translation

Among the triads: in the first triad, virtue brought about by inferior desire, mind, energy, or investigation is inferior; that brought about by medium desire and the rest is medium; by superior, superior. Or: virtue undertaken out of desire for fame is inferior; out of desire for the fruit of merit, medium; undertaken thinking simply "this is to be done," in dependence on the noble state, is superior. Or: virtue tainted by such self-exaltation and disparagement of others, thinking, "I am accomplished in virtue, but these other monks are of bad conduct, of evil nature," is inferior; untainted, mundane virtue is medium; supramundane virtue is superior. Or: virtue engaged in, by the power of craving, for the sake of future existence and enjoyment, is inferior; that engaged in for the sake of one's own liberation is medium; that engaged in for the sake of the liberation of all beings — the virtue of the perfections — is superior. Thus it is threefold by way of inferior, medium, and superior.

In the second triad: virtue engaged in by one who wishes to abandon what is unbecoming to himself, treating himself as authority, out of regard for himself, is self-dominated. That engaged in by one who wishes to avoid the world's censure, treating the world as authority, out of regard for the world, is world-dominated. That engaged in by one who wishes to honor the greatness of the Dhamma, treating the Dhamma as authority, out of regard for the Dhamma, is Dhamma-dominated. Thus it is threefold by way of self-dominated, world-dominated, and Dhamma-dominated.

In the third triad: that which was called "dependent" among the pairs above — being misapprehended through craving and views — is misapprehended. That which is a requisite of the path for the virtuous ordinary person, and that associated with the path for trainees, is unmisapprehended. That associated with the fruit, for both trainees and adepts, is tranquilized. Thus it is threefold by way of misapprehended, unmisapprehended, and tranquilized.

In the fourth triad: that fulfilled by one who commits no offense, or, having committed one, has since made amends — that is purified. That of one who has committed an offense and made no amends is unpurified. The virtue of one who is in doubt about the grounds, the offense, or the transgression is called doubtful virtue. Herein, the meditator should purify unpurified virtue; and, without indulging the ground of doubt, should dispel the doubt in what is doubtful, thinking, "thus it will be comfortable for me." Thus it is threefold by way of purified, unpurified, and doubtful.

In the fifth triad: virtue associated with the four noble paths and the first three fruits of the ascetic life is the trainee's virtue. That associated with the fruit of Arahantship is the adept's virtue. The rest is neither the trainee's nor the adept's. Thus it is threefold by way of the trainee's, the adept's, and neither.

But in the Paṭisambhidā, since even the ordinary nature of various beings in the world is loosely called "sīla" — as when people say, "this one is of a happy nature, this one of a suffering nature, this one of a quarrelsome nature, this one of a vain nature" — it is therefore said, in that sense: "There are three virtues: wholesome virtue, unwholesome virtue, and indeterminate virtue" (Paṭis I.39). Thus it is also said to be threefold by way of wholesome and the rest. But here, the unwholesome does not accord with even one of the characteristics and so on of the virtue meant in this technical sense, so it is not brought in here — its threefold nature, therefore, should be understood only by the method already given.

Reading note — Ru-Yi

Watch how many times "inferior, medium, superior" gets reused across this whole question with a completely different basis each time — by mental factor, by motive, by moral taint, by object of craving — and how the second through fifth triads each mint an entirely new set of three labels. The chapter is not building one taxonomy; it is showing that "how many kinds" has no single correct answer, only as many answers as there are legitimate criteria for dividing the same thing. That, itself, is a very Abhidhamma stance to take: a phenomenon's "kind" is always relative to the lens applied to it.

The closing remark on kusala/akusala/abyākata (wholesome/unwholesome/indeterminate) is a small but pointed methodological aside: this is Abhidhamma's single most fundamental three-way classification, applied to every mental state without exception — and the chapter explicitly declines to use it here, on the ground that "unwholesome virtue" is a contradiction in terms for the technical sense of sīla it has spent this entire question defining. The Paṭisambhidā's looser, everyday use of "sīla" to mean simply "a person's nature or habit" (as in "a quarrelsome sort") is acknowledged, then set firmly aside.

The Fourfold Schemes, Explained

Four more tetrads follow — the first evaluates a monk's own attitude toward his practice rather than any external feature of the precepts themselves; the fourth names, in brief, the "four purifications" that the rest of this chapter goes on to unpack at enormous length, one subheading at a time.

Ru-Yi Translation

Among the tetrads, in the first tetrad —

Whoever here associates with those of bad virtue, and does not associate with the virtuous — such an unwise person does not see the fault in transgressing a ground of training.

One who is full of wrong intention and does not guard the faculties — the virtue of one such as this arises as partaking of decline.

But whoever is content, here, with mere accomplishment in virtue, and does not arouse further resolve toward devotion to a meditation subject —

for that monk, satisfied with virtue alone and not striving further, his virtue becomes one that partakes of stability.

But whoever, accomplished in virtue, strives for the sake of concentration — for this monk, his virtue is one that partakes of distinction.

Not content with virtue alone, but devoting himself to disenchantment — for this monk, his virtue becomes one that partakes of penetration.

Thus it is fourfold by way of that which partakes of decline and the rest.

In the second tetrad: the training precepts laid down with reference to monks, together with those precepts of monks that nuns too must observe by their own promulgation — this is monks' virtue. The training precepts laid down with reference to nuns, together with those precepts of nuns that monks too must observe by their own promulgation — this is nuns' virtue. The ten precepts of novice monks and novice nuns are the virtue of the not-fully-ordained. For male and female lay disciples: the five training precepts as their constant virtue, or, when zeal permits, ten, or, by way of the Uposatha factors, eight — this is householders' virtue. Thus it is fourfold by way of monks' virtue and the rest.

In the third tetrad: the non-transgression natural to the people of Uttarakuru is natural virtue. The customary conduct proper to each family, region, or sect is customary virtue. The virtue of the Bodhisatta's mother, spoken of thus — "It is a natural law, Ānanda, that when the Bodhisatta has descended into his mother's womb, no thought accompanied by sensual desire arises in the Bodhisatta's mother toward men" — is virtue-by-natural-law. But the virtue of Mahākassapa and other beings of pure nature, and the Bodhisatta's own virtue in his various births, is virtue caused by a prior condition. Thus it is fourfold by way of natural virtue, customary virtue, virtue-by-natural-law, and virtue caused by a prior condition.

In the fourth tetrad: the virtue spoken of by the Blessed One thus — "here a monk dwells restrained by the restraint of the Pātimokkha, possessed of good conduct and resort, seeing danger in the slightest faults, he trains, having undertaken the training precepts" (Vibh. 508; DN I.193) — this is called virtue as restraint by the Pātimokkha. And the virtue spoken of thus — "having seen a form with the eye, he does not grasp at its sign nor at its details, since, if he were to dwell with the eye faculty unrestrained, evil unwholesome states of covetousness and grief might overwhelm him, he practices for its restraint, he guards the eye faculty, he enters upon restraint of the eye faculty. Having heard a sound with the ear... having smelled an odor with the nose... having tasted a flavor with the tongue... having felt a tangible with the body... having cognized a mental object with the mind, he does not grasp at its sign... he enters upon restraint of the mind faculty" (MN I.22, 411; DN I.213; AN 4.198) — this is virtue as restraint of the faculties. And whatever abstinence there is from transgressing the six training precepts laid down for the sake of livelihood, together with the abstinence from wrong livelihood that proceeds through such evil states as trickery, talking for gain, hinting, belittling others for gain, and pursuing gain with gain — this is virtue as purification of livelihood. The use of the four requisites, purified by reflection, spoken of by the method beginning, "reflecting wisely, he makes use of the robe only for the sake of warding off cold" (MN I.23; AN 6.58) — this is called virtue connected with the requisites.

Reading note — Ru-Yi

The first tetrad is a genuine change of subject from everything before it: every earlier scheme classified virtue by an external feature — its object, its motive, its purity — while this one classifies a monk by his own attitude toward virtue he has already achieved. Complacency with mere precept-keeping, it says, is itself a kind of decline; only continuing to strive toward concentration, and then toward disenchantment, keeps virtue "progressing" rather than merely "holding." It is less a classification of virtue than a warning about resting on it.

The fourth tetrad deserves to be read slowly, because it is the chapter's hinge: these four items — restraint by the Pātimokkha, restraint of the six sense faculties, purification of livelihood, and reflective use of the four requisites — are the famous "four purifications of virtue" (catupārisuddhisīla), still taught today as the standard practical framework for a monastic's moral training. Everything said about them here is deliberately compressed to one citation apiece; the chapter's own subheadings from this point on name each of these four in turn, because the rest of Sīlaniddesa is essentially a word-by-word commentary on this one paragraph.

Restraint by the Pātimokkha — Word by Word

Having named the four purifications in brief, the chapter now commits to unpacking each in turn, one subheading at a time. Ru-Yi Translation

Herein, starting from the beginning, is a discussion determining the meaning, together with a word-by-word commentary. "Here" means: in this dispensation. "Monk" means: a clansman gone forth out of faith, who has acquired this designation either because he sees danger in saṃsāra, or because he wears cast-off cloth and the like. As to "restrained by the restraint of the Pātimokkha" — here, "Pātimokkha" means the virtue of the training precepts. For it releases and frees whoever guards and protects it from the sufferings of the states of loss and the rest — therefore it is called the "Pātimokkha." "Restraining" is restraint — a name for bodily and verbal non-transgression. The Pātimokkha itself, as a restraint, is "restraint by the Pātimokkha." Restrained by that restraint is "restrained by the restraint of the Pātimokkha" — meaning: arrived at, endowed with it. "Dwells" means: conducts himself. The meaning of "possessed of good conduct and resort" and the rest should be understood by the very method that has come down in the canonical text itself. For this was said —

"Possessed of good conduct and resort": there is good conduct, and there is bad conduct.

Herein, what is bad conduct? Bodily transgression, verbal transgression, bodily-and-verbal transgression — this is called bad conduct. All bad moral conduct, too, is bad conduct. Here, someone makes his living by giving away bamboo, or leaves, or flowers, fruit, bath-powder, or tooth-wood, or by flattery, or by ingratiating talk, or by fawning service, or by running errands, or by any other kind of wrong livelihood condemned by the Buddha — this is called bad conduct.

Herein, what is good conduct? Bodily non-transgression, verbal non-transgression, bodily-and-verbal non-transgression — this is called good conduct. All restraint by virtue, too, is good conduct. Here, someone does not make his living by any of those means, nor by any other kind of wrong livelihood condemned by the Buddha — this is called good conduct.

"Resort": there is proper resort, and there is improper resort.

Herein, what is improper resort? Here, someone frequents prostitutes, or widows, mature unmarried women, eunuchs, or nuns improperly, or frequents taverns, or lives in improper association with kings, royal ministers, sectarian teachers, or their disciples; or he associates with, frequents, and attends upon families that lack faith and confidence, that are no source of support, that are abusive and reviling, and that wish no good, no benefit, no comfort, and no security to monks, nuns, laymen, or laywomen — this is called improper resort.

Herein, what is proper resort? Here, someone does not frequent prostitutes ... nor taverns, nor lives in improper association with kings ... or sectarians' disciples; but he associates with, frequents, and attends upon families that have faith and confidence, that are a source of support, that shine with the ochre robe, that are swept by the breeze of sages coming and going, and that wish good ... and security to monks ... and laywomen — this is called proper resort. Thus, possessed, fully possessed, arrived at, fully arrived at, endowed, accomplished, and equipped with this good conduct and this resort — hence he is called "possessed of good conduct and resort" (Vibh. 511).

And here, good conduct and resort should also be understood by this further method. For bad conduct is twofold: bodily and verbal. Herein, what is bodily bad conduct? Here, someone, even when he has gone to the assembly hall, without any regard for the elder monks, jostles them while standing, jostles them while sitting, stands in front of them, sits in front of them, sits on a high seat, sits with his head covered, speaks while standing, speaks while waving his arms about; when the elder monks are walking for exercise without sandals, he walks wearing sandals; when they walk on a low walkway, he walks on a high one; when they walk on the bare ground, he walks on the walkway; he stands or sits crowding the elder monks; he keeps newly-ordained monks from a seat; in the steam-room, without asking the elder monks' permission, he feeds the fire and shuts the door; at the bathing-place, he jostles the elder monks or goes down ahead of them, jostles them or bathes ahead of them, jostles them or comes up ahead of them; entering an inner house, he jostles the elder monks or goes ahead of them, or, cutting out of line, walks again and again right in front of them; he even barges suddenly into a household's inner chambers, hidden and curtained off, where the women and daughters of the family sit; he even pats a little boy's head. This is called bodily bad conduct.

Herein, what is verbal bad conduct? Here, someone, even when gone to the assembly, without regard for the elder monks, teaches the Dhamma without being asked, answers a question without being asked, recites the Pātimokkha without authorization, speaks while standing, speaks while waving his arms about; or, entering an inner house, babbles to a woman or a girl: "So-and-so, of such-and-such family — what is there? Is there rice gruel? Is there boiled rice? Is there something to eat? What shall we drink, what shall we eat, what shall we partake of? Or what will you give me?" This is called verbal bad conduct (Mahāniddesa 87). Its opposite should be understood as good conduct.

Moreover, a monk who is respectful, deferential, possessed of shame and moral dread, well dressed and well robed, pleasing in his manner of going forward and returning, of looking ahead and aside, of bending and stretching his limbs, with downcast eyes, possessed of proper deportment, with the doors of his sense faculties guarded, moderate in eating, devoted to wakefulness, endowed with mindfulness and clear comprehension, wanting little, content, with energy aroused, careful in matters of good conduct, and mostly reverential in attitude — this too is called good conduct. Thus, first of all, good conduct should be understood.

Resort, however, is threefold: resort as supporting condition, resort as safeguard, and resort as anchor. Herein, what is resort as supporting condition? A good friend endowed with the qualities of the ten grounds for proper talk, in dependence on whom one hears what one has not yet heard, clarifies what one has heard, gets beyond doubt, straightens one's view, and gains confidence of mind; or in emulation of whom one grows in faith, in virtue, in learning, in generosity, and in wisdom — this is called resort as supporting condition.

What is resort as safeguard? Here, a monk, having entered an inner dwelling, walking along a street, goes with downcast eyes, seeing only a yoke's length ahead, well restrained — not looking at an elephant, a horse, a chariot, or a foot-soldier, not looking at a woman or a man, not looking up, not looking down, not gazing about in all directions — this is called resort as safeguard.

What is resort as anchor? The four foundations of mindfulness, wherein the mind is anchored. For this was said by the Blessed One: "And what, monks, is a monk's own resort, his own ancestral domain? It is just the four foundations of mindfulness" (SN 5.372). This is called resort as anchor. Thus, possessed ... endowed with this good conduct and this resort — by this method too he is called "possessed of good conduct and resort."

"Seeing danger in the slightest faults": one whose habit is to see danger in faults of minute extent — such as a training rule unintentionally transgressed, or merely the arising of an unwholesome thought, and so on. "He trains, having undertaken the training precepts": whatever there is to be trained in among the training precepts, all of that he rightly undertakes and trains in. And here, by this much — "restrained by the restraint of the Pātimokkha" — virtue as restraint by the Pātimokkha has been shown by way of a teaching centered on the person. But everything beginning with "possessed of good conduct and resort" should be understood as spoken in order to show the practice by practicing which that virtue is accomplished.

Reading note — Ru-Yi

Notice that the chapter gives two entirely different explanations of the same phrase, "possessed of good conduct and resort," back to back, without flagging any tension between them. The first, formal explanation (citing Vibh. 511) is about propriety in the world — the right livelihood, the right company, the right households to associate with. The second explanation shifts registers completely: "good conduct" becomes a monk's courtesy toward his own seniors, illustrated with a startlingly concrete list — jostling elders at the bathing place, hogging the steam-room fire, patting a strange child's head — and "resort" becomes something almost entirely inward: a spiritual friend, physical self-restraint on the alms round, and finally the four foundations of mindfulness themselves. The text simply lays both explanations side by side and lets the reader hold them together; this is characteristic of how Pāli commentarial literature handles received material it has no interest in reconciling into one single account.

The concrete list of "bodily bad conduct" is worth pausing on for what it reveals about the community it describes: a young monk's failures are not moral catastrophes but small breaches of deference — walking in sandals when an elder walks barefoot, sitting on a high seat, entering a house ahead of a senior. The text is at least as interested in monastic hierarchy and courtesy as it is in ethics in the abstract sense modern readers might expect from a chapter on "virtue."

Key vocabulary — for Pāli learners
anāpucchā

"without having asked [permission]" — from āpucchati, "to ask, to consult." A recurring courtesy-marker throughout monastic literature: the opposite, āpucchitvā, "having asked," signals proper deference.

kalyāṇamittam.

"good friend" / "spiritual friend" — kalyāṇa (good, beautiful) + mitta (friend). A foundational concept: the Buddha elsewhere calls good friendship "the whole of the holy life," not merely half of it.

satipaṭṭhānant.

"foundation of mindfulness" — sati (mindfulness) + paṭṭhāna (foundation, establishment). The cattāro satipaṭṭhānā (four foundations: body, feeling, mind, and mental objects) are among the most important terms in the entire Buddhist path.

yugamatta

"a yoke's length" (yuga = yoke, the width of a plow-yoke) — the traditional short downward distance a mindful monk is said to look while walking, roughly the ground just ahead of his feet.

Restraint of the Faculties — The Elder Mahātissa

The second purification's canonical formula ("having seen a form with the eye, he is not a grasper of signs or details...") gets a word-by-word gloss, culminating in one of the Visuddhimagga's most famous short stories — offered not as an argument but as a demonstration of what "not grasping the details" actually looks like in a real person.

Pali Source

Yaṃ panetaṃ tadanantaraṃ "so cakkhunā rūpaṃ disvā"tiādinā nayena dassitaṃ indriyasaṃvarasīlaṃ, tattha so ti pātimokkhasaṃvarasīle ṭhito bhikkhu. Cakkhunā rūpaṃ disvāti kāraṇavasena cakkhūti laddhavohārena rūpadassanasamatthena cakkhuviññāṇena rūpaṃ disvā. "Nābhijānāmi itthī vā, puriso vā ito gato;

Ru-Yi Translation

And that restraint-of-the-faculties virtue which, immediately following, is shown by the method beginning "having seen a form with the eye" — herein, "he" means a monk established in the virtue of restraint by the Pātimokkha. "Having seen a form with the eye" means: having seen a form by means of eye-consciousness, which is capable of seeing form and which has acquired the designation "eye" by way of being its instrumental cause. But the ancient teachers said: "The eye does not see form, since it is without mind; and the mind does not see, since it is without eyes. But when door and object come together, one sees by means of the mind that has the eye-sensitivity as its physical basis." This way of speaking is like saying "he shoots with a bow" — it is called speech in terms of the accompanying apparatus. Therefore "having seen a form by eye-consciousness" is the only meaning intended here. "Not a grasper of signs" means: he does not grasp a sign that is the ground for defilement — whether the sign "woman" or "man," or the sign of beauty and so on — he stops at the mere fact of having seen. "Not a grasper of details" means: he does not grasp the various aspects — movements of hand or foot, a smile, laughter, speech, a glance, and so on — that have acquired the name "detail" because they make the defilements manifest, again and again, in a distinct way; he grasps only what is bare fact present there — like the Elder Mahātissa, who dwelt at Cetiya Mountain.

It is said that, as the Elder was coming from Cetiya Mountain to Anurādhapura for alms, a certain daughter-in-law of good family, having quarreled with her husband, beautifully adorned and dressed like a celestial nymph, having set out early from Anurādhapura to go to her relatives' house, saw him on the road and, her mind distorted, burst into loud laughter. The Elder, looking to see what this was, gained the perception of foulness in the bones of her teeth, and attained Arahantship. Hence it was said —

"Having seen the bones of her teeth, he recalled his earlier perception; standing right there, that Elder attained Arahantship."

Now her husband too, coming along the same road, saw the Elder and asked: "Venerable sir, did you happen to see a woman?" The Elder said to him —

"I do not know whether it was a woman or a man that went by here; but a collection of bones is going along this highway."

Reading note — Ru-Yi

Notice that the chapter shifts from argument to anecdote to make its point. "Not grasping the details" could easily stay an abstract instruction, so the text supplies a concrete demonstration: a monk whose mind has been so reshaped by the meditation on foulness that a stranger's laugh registers, to him, as nothing but exposed teeth — bone, not a person, and certainly not a woman. His answer to the husband is not evasive; it is exactly true to what he actually perceived. The story does real philosophical work that the definition alone could not: it shows a mind in which "sign" and "detail" have genuinely stopped being available as objects of attention, rather than a mind that is simply refusing to look.

Restraint of the Faculties — Where Does Restraint Actually Happen?

The chapter now asks a genuinely difficult question: restraint is supposed to happen "in" the eye faculty, but the eye faculty has no consciousness of its own, and full-fledged moral quality only crystallizes at one brief moment deep inside an otherwise involuntary sequence of mind-moments. The answer draws on the Abhidhamma's technical model of the cognitive process, and resolves the puzzle with the chapter's own memorable simile of a walled city.

Pali Source

Yatvādhikaraṇamentiādimhi yaṃkāraṇā yassa cakkhundriyāsaṃvarassa hetu etaṃ puggalaṃ satikavāṭena cakkhundriyaṃ asaṃvutaṃ apihitacakkhudvāraṃ hutvā viharantaṃ ete abhijjhādayo dhammā anvāssaveyyuṃ anubandheyyuṃ. Tassa saṃvarāya paṭipajjatīti tassa cakkhundriyassa satikavāṭena pidahanatthāya paṭipajjati. Evaṃ paṭipajjantoyeva ca rakkhati cakkhundriyaṃ, cakkhundriye saṃvaraṃ āpajjatītipi vuccati.

Nagaradvāresu hi pihitesu corānaṃ paveso natthi, evameva javane sīlādīsu uppannesu dvārampi guttaṃ hoti, bhavaṅgampi āvajjanādīnipi vīthicittāni. Ru-Yi Translation

As to the phrase beginning "since, if...": for whatever reason it might be that, were this person to dwell with the eye faculty unrestrained by the bolt of mindfulness, with the eye-door left unclosed, these states beginning with covetousness would flow in upon him and pursue him — "he practices for its restraint" means: he practices for the purpose of closing that eye faculty with the bolt of mindfulness. And it is precisely by practicing in this way that he is also said to "guard the eye faculty" and to "enter upon restraint of the eye faculty."

Herein, although there is, strictly speaking, neither restraint nor non-restraint in the eye faculty itself — for neither mindfulness nor forgetfulness arises in dependence on the eye-sensitivity — yet when a visible object comes into the eye's range, then, after the life-continuum has arisen twice and ceased, the functional mind-element, performing the function of adverting, arises and ceases. After that, eye-consciousness performs the function of seeing. After that, the resultant mind-element performs the function of receiving. After that, the rootless resultant mind-consciousness-element performs the function of investigating. After that, the rootless functional mind-consciousness-element, performing the function of determining, arises and ceases; immediately after that, impulsion runs its course.

And even there, neither at the moment of the life-continuum, nor at any moment of adverting and the rest, is there restraint or non-restraint. But at the moment of impulsion, if bad conduct, or forgetfulness, or unknowing, or impatience, or laziness arises, there is non-restraint. When this is so, it is called non-restraint in the eye faculty. Why? Because, when that is present, the door too becomes unguarded — and so too the life-continuum, and the adverting and other process-consciousnesses.

Like what? Just as, when a city's four gates are left unguarded, even though the doors, storerooms, and inner chambers of the houses within are well guarded, still all the goods within the city remain, in effect, unprotected and unguarded — for thieves, once they had entered through the city gate, could do as they pleased — just so, when bad conduct and the rest have arisen at the moment of impulsion, that non-restraint being present, the door too becomes unguarded, and so too the life-continuum and the adverting and other process-consciousnesses.

But when virtue and the rest have arisen at that same moment, the door becomes guarded — and so too the life-continuum and the adverting and other process-consciousnesses. Like what? Just as, when a city's gates are shut, even though the houses within are unguarded, still all the goods within the city remain, in effect, well protected and well guarded — for when a city's gates are closed, there is no entry for thieves — just so, when virtue and the rest have arisen at the moment of impulsion, the door becomes guarded, and so too the life-continuum and the adverting and other process-consciousnesses. Therefore, even though arising only at the moment of impulsion, it is called restraint in the eye faculty.

The same method applies to "having heard a sound with the ear" and the rest. Thus, in brief, this should be understood as restraint-of-the-faculties virtue, having as its characteristic the avoidance of grasping at signs and the rest — the ground for defilements to pursue one — with respect to visible forms and the other sense objects.

Reading note — Ru-Yi

The puzzle this passage solves is a real one: if restraint has to happen "in" the eye faculty, but seeing unfolds as a rapid, largely involuntary sequence of mind-moments — life-continuum, adverting, seeing, receiving, investigating, determining, only then impulsion — where in that sequence could a moral quality actually take hold? The answer is precise: nowhere before impulsion (javana), the one point in the whole sequence where wholesome or unwholesome factors can actually arise. Everything upstream of it — the life-continuum, the adverting, the bare act of seeing itself — is morally neutral, simply mechanical.

The walled-city simile is doing real conceptual work, not just decorating the point: it explains how a single, later, brief event (the state of mind at impulsion) can be said to determine the character of an entire process that has already mostly finished by the time that event occurs — the outer gate's condition retroactively describes the security of everything already inside, in exactly the way the presence or absence of restraint at javana gets projected back onto the whole cognitive series as "restrained" or "unrestrained" eye faculty. This is a good example of how Vism uses everyday similes to make Abhidhamma's notoriously fine-grained, moment-by-moment (khaṇika) analysis of mind intuitively graspable.

Key vocabulary — for Pāli learners
nimitta / anubyañjana

"sign" and "detail" — the two things restraint of the faculties specifically declines to grasp: the overall sign (e.g. "woman," "beautiful") and its specific particulars (a smile, a glance).

asubhant.

foulness, repulsiveness — the meditation subject (asubha-bhāvanā, contemplating a corpse's stages of decay, or here, simply bones) that reshaped the Elder Mahātissa's perception so thoroughly that he saw only teeth, not a woman.

bhavaṅgant.

"life-continuum" — the mind's passive, resting state between active moments of cognition; a foundational concept in Abhidhamma's model of mental process.

javanant.

"impulsion" — from javati, "to run swiftly." The single phase of a cognitive process where kammically wholesome or unwholesome quality is actually generated; everything before it in this passage's sequence is morally neutral.

vīthicittant.

"process-consciousness" — vīthi (a path, a street — the same word, fittingly, used for the street in the city simile) + citta (mind). The technical name for one mind-moment within an active cognitive series, as opposed to the passive bhavaṅga between series.

"Herein, what is talking for gain? In one who is intent on gain, honor, and praise, who is of evil wishes and overcome by desire — addressing others, talking, chatting, exalted talk, mutual exalted talk, flattering, mutual flattering, extolling, mutual extolling, speaking pleasantly, ingratiating conduct, mung-bean-soup speech, and fawning service toward others — this is called talking for gain.

"Herein, what is hinting? In one who is intent on gain, honor, and praise, who is of evil wishes and overcome by desire — giving others a hint, the act of hinting, an intimation, the act of intimating, insinuating talk, roundabout talk — this is called hinting.

"Herein, what is belittling? In one who is intent on gain, honor, and praise, who is of evil wishes and overcome by desire — abusing others, disparaging, finding fault, casting reproach, thorough casting of reproach, mocking, thorough mocking, defaming, thorough defaming, spreading ill repute, and speaking ill of others behind their backs — this is called belittling.

"Herein, what is pursuing gain with gain? One who is intent on gain, honor, and praise, of evil wishes, overcome by desire, carries material goods received from here to there, or brings material goods received from there to here. Whatever such wanting, seeking, and searching for material goods by means of material goods, such questing, hunting, and pursuing — this is called pursuing gain with gain" (Vibh. 862-865).

Reading note — Ru-Yi

Notice the change in register from everything before it: this is canonical legal-code prose, not narrative or verse, and it defines each term not with a single crisp sentence but with a chain of near-synonyms piled up together — "deceiving, the act of deceiving, the state of having deceived," "wanting, seeking, and searching," "questing, hunting, and pursuing." This is the Vibhaṅga's characteristic method of definition by accumulation: circling a concept with overlapping near-equivalents rather than isolating one precise boundary. It is also worth noticing that this passage openly splices two different canonical sources together — the Parivāra for the six training rules, the Vibhaṅga for the five terms — exactly the citation-based method this whole chapter has used from its first paragraph onward.

The five terms named here — deceit, talking for gain, hinting, belittling, and pursuing gain with gain — are bare definitions only. Each will get its own vivid illustration in the commentary that follows, including two of the Visuddhimagga's best-loved stories: a monk who publicly refuses fine gifts to look ascetic while privately maneuvering donors into giving him even more, and a monk who turns a household's excuse of "we have no rice" into an elaborate riddle about a snake that leaves the housewife with no choice but to admit she was caught.

Deceit — The Ascetic Who Refuses Gifts

The detailed commentary finally begins, working through the Mahāniddesa's threefold breakdown of "deceit" one ground at a time. The first — deceit by way of the use of requisites — is illustrated with the chapter's most quietly cynical story: a monk whose theatrical renunciation is, in fact, a fundraising strategy.

Pali Source

Imissā pana pāḷiyā evamattho veditabbo. Kuhananiddese tāva lābhasakkārasilokasannissitassāti lābhañca sakkārañca kittisaddañca sannissitassa, patthayantassāti attho. Pāpicchassāti asantaguṇadīpanakāmassa. Icchāpakatassāti icchāya apakatassa, upaddutassāti attho.

Ru-Yi Translation

The meaning of this canonical passage should be understood as follows. First, in the exposition of "deceit": "intent on gain, honor, and praise" means intent on gain, honor, and repute — that is, longing for them. "Of evil wishes" means wishing to display qualities one does not actually possess. "Overcome by desire" means overcome — that is, afflicted — by desire.

Beyond this point: since a threefold "ground for deceit" has come down in the Mahāniddesa — connected with the use of requisites, with insinuating talk, and with deportment — therefore, in order to show this threefold scheme, the phrase beginning "or by way of the use of requisites" was introduced. Herein: a monk, though he actually wants the robes and the like with which he is invited, refuses them out of his evil wishes; and, knowing that those householders already have well-established faith in him, he foresees that they will think, "oh, our master wants so little that he won't accept anything at all — how fortunate we would truly be if he would accept even some small thing." When they then bring him fine robes and the like by various means, he accepts them, showing only that he does so out of a wish to favor them — and from that point on, the very astonishment this produces becomes the cause for donors to bring him gifts even by the cartload. This should be understood as the ground for deceit reckoned by the use of requisites. For this was said in the Mahāniddesa —

"What is the ground for deceit reckoned by the use of requisites? Here, householders invite a monk with robes, almsfood, lodging, and medicinal requisites for the sick. He, though of evil wishes, overcome by desire, and actually wanting the robe ... and the other requisites, out of a wish for more, refuses the robe. He likewise refuses the almsfood ... the lodging. He refuses the medicinal requisites for the sick. He speaks thus: "What use has an ascetic for an expensive robe? This alone is fitting: that an ascetic should gather rags from a charnel ground, a rubbish-heap, or a shop-front, make them into a patched robe, and wear that. What use has an ascetic for expensive almsfood? This alone is fitting: that an ascetic should make his living by gleaning scraps, by mouthfuls gathered on the almsround. What use has an ascetic for an expensive lodging? This alone is fitting: that an ascetic should dwell at the root of a tree, or in the open air. What use has an ascetic for expensive medicinal requisites? This alone is fitting: that an ascetic should make his medicine from fermented urine or a piece of myrobalan." In accordance with this, he wears a coarse robe, eats coarse almsfood, uses a coarse lodging, and uses coarse medicinal requisites. Then the householders come to know him thus: "This ascetic wants little, is content, secluded, unattached, energetic — an advocate of the ascetic practices." They invite him again and again, more and more, with robes ... and the other requisites. He speaks thus: "From the presence of three things, a faithful clansman generates much merit. From the presence of faith, a faithful clansman generates much merit. From the presence of a gift ... and from the presence of those worthy to receive it, a faithful clansman generates much merit. And you have this faith, and a gift is at hand, and I am the recipient — if I do not accept it, you will be excluded from the merit. I myself have no need of this; but I accept it purely out of compassion for you." On this account he accepts many robes. He accepts much almsfood ... and medicinal requisites. Whatever such scowling, the state of scowling, deceiving, the act of deceiving, and the state of having deceived there is — this is the ground for deceit reckoned by the use of requisites" (Mahāniddesa 87).

Reading note — Ru-Yi

What makes this story land is that the monk never actually lies. Every individual statement he makes is defensible, even admirable — an ascetic really should be content with little; accepting a gift out of compassion for the giver really is a legitimate reason to accept it. The "deceit" is not in any single sentence but in the whole engineered sequence: performing contentment in order to make people more eager to give, then reframing acceptance itself as a favor done for the donor's sake. It is a portrait of how virtue-signaling can function as a fundraising strategy without a single false statement ever being spoken — which is precisely why the text needs a story to make the point, rather than a rule that could simply be checked against.

Deceit — Insinuating Talk and Affected Deportment

The Mahāniddesa's remaining two grounds for deceit both work by implication rather than direct claim: the first lets a monk praise the trappings of sainthood so that listeners infer he possesses it himself; the second performs the outward appearance of deep meditation through carefully staged posture and gait.

"What is the ground for deceit reckoned by insinuating talk? Here, someone of evil wishes, overcome by desire, intending to be esteemed — thinking, 'thus will people esteem me' — speaks words connected with a noble attainment. He says: 'Whoever wears such a robe is a greatly powerful ascetic.' 'Whoever carries such a bowl, a copper vessel, a water-strainer with its filter-cloth, a key, a waist-band, or sandals of such a kind, is a greatly powerful ascetic,' he says. 'He is one who has such a preceptor, such a teacher, such fellow-pupils under the same preceptor or teacher, such a friend, acquaintance, intimate, or companion. Whoever dwells in such a monastery, a vaulted building, a mansion, a long house, a cave, a grotto, a hut, a gabled house, a watch-tower, a round hut, a look-out room, an assembly hall, a pavilion, or at the root of a tree, is a greatly powerful ascetic,' he says. Or else he says: 'Grimacer upon grimacer, frowner upon frowner, deceiver upon deceiver, talker upon talker, one impressive of face — this ascetic is a gainer of such peaceful dwellings and attainments' — speaking words that are deep, hidden, subtle, concealed, supramundane, and connected with emptiness. Whatever such scowling, the state of scowling, deceiving, the act of deceiving, and the state of having deceived there is — this is the ground for deceit reckoned by insinuating talk" (Mahāniddesa 87).

But of one who is simply of evil wishes, astonishing others by a deportment adopted with the intention of being esteemed — this should be understood as the ground for deceit connected with deportment. As it is said: "What is the ground for deceit reckoned by deportment? Here, someone of evil wishes, overcome by desire, intending to be esteemed — thinking, 'thus will people esteem me' — arranges his gait, arranges his standing, arranges his sitting, arranges his lying down; he walks with studied intention, stands with intention, sits with intention, lies down with intention; he walks as if concentrated, stands, sits, and lies down as if concentrated; and he meditates ostentatiously, in full view of others. Whatever such fixing, setting up, and arranging of deportment, together with scowling, the state of scowling, deceiving, the act of deceiving, and the state of having deceived there is — this is called the ground for deceit reckoned by deportment" (Mahāniddesa 87).

Herein, "reckoned by the use of requisites" means: by the use of requisites so reckoned, or by what is so reckoned. "By insinuating talk" means: by talk spoken close to the point, indirectly. "Or of deportment" means: of the fourfold deportment. "Fixing" and the rest mean: a setting up, or a setting up with studied care. "Setting up" means: the manner of setting up. "Arranging" means: a thorough contriving — that is, making oneself appear pleasing. "Scowling" means: producing a frown by displaying a state of being established in exertion beforehand — that is, a contraction of the face. One whose habit is to produce such a frown is "a scowler." The state of being a scowler is "the state of scowling." "Deceit" means: causing astonishment. The proceeding of a deceiver is "the act of deceiving." The state of one who has deceived is "the state of having deceived."

Reading note — Ru-Yi

Notice that the second ground for deceit never has the speaker claim anything about himself directly — he only describes what kind of ascetic owns such-and-such robe, bowl, or dwelling, and lets the listener supply the inference. This is a study in implicature as a deceptive tool: nothing said is technically a claim about the speaker, so nothing said is technically false, yet the entire performance is designed to produce one specific false belief in the hearer's mind. The string of rhyming, doubled epithets — "grimacer upon grimacer, frowner upon frowner" — is, tellingly, described by the text itself as sounding "deep, hidden, subtle, concealed, supramundane, and connected with emptiness": genuinely profound Buddhist vocabulary (suññatā, emptiness, is one of the tradition's most serious concepts) being imitated purely for its impressive sound. The text is, in effect, warning its own readers against a kind of counterfeit profundity that borrows the tradition's most serious words as costume.

Talking for Gain — Flattery, Sugarcane, and Self-Promotion

The second wrong-livelihood term gets the same word-by-word treatment as the first, gliding from dry definition into another small, vivid scene — a monk maneuvering a lay donor into giving him sugarcane without ever actually asking for it.

Ru-Yi Translation

In the exposition of "talking for gain": "addressing" means, having seen people who have come to the monastery, saying, "For what purpose, good sirs, have you come? Is it to invite the monks? If so, go on ahead — I will follow after with my bowl" — such talk, opening the conversation from the very start, is "addressing." Or else, bringing oneself forward, saying, "I am Tissa; the king is devoted to me; such-and-such minister is devoted to me" — such self-promoting talk is also "addressing." "Talking" is simply talk of the kind already described, but when one has been asked a question first. "Chatting" means: fearing that the householders might grow bored, one gives them opening after opening to respond, talking skilfully to keep them engaged. "Exalted talk" means talk that raises someone up: "a great householder, a great sea-captain, a great benefactor." "Mutual exalted talk" is talk that raises both parties up, in every respect.

"Flattering" means: saying, "Lay disciples, at a time like this you used to give the offering of new robe-cloth — why do you not give it now?" — speaking on in this way until they say things such as, "We will give it, venerable sir, we just haven't found the opportunity" — this repeated pressing, up to that point, is called a kind of binding, or entangling. Or else, having seen a handful of sugarcane, he asks, "Where was this brought from, lay disciple?" "From the sugarcane field, venerable sir." "Is the sugarcane there sweet?" "One would have to taste it to know, venerable sir." It is then proper for him to say: "Well then, lay disciple — give some sugarcane to the monk!" Whatever such entangling talk there is — pressed even upon someone trying to wriggle free of giving — this is "flattering." Flattering done repeatedly, in every respect, is "mutual flattering."

"Extolling" means: saying, "This family knows only me — if any gift is to be given here, it is given only to me." Such self-promoting proclamation is called "extolling," that is, an inflating of oneself. The story of Telakandarika should be told here. Extolling done repeatedly, in every respect, is "mutual extolling."

"Speaking pleasantly" is simply speaking agreeably again and again, without regard for whether it accords with truth or with the Dhamma. "Ingratiating conduct" is a lowly manner of behaving — conducting oneself by placing oneself lower and lower than others. "Mung-bean-soup-ness" means being like mung-bean soup: just as, when mung beans are cooked, some grains fail to soften while the rest do, so too a person in whose speech only a little is true and the rest false is called "mung-bean-soup-like"; the state of being such a person is "mung-bean-soup-ness." "Fawning service" means the state of one who fawningly serves — like a nurse who carries the children of a household about on her hip or shoulder, that is, who bears them to win favor. The work of one who so fawningly serves is "fawning-bearing"; the state of such fawning-bearing is "fawning service."

Reading note — Ru-Yi

The sugarcane exchange is a small masterpiece of never asking directly: the monk's three questions — where is it from, is it sweet, how would one know — are each individually innocent, and only the final line, "give some sugarcane to the monk," retroactively reveals the entire exchange as an approach march. This is the same structural trick as the "insinuating talk" ground for deceit two sections back — never state the request, only arrange the conversation so that someone else states it for you — reapplied here to a much smaller, everyday transaction than robes or reputation. The mung-bean-soup image is worth sitting with on its own terms: it is a folk-cooking observation (some beans in a pot of mung beans just never soften, no matter how long they cook) turned into a remarkably precise metaphor for a specific kind of untrustworthy speech — not an outright liar, but someone whose words are unpredictably, unevenly true.

mung-bean soup — mugga (mung bean) + sūpa (soup, curry); the source image for muggasūpyatā, "mung-bean-soup-ness," describing speech that is only partly true.

gahapatikam.

Hinting — The Monk and the Snake

The third wrong-livelihood term closes with the chapter's other celebrated story: a monk who, refused with the excuse "there is no rice," discovers exactly what the household actually has stored away — and reports it back not as an accusation, but as an elaborate riddle about a snake.

Pali Source

Nemittikatāniddese nimittanti yaṃkiñci paresaṃ paccayadānasaññājanakaṃ kāyavacīkammaṃ. Nimittakammanti khādanīyaṃ gahetvā gacchante disvā "kiṃ khādanīyaṃ labhitthā"tiādinā nayena nimittakaraṇaṃ. Obhāsoti paccayapaṭisaṃyuttakathā. Obhāsakammanti vacchapālake disvā "kiṃ ime vacchā khīragovacchā udāhu takkagovacchā"ti pucchitvā "khīragovacchā, bhante"ti vutte "na khīragovacchā, yadi khīragovacchā siyuṃ, bhikkhūpi khīraṃ labheyyu"nti evamādinā nayena tesaṃ dārakānaṃ mātāpitūnaṃ nivedetvā khīradāpanādikaṃ obhāsakaraṇaṃ. Sāmantajappāti samīpaṃ katvā jappanaṃ. Kulūpakabhikkhu vatthu cettha vattabbaṃ.

Kulūpako kira bhikkhu bhuñjitukāmo gehaṃ pavisitvā nisīdi. Taṃ disvā adātukāmā gharaṇī "taṇḍulā natthī"ti bhaṇantī taṇḍule āharitukāmā viya paṭivissakagharaṃ gatā. Bhikkhupi antogabbhaṃ pavisitvā olokento kavāṭakoṇe ucchuṃ, bhājane guḷaṃ, piṭake loṇamacchaphāle, kumbhiyaṃ taṇḍule, ghaṭe ghataṃ disvā nikkhamitvā nisīdi. Gharaṇī "taṇḍule nālatthaṃ"nti āgatā. Bhikkhu "upāsike, ajja bhikkhā na sampajjissatīti paṭikacceva nimittaṃ addasa"nti āha. Kiṃ, bhanteti. Kavāṭakoṇe nikkhittaṃ ucchuṃ viya sappaṃ addasaṃ, "taṃ paharissāmī"ti olokento bhājane ṭhapitaṃ guḷapiṇḍaṃ viya pāsāṇaṃ, leḍḍukena pahaṭena sappena kataṃ piṭake nikkhittaloṇamacchaphālasadisaṃ phaṇaṃ, tassa taṃ leḍḍuṃ ḍaṃsitukāmassa kumbhiyā taṇḍulasadise dante, athassa kupitassa ghaṭe pakkhittaghatasadisaṃ mukhato nikkhamantaṃ visamissakaṃ kheḷanti. Sā "na sakkā muṇḍakaṃ vañcetu"nti ucchuṃ datvā odanaṃ pacitvā ghataguḷamacchehi saddhiṃ sabbaṃ adāsīti. Evaṃ samīpaṃ katvā jappanaṃ sāmantajappāti veditabbaṃ. Parikathāti yathā taṃ labhati tassa parivattetvā kathananti.

Ru-Yi Translation

In the exposition of "hinting": "a hint" is any bodily or verbal act that produces in others the perception that they should give a requisite. "The act of hinting" is the making of a hint by such means as, having seen people going along carrying food, asking, "Did you get some food?" and the like. "An intimation" is talk connected with obtaining a requisite. "The act of intimating" is: having seen cowherds, asking, "Are these calves milk-calves or buttermilk-calves?"; when told, "They are milk-calves, venerable sir," saying, "They can't be milk-calves — if they really were, the monks would be getting some milk too" — making an intimation by such means, reported to the children's parents, so as to bring about the giving of milk and the like. "Insinuating talk" is talk brought close to the point, without stating it directly. The story of the family-visiting monk should be told here.

It is said that a monk who regularly visited a certain family, wishing to eat, entered the house and sat down. Seeing him, the housewife, unwilling to give anything, said, "There is no rice," and went off to a neighbor's house as if to borrow some. The monk, too, entering the inner room and looking around, saw sugarcane in the corner behind the door, jaggery in a bowl, pieces of salted fish in a basket, rice in a pot, and ghee in a jar; having seen these, he came out and sat down again. The housewife came back, saying, "I couldn't get any rice." The monk said, "Lay sister, I already saw the sign beforehand that today the alms would not turn out well." "What was that, venerable sir?" "I saw, in the corner behind the door, what looked like a piece of sugarcane — a snake. Looking for something to strike it with, I saw a stone like the lump of jaggery in the bowl. When I struck it with the stone, the snake raised a hood like the pieces of salted fish in the basket. Wanting to bite the stone, it showed teeth like the rice in the pot. And then, enraged, it spat venom-mixed spittle from its mouth like the ghee poured into the jar." She thought, "There's no fooling this shaveling," and gave him the sugarcane, cooked the rice, and gave him everything — the ghee, the jaggery, and the fish as well. Talk brought close to the point in this way should be understood as "insinuating talk." "Roundabout talk" is talk turned this way and that, with the aim of getting whatever one is after.

Reading note — Ru-Yi

The snake riddle is constructed with real narrative craft: each clause maps one hidden item onto one part of the snake encounter — sugarcane to snake, jaggery to the stone thrown at it, salted fish to its raised hood, rice to its teeth, ghee to its venomous spit — so that the story is simultaneously a complete, coherent anecdote and a complete, itemized inventory of everything the monk saw in the back room. Nothing in it could be prosecuted as a lie; he never says "I saw your hidden food," only tells a story that happens to correspond to it point for point. The housewife's own verdict — "there's no fooling this shaveling" — is the text's way of confirming that she understood exactly what had happened, and gave everything anyway, precisely because the trick was clever enough to admire.

gharaṇīf.

Belittling and Pursuing Gain With Gain

The commentary closes out the five wrong-livelihood terms with the two least narrative and most purely lexical: a term-by-term gloss of "belittling," including two vivid etymological images, and a brief gloss of "pursuing gain with gain," pointing to one more story the reader is left to supply.

Pali Source

Nippesikatāniddese akkosanāti dasahi akkosavatthūhi akkosanaṃ. Vambhanāti paribhavitvā kathanaṃ. Garahaṇāti assaddho appasannotiādinā nayena dosāropanā. Ukkhepanāti mā etaṃ ettha kathethāti vācāya ukkhipanaṃ. Sabbatobhāgena savatthukaṃ sahetukaṃ katvā ukkhepanā samukkhepanā. Atha vā adentaṃ disvā "aho dānapatī"ti evaṃ ukkhipanaṃ ukkhepanā. Mahādānapatīti evaṃ suṭṭhu ukkhepanā samukkhepanā. Khipanāti kiṃ imassa jīvitaṃ bījabhojinoti evaṃ uppaṇḍanā. Saṃkhipanāti kiṃ imaṃ adāyakoti bhaṇatha, yo niccakālaṃ sabbesampi natthīti vacanaṃ detīti suṭṭhutaraṃ uppaṇḍanā. Pāpanāti adāyakattassa avaṇṇassa vā pāpanaṃ. Sabbatobhāgena pāpanā sampāpanā. Avaṇṇahārikāti evaṃ me avaṇṇabhayāpi dassatīti gehato gehaṃ gāmato gāmaṃ janapadato janapadaṃ avaṇṇaharaṇaṃ. Parapiṭṭhimaṃsikatāti purato madhuraṃ bhaṇitvā parammukhe avaṇṇabhāsitā. Esā hi abhimukhaṃ oloketuṃ asakkontassa parammukhānaṃ piṭṭhimaṃsaṃ khādanamiva hoti, tasmā parapiṭṭhimaṃsikatāti vuttā. Ayaṃ vuccati nippesikatāti ayaṃ yasmā veḷupesikāya viya abbhaṅgaṃ parassa guṇaṃ nippeseti nipuñchati, yasmā vā gandhajātaṃ nipisitvā gandhamagganā viya paraguṇe nipisitvā vicuṇṇetvā esā lābhamagganā hoti, tasmā nippesikatāti vuccatīti.

Lābhena lābhaṃ nijigīsanatāniddese nijigīsanatāti magganā. Ito laddhanti imamhā gehā laddhaṃ. Amutrāti amukamhi gehe. Eṭṭhīti icchanā. Gaveṭṭhīti magganā. Pariyeṭṭhīti punappunaṃ magganā. Ādito paṭṭhāya laddhaṃ laddhaṃ bhikkhaṃ tatra tatra kuladārakānaṃ datvā ante khīrayāguṃ labhitvā gatabhikkhuvatthu cettha kathetabbaṃ. Esanātiādīni eṭṭhiādīnameva vevacanāni, tasmā eṭṭhīti esanā. Gaveṭṭhīti gavesanā, pariyeṭṭhīti pariyesanā. Iccevamettha yojanā veditabbā. Ayaṃ kuhanādīnaṃ attho.

Ru-Yi Translation

In the exposition of "belittling": "abusing" is abuse by means of the ten grounds for abuse. "Disparaging" is speaking having held someone in contempt. "Finding fault" is imputing blame by such means as saying, "faithless, without confidence," and the like. "Casting reproach" is a verbal casting-off — saying, "Don't speak of this here." Casting reproach done thoroughly, giving it grounds and reasons in every respect, is "thorough casting of reproach." Or else, having seen someone who is not giving, saying sarcastically, "Oh, what a benefactor!" — such a casting-up is "casting reproach." Saying, "What a great benefactor!" in this way is a thorough casting-up — "thorough casting of reproach." "Mocking" is ridicule such as, "What kind of life does this seed-eater have?" "Thorough mocking" is a more thorough ridicule — saying, "Why do you call this person a non-giver? He constantly gives everyone the very same answer, 'there isn't any.'" "Defaming" is causing someone to arrive at a reputation for being a non-giver, or at disrepute generally. Defaming done thoroughly, in every respect, is "thorough defaming." "Spreading ill repute" is carrying a bad reputation from house to house, village to village, country to country, thinking, "Out of fear of this bad reputation, he will give to me too." "Speaking ill of others behind their backs" is speaking sweetly to someone's face, and speaking ill of them once their back is turned — for, being something one cannot do while facing a person directly, it is like eating the flesh of another's back once they have turned away, hence it is called "back-flesh-eating." This is called "belittling" because, like a bamboo scraper, it scrapes and wipes off another's oiled-on virtue; or because, just as one seeking perfume grinds down fragrant substances to extract their scent, this seeking of gain grinds down and pulverizes another's good qualities — hence it is called "belittling."

In the exposition of "pursuing gain with gain": "pursuing" is seeking. "Received from here" means received from this house; "there" means at such-and-such other house. "Wanting" is wishing; "seeking" is searching; "searching" in its intensified form is searching again and again. The story of the monk who, from the very start, gave away whatever almsfood he received, here and there, to the children of various families, and in the end obtained milk-rice-gruel in return and went on his way, should be told here. "Questing," "hunting," and "pursuing" are simply further synonyms for "wanting," "seeking," and "searching" respectively. Thus should the construction of these terms be understood. This is the meaning of "deceit" and the rest.

Reading note — Ru-Yi

The two etymological images the text offers for why this vice is called "belittling" (nippesikatā) are worth holding side by side: a bamboo scraper wiping oil off skin, and a perfumer grinding fragrant matter down to extract its scent. Both images describe an abrasive, reductive process applied to something valuable — another person's good reputation — and both frame the belittler not as inventing lies from nothing but as wearing down something real until only the extractable "gain" is left. It is a strikingly physical, almost industrial way to describe gossip.

Notice, too, that the commentary simply names a story — "the story of the monk who gave away almsfood to children and got milk-rice-gruel in return" — without narrating it, exactly as it named the Telakandarika story earlier without telling it either. This is a real feature of how this commentarial tradition works: certain illustrative stories were common enough currency among the tradition's teachers and students that a bare pointer sufficed, the way a modern writer might say "you know the one about..." without repeating a well-known joke in full.

literally "eating others' back-flesh" — para (other) + piṭṭhi (back) + maṃsa (flesh); a vivid compound for talking behind someone's back, explained in the text itself via exactly this image.

Purification of Livelihood, Defined

The section closes by widening the frame one last time — pointing to the much longer list of wrong-livelihood practices catalogued in the Brahmajāla Sutta — before pulling every thread of the last several pages into one final definition, closed with the etymology promised all the way back at the chapter's opening.

Pali Source

Idāni evamādīnañca pāpadhammānanti ettha ādisaddena "yathā vā paneke bhonto samaṇabrāhmaṇā saddhādeyyāni bhojanāni bhuñjitvā te evarūpāya tiracchānavijjāya micchājīvena jīvikaṃ kappenti. Seyyathidaṃ, aṅgaṃ, nimittaṃ, uppātaṃ, supinaṃ, lakkhaṇaṃ, mūsikacchinnaṃ, aggihomaṃ, dabbihoma"nti (DN I.21) ādinā nayena brahmajāle vuttānaṃ anekesaṃ pāpadhammānaṃ gahaṇaṃ veditabbaṃ.

Iti yvāyaṃ imesaṃ ājīvahetu paññattānaṃ channaṃ sikkhāpadānaṃ vītikkamavasena, imesañca "kuhanā lapanā nemittikatā nippesikatā lābhena lābhaṃ nijigīsanatā"ti evamādīnaṃ pāpadhammānaṃ vasena pavatto micchājīvo, yā tasmā sabbappakārāpi micchājīvā virati, idaṃ ājīvapārisuddhisīlaṃ. Tatrāyaṃ vacanattho. Etaṃ āgamma jīvantīti ājīvo. Ko so, paccayapariyesanavāyāmo. Pārisuddhīti parisuddhatā. Ājīvassa pārisuddhi ājīvapārisuddhi.

Ru-Yi Translation

Now, as to "and such evil states as these" — here, by the word "and the rest," should be understood the inclusion of the many evil states spoken of in the Brahmajāla Sutta, by the method beginning: "Or, just as some ascetics and brahmins, having eaten food given out of faith, make their living by wrong livelihood through such base arts as these: namely, reading limbs, reading omens, portents, dreams, bodily marks, mouse-gnawings, fire-oblations, and ladle-oblations" (DN I.21).

Thus, whatever wrong livelihood occurs by way of transgressing these six training precepts laid down for the sake of livelihood, and whatever occurs by way of these evil states — deceit, talking for gain, hinting, belittling, and pursuing gain with gain, and the rest — the abstinence from wrong livelihood of every such kind: this is purification-of-livelihood virtue. Here is the verbal meaning: one lives by resorting to this — hence "livelihood." What is that? The effort of seeking requisites. "Purification" is the state of being purified. The purification of livelihood is "purification of livelihood."

Reading note — Ru-Yi

The Brahmajāla Sutta reference is a deliberate gesture outward: rather than reproduce that sutta's much longer catalogue of divination, omen-reading, and folk-magical practices in full, the chapter simply cites its opening items and says "and the rest," trusting the reader to know — or go look up — the fuller list. Combined with the bare story-pointers noted earlier, this section shows a commentary written for an audience assumed to already have the wider canon at their fingertips, rather than for a reader meeting these materials for the first time.

The closing etymology is characteristically spare after everything that came before it: "one lives by resorting to this" is offered as the entire justification for the word ājīva, "livelihood" — a reminder that for all the vivid stories along the way, this whole section has technically been in service of glossing a single compound word, ājīvapārisuddhisīla, one piece at a time.

Virtue Connected With the Requisites — The Robe

The fourth and final purification word-by-word glosses the reflection formula every monk is meant to recite before using each of the four requisites. This is the chapter's most quietly practical passage: the source, still recited today, for the standard reflection on the robe.

Pali Source

Yaṃ panetaṃ tadanantaraṃ paccayasannissitasīlaṃ vuttaṃ, tattha paṭisaṅkhā yonisoti upāyena pathena paṭisaṅkhāya ñatvā, paccavekkhitvāti attho. Ettha ca sītassa paṭighātāyātiādinā nayena vuttapaccavekkhaṇameva "yoniso paṭisaṅkhā"ti veditabbaṃ. Tattha cīvaranti antaravāsakādīsu yaṃkiñci. Paṭisevatīti paribhuñjati, nivāseti vā pārupati vā. Yāvadevāti payojanāvadhiparicchedaniyamavacanaṃ, ettakameva hi yogino cīvarapaṭisevane payojanaṃ yadidaṃ sītassa paṭighātāyātiādi, na ito bhiyyo. Sītassāti ajjhattadhātukkhobhavasena vā bahiddhāutupariṇāmanavasena vā uppannassa yassa kassaci sītassa. Paṭighātāyāti paṭihananatthaṃ. Yathā sarīre ābādhaṃ na uppādeti, evaṃ tassa vinodanatthaṃ. Sītabbhāhate hi sarīre vikkhittacitto yoniso padahituṃ na sakkoti, tasmā sītassa paṭighātāya cīvaraṃ paṭisevitabbanti bhagavā anuññāsi. Esa nayo sabbattha.

Kevalañhettha uṇhassāti aggisantāpassa. Tassa vanadāhādīsu sambhavo veditabbo. Ḍaṃsamakasavātātapasarīsapasamphassānanti ettha pana ḍaṃsāti ḍaṃsanamakkhikā, andhamakkhikātipi vuccanti. Makasā makasā eva. Vātāti sarajaarajādibhedā. Ātapoti sūriyātapo. Sarīsapāti ye keci sarantā gacchanti dīghajātikā sappādayo, tesaṃ daṭṭhasamphasso ca phuṭṭhasamphasso cāti duvidho samphasso, sopi cīvaraṃ pārupitvā nisinnaṃ na bādhati, tasmā tādisesu ṭhānesu tesaṃ paṭighātatthāya paṭisevati. Yāvadevāti puna etassa vacanaṃ niyatapayojanāvadhiparicchedadassanatthaṃ, hirikopīnapaṭicchādanañhi niyatapayojanaṃ, itarāni kadāci kadāci honti. Tattha hirikopīnanti taṃ taṃ sambādhaṭṭhānaṃ. Yasmiṃ yasmiñhi aṅge vivariyamāne hirī kuppati vinassati, taṃ taṃ hiriṃ kopanato hirikopīnanti vuccati. Tassa ca hirikopīnassa paṭicchādanatthanti hirikopīnapaṭicchādanatthaṃ. Hirikopīnaṃ paṭicchādanatthantipi pāṭho.

Ru-Yi Translation

And as to that virtue connected with the requisites, spoken of immediately following: herein, "reflecting wisely" means having reflected and understood by the right method, the right way — that is, having reviewed. And here, precisely the reviewing spoken of by the method beginning "for warding off cold" should be understood as "reflecting wisely." Herein, "robe" is any one of the inner garment and the rest. "He makes use of" means: he uses it — either wearing it as an inner garment, or draping it as an outer robe. "Only for the purpose of" is a phrase that fixes and limits the extent of the purpose: for the meditator's purpose in using the robe is exactly this much — warding off cold and the rest — and nothing more. "Of cold" means any cold whatsoever, arisen either by way of internal disturbance of the elements, or by way of external change of season. "For warding off" means: for the purpose of repelling it, in such a way that it does not produce affliction in the body. For when the body is assailed by cold, one whose mind is thereby distracted is unable to strive properly; therefore the Blessed One permitted that the robe be used for warding off cold. This is the method everywhere.

Only here: "of heat" means the heat of fire, whose occurrence should be understood as arising in such circumstances as forest fires. As to "the touch of gadflies, mosquitoes, wind, sun, and creeping things" — "gadflies" are biting flies, also called "blind flies." "Mosquitoes" are simply mosquitoes. "Wind" is of the kind distinguished as dusty or dustless, and so on. "Sun" is the heat of the sun. "Creeping things" are whatever long-bodied creatures move by crawling, such as snakes; contact with them is of two kinds — being bitten, and being merely touched — and even the latter does not trouble one who sits with the robe properly worn, so in such places he uses the robe for warding them off. "Only for the purpose of" is repeated here to show the boundary of a purpose that is constant: for covering the private parts is a constant purpose of the robe, whereas the other purposes arise only on occasion. Herein, "the private parts" is that particular concealed area of the body — for whatever limb, being exposed, disturbs one's sense of shame, is, on that account, called "the shame-disturber." "For the purpose of covering that shame-disturber" gives "for the purpose of covering the private parts." There is also a reading, "for the purpose of covering the private parts," directly.

Virtue Connected With the Requisites — Almsfood

The almsfood reflection is the longest and richest of the four: it names four wrong motives for eating by comparing a monk unfavorably to children, wrestlers, courtesans, and dancers; then works through what "old feeling" and "new feeling" mean; then closes with a monk-poet's own recipe for how much less than full to eat.

Ru-Yi Translation

"Almsfood" is any food whatsoever. Any food is called piṇḍapāta ("fall of lumps") because it falls into a monk's bowl by way of his almsround; or, piṇḍapāta is the gathering together of the scraps of food received from here and there. "Not for play" means: not for the sake of play, as village children eat — that is, not for the sake of amusement. "Not for intoxication" means: not for the sake of intoxication, as boxers and the like eat — that is, not for the conceit of physical strength or manly vigor. "Not for adornment" means: not for the sake of adornment, as palace women and courtesans eat — that is, not for a plump and rounded physique. "Not for beautification" means: not for beautification, as actors and dancers eat — that is, not for a clear and radiant complexion. And here: "not for play" is stated for abandoning a support for delusion; "not for intoxication," for abandoning a support for hatred; "not for adornment, not for beautification," for abandoning a support for lust. "Not for play, not for intoxication" is for preventing the arising of fetters in oneself; "not for adornment, not for beautification" is for preventing the arising of fetters in others as well. By these four together, the abandoning of both improper practice and devotion to sensual pleasure should be understood as expressed.

"Only for the purpose of" has the meaning already stated. "Of this body" means of this material body composed of the four great elements. "For its continuance" means for the purpose of its uninterrupted persistence. "For its sustenance" means for the unbroken continuation of its functioning, or for its lasting a long time. Just as the owner of a decaying house props it up with a support-post, and just as a cart-driver greases an axle, so too he uses almsfood for the body's continuance and sustenance, not for play, intoxication, adornment, or beautification. Moreover, "continuance" is a designation for the life faculty; therefore this phrase should also be understood as expressing the purpose of keeping the body's life faculty functioning. "For stopping discomfort": "discomfort" here means hunger, in the sense of an affliction; he uses almsfood to stop it, just as one applies ointment to a wound, or a remedy against heat and cold. "For assisting the holy life" means for the purpose of assisting both the holy life of the entire dispensation and the holy life of the path — for this practitioner, relying on the bodily strength gained from almsfood and practicing the threefold training in order to cross the wilderness of existence, uses almsfood to assist the holy life, just as travelers wishing to cross a wilderness might eat their own child's flesh out of sheer necessity, just as one wishing to cross a river uses a raft, and just as one wishing to cross the ocean uses a ship.

"Thus I shall dispel the old feeling, and not give rise to a new feeling": thinking in this way, he uses almsfood — "by this use of almsfood I shall dispel the old feeling, the feeling of hunger, and I shall not give rise to a new feeling caused by immoderate eating, like one of those brahmins — Āharahatthaka, Alaṃsāṭaka, Tatravaṭṭaka, or Kākamāsaka — who ate and then vomited." He uses it thus, just as a sick person uses medicine. Or else: whatever feeling arises now, in dependence on unsuitable or immoderate eating, by way of a condition rooted in past kamma, is called "old feeling." By suitable, moderate eating, destroying its condition, he thinks, "I shall dispel that old feeling." And whatever feeling will arise in the future, in dependence on the accumulation of kamma produced now through improper use of food, is called "new feeling." By way of proper use, not generating its root, he thinks, "I shall not give rise to that new feeling." The meaning here should be seen in this way too. By this much, it should be understood that proper use is comprised, the abandoning of devotion to self-mortification is shown, and the non-relinquishing of legitimate happiness is shown.

"And it will be sustenance for me": by moderate use, since there is then no danger of cutting off the life faculty or breaking down bodily function, sustenance — reckoned as a long continuance of life — will be mine, for this body whose functioning depends on requisites. With this thought too he uses it, just as one with a chronic illness uses its remedy. "And blamelessness, and living in comfort": blamelessness comes through avoiding improper seeking, receiving, and use of food; living in comfort comes through moderate use. Otherwise, from unsuitable or immoderate use come discontent, listlessness, and drowsiness. Or: blamelessness comes through the absence of faults such as the censure of the wise; living in comfort comes through the bodily strength arising from suitable, moderate eating. Or: blamelessness comes from avoiding eating that fills the belly to capacity — that is, from abandoning the pleasures of lying down, of the side, and of torpor; living in comfort comes from eating four or five mouthfuls less than one's fill, thereby producing a state of fitness for all four bodily postures. With this thought too, "blamelessness and living in comfort will be mine," he uses almsfood. For this was said —

"Leaving four or five mouthfuls unfinished, one should drink water — this is enough for the comfortable living of a monk who has applied himself." (Theragāthā 983)

By this much, it should be understood that the grasping of the purpose of eating, and the middle way itself, are both illustrated.

Reading note — Ru-Yi

The four rejected motives — play, intoxication, adornment, beautification — are each paired with a specific unflattering comparison (village children, wrestlers, palace women, dancers), and the text then re-sorts all four under the three roots of unwholesomeness at once: play under delusion, intoxication under hatred, adornment and beautification under lust. A single reflection formula is being shown to do triple duty against all three roots simultaneously, which is a good example of how densely this text expects a short canonical phrase to be unpacked.

The "four or five mouthfuls less" instruction, credited to a verse in the Theragāthā, is about as concrete and unmysterious as monastic advice gets — no metaphysics, just a specific, checkable habit, framed as sufficient on its own for physical comfort. It is a useful reminder that a text this dense with Abhidhamma analysis and etymological wordplay is still, at bottom, a practical manual, and is willing to end a highly abstract discussion of "old and new feeling" with a plain piece of advice about not eating quite to fullness.

Ru-Yi Translation

"Lodging" is both a resting place and a seat. Wherever one lies down — whether in a monastery, a vaulted building, or the like — that is a "resting place." Wherever one sits down, that is a "seat." Taking these together, it is called "lodging." As to "for the purpose of dispelling the danger of climate, and for the enjoyment of solitude": in the sense of oppressing the body, the climate itself is called "the danger of climate" — this covers both the purpose of dispelling that danger and the purpose of enjoying solitude. Whatever unsuitable climate causes bodily affliction or mental distraction is to be dispelled by the use of lodging; this is said for the purpose of dispelling it, and for the purpose of the happiness of solitude. Admittedly, the dispelling of the danger of climate was already mentioned, by way of warding off cold and the rest, in the case of the robe. But just as it was said there that covering the private parts is the constant purpose of the robe while the others arise only occasionally, so too here it should be understood that this is said with reference to the dispelling of climate-danger as a constant purpose of lodging. Or else: this climate, described in this way, is simply climate. But danger is twofold: manifest danger and hidden danger (Mahāniddesa 5). Herein, manifest danger is such things as lions and tigers; hidden danger is such things as lust and hatred. A monk who, having known and reviewed that a given lodging is one where these dangers do not arise — whether through lack of protection, or through exposure to unsuitable sights and the like — and who then makes use of it, should be understood as one who, reflecting wisely, uses lodging for the purpose of dispelling the danger of climate and enjoying solitude.

As to "requisite of medicine for the sick" — herein, in the sense of counteracting disease, that is, of acting as its opponent, it is called a "requisite." This is a designation for whatever is suitable as a remedy. It is "medicine" because it is the physician's prescribed treatment. The requisite for the sick that is medicine is "requisite-medicine for the sick" — that is, whatever is suitable for a sick person as the physician's prescribed treatment, such as oil, honey, or molasses. "Requisite" (parikkhāra), however, in such contexts as "it is well equipped with the seven requisites of a city" (AN 7.67), means an accompaniment. In such contexts as "the chariot has virtue as its requisite, jhāna as its axle, energy as its wheels" (SN 5.4), it means an ornament. In such contexts as "and these are the requisites of life that must be acquired by one who has gone forth" (MN I.191–192), it means a necessary provision. But here, both "necessary provision" and "accompaniment" are applicable — for that requisite-medicine for the sick is both an accompaniment of life and a necessary provision, in that it protects life by not giving an opening to the arising of a life-destroying affliction. Since it is thus a cause for life continuing for a long time, it is called a "requisite." Thus, being both the requisite-medicine for the sick and a requisite in the fuller sense, it is "the requisite of medicine for the sick" — meaning whatever is suitable for a sick person, prescribed by a physician, such as oil, honey, or molasses, serving as a requisite of life. "Arisen" means: born, come to be, produced. As to "connected with affliction": "affliction" here is a disturbance of the bodily elements, and such things as leprosy, boils, and pustules that arise from it; being arisen from affliction, these feelings are "connected with affliction." "Feelings" means painful feeling, the resultant feeling of unwholesome kamma, connected with such affliction. "For the maximum freedom from affliction" means for the state of being utterly without pain — the meaning being, until all that pain has been entirely abandoned.

Thus, in brief, this should be understood as virtue connected with the requisites, having as its characteristic the use of requisites after wise reflection. Here is the verbal meaning: since living beings, by making use of the robe and the other requisites in dependence on them, are able to proceed and carry on with life, they are therefore called "requisites" (paccaya). Virtue "connected with those requisites" is "virtue connected with the requisites."

Reading note — Ru-Yi

The distinction between "manifest danger" (a tiger) and "hidden danger" (lust, hatred) quietly reframes what a lodging is even for: choosing where to live is not just a matter of physical safety but of what sights and situations will or will not disturb the mind — the same logic already seen in restraint of the faculties, now applied to the choice of a dwelling rather than to what one looks at once there.

The three-way gloss of parikkhāra ("requisite") — accompaniment, ornament, necessary provision — drawn from three unrelated canonical contexts (a well-equipped city, a chariot built from virtues, and a monk's basic needs) is a small demonstration of the same method this whole chapter has used from its first paragraph: settling a word's technical meaning here by triangulating from how it is used elsewhere in the canon, rather than simply asserting a definition. And the final etymology — paccaya, "requisite," because living beings paṭicca, "in dependence on" it, carry on — closes the entire four-purifications section by returning to exactly the kind of sound-based etymology (sīlana for sīla, pāti-mokkheti for Pātimokkha) that has structured this chapter's method from its very first page.
