Chapter 1 · Sīlaniddesa · In progress

What Is Virtue?

This page carries Ru-Yi's translation of the Visuddhimagga's chapter 1, Sīlaniddesa ("The Description of Virtue") — the seven-question matrix that structures the whole chapter, the complete answers to its first four questions (what virtue is, in what sense it is virtue, its characteristic/function/manifestation/proximate cause, and its benefits), and, for its fifth question, the full list of classification schemes with the onefold, twofold, and threefold schemes explained. The rest is translated in the sessions ahead and will be added to this same page.

Status: in progress — Q1–4 answered; Q5 (kinds) onefold–threefold done Translator: Ru-Yi Meditation Center Source: Vism I, Sīlaniddesa §6 (VRI/CST numbering) Author: Bhadantācariya Buddhaghosa, 5th c. CE

Translation Method

The Pāli source is the Chaṭṭha Saṅgāyana (Sixth Council) romanized edition maintained by the Vipassana Research Institute (VRI, tipitaka.org), cross-checked against the Pali Text Society's Rhys Davids edition (1920–21) as digitized on GRETIL — used here only to verify readings, not copied, since that particular digital file carries its own CC BY-SA licence from PTS and the Dhammakaya Foundation. The VRI text itself is used under its stated terms: freely available for non-commercial use, with attribution to the Vipassana Research Institute.

The English translation is Ru-Yi's own, made directly from the Pāli. No existing English translation of the Visuddhimagga — including Bhikkhu Ñāṇamoli's copyrighted The Path of Purification — was consulted, paraphrased, or used as a structural guide for this wording. Pāli technical terms that are standard scholarly convention (sīla, saṅgāhaka, sīlaniddesa, and the like) are rendered or left untranslated following ordinary usage; this does not draw on any single translator's specific choices.

Reading notes are grounded only in the passage's own internal citations — to the Paṭisambhidāmagga, the Vibhaṅga, the Dīgha Nikāya, the Majjhima Nikāya, the Aṅguttara Nikāya, and the Suttanipāta — and in the general, uncontested convention of the Pāli commentarial (aṭṭhakathā) genre. They do not draw on Ñāṇamoli's or any other modern scholar's introduction, footnotes, or interpretive framing. Each translated passage also carries a short vocabulary list for readers learning Pāli alongside the text.

The Seven Questions

The chapter opens by admitting that virtue, concentration, and understanding — however comprehensive as a threefold outline — have so far been announced only in the barest summary. It then poses the seven questions that the rest of the chapter answers in turn, taking up virtue first.

Pali Source

Evaṃ anekaguṇasaṅgāhakena sīlasamādhipaññāmukhena desitopi panesa visuddhimaggo atisaṅkhepadesitoyeva hoti. Tasmā nālaṃ sabbesaṃ upakārāyāti vitthāramassa dassetuṃ sīlaṃ tāva ārabbha idaṃ pañhākammaṃ hoti.

Kiṃ sīlaṃ, kenaṭṭhena sīlaṃ, kānassa lakkhaṇarasapaccupaṭṭhānapadaṭṭhānāni, kimānisaṃsaṃ sīlaṃ, katividhaṃ cetaṃ sīlaṃ, ko cassa saṃkileso, kiṃ vodānanti.

Ru-Yi Translation

Thus, although this Path of Purification has been taught by way of virtue, concentration, and understanding — a division that gathers in many good qualities — it has still been taught only in the barest outline. Therefore, thinking, "this will not suffice to help everyone," in order to show its elaboration, this question-and-answer treatment now begins, taking up virtue first.

What is virtue? In what sense is it virtue? What are its characteristic, function, manifestation, and proximate cause? What are the benefits of virtue? How many kinds does this virtue have? What is its defilement? And what is its cleansing?

Key vocabulary — for Pāli learners
sīlant.

virtue, moral conduct — the whole chapter's subject. Same word can mean "nature, habit" more generally; "virtue" is what one's habitual conduct has become.

lakkhaṇa · rasa · paccupaṭṭhāna · padaṭṭhāna

"characteristic, function, manifestation, proximate cause" — a fixed quartet of questions Abhidhamma asks about almost every phenomenon it defines. Worth learning as a set now; it recurs constantly.

ānisaṃsam.

benefit, advantage, reward.

saṅkilesa / vodāna

a paired opposite you'll see often: "defilement" vs. "cleansing, purification."

-ādi

not a standalone word but a very common suffix meaning "and so on, etc." — attached to the last item of an implied list (as in pāṇātipātādīhi, "from killing living beings, etc.").

What Is Virtue? — Volition and the Mental Factor

The chapter answers only the first question here, citing the Paṭisambhidāmagga's own fourfold analysis of virtue, then glossing each of its first two terms in turn.

Pali Source

Tatridaṃ vissajjanaṃ. Kiṃ sīlanti pāṇātipātādīhi vā viramantassa vattapaṭipattiṃ vā pūrentassa cetanādayo dhammā. Vuttañhetaṃ paṭisambhidāyaṃ "kiṃ sīlanti cetanā sīlaṃ, cetasikaṃ sīlaṃ, saṃvaro sīlaṃ, avītikkamo sīla"nti (Paṭis I.39).

Tattha cetanā sīlaṃ nāma pāṇātipātādīhi vā viramantassa vattapaṭipattiṃ vā pūrentassa cetanā. Cetasikaṃ sīlaṃ nāma pāṇātipātādīhi viramantassa virati. Apica cetanā sīlaṃ nāma pāṇātipātādīni pajahantassa satta kammapathacetanā. Cetasikaṃ sīlaṃ nāma "abhijjhaṃ pahāya vigatābhijjhena cetasā viharatī"ti (DN I.217) ādinā nayena vuttā anabhijjhābyāpādasammādiṭṭhidhammā.

Ru-Yi Translation

Here now is the answer. As to "what is virtue" — it is the states beginning with volition, in one who abstains from killing living beings and so on, or who fulfills the observance of duties. For this was said in the Paṭisambhidā: "What is virtue? Volition is virtue, the mental factor is virtue, restraint is virtue, non-transgression is virtue" (Paṭis I.39).

Herein, "volition is virtue" means the volition in one who abstains from killing living beings and so on, or who fulfills the observance of duties. "The mental factor is virtue" means the abstinence in one who abstains from killing living beings and so on. And further, "volition is virtue" also means the seven volitions of the courses of action, in one who abandons killing living beings and the rest. "The mental factor is virtue" also means the states of non-covetousness, non-ill-will, and right view, spoken of by the method beginning, "having abandoned covetousness, he dwells with a mind free from covetousness" (DN I.217).

Key vocabulary — for Pāli learners
cetanāf.

volition, intention — from ceteti, "to intend." One of Abhidhamma's most load-bearing terms: it is cetanā that later theory treats as what actually makes an act count as kamma.

cetasikant./adj.

"belonging to citta (mind)" — a mental factor that arises together with a moment of consciousness.

kammapatham.

"course of action" — the classic set of bodily and verbal deeds (here, the seven unwholesome ones being abandoned).

avītikkamam.

non-transgression — a- (not) + vīti-kkama ("stepping across/beyond"). The fourth of the chapter's fourfold definition of virtue.

What Is Virtue? — The Fivefold Restraint, and Non-transgression

The chapter turns to the third term, restraint, dividing it into five kinds and supporting each with a separate canonical citation, before closing with the fourth term and a summary line.

Pali Source

Saṃvaro sīlanti ettha pañcavidhena saṃvaro veditabbo — pātimokkhasaṃvaro, satisaṃvaro, ñāṇasaṃvaro, khantisaṃvaro, vīriyasaṃvaroti. Tattha "iminā pātimokkhasaṃvarena upeto hoti samupeto"ti (Vibh. 511) ayaṃ pātimokkhasaṃvaro. "Rakkhati cakkhundriyaṃ, cakkhundriye saṃvaraṃ āpajjatī"ti (DN I.213) ayaṃ satisaṃvaro.

Yāni sotāni lokasmiṃ, (ajitāti bhagavā;)
sati tesaṃ nivāraṇaṃ;
sotānaṃ saṃvaraṃ brūmi, paññāyete pidhiyyareti. (Sn 1041)

Ayaṃ ñāṇasaṃvaro. Paccayapaṭisevanampi ettheva samodhānaṃ gacchati. Yo panāyaṃ khamo hoti sītassa uṇhassātiādinā (MN I.24; AN 6.58) nayena āgato, ayaṃ khantisaṃvaro nāma. Yo cāyaṃ uppannaṃ kāmavitakkaṃ nādhivāsetītiādinā (MN I.26; AN 6.58) nayena āgato, ayaṃ vīriyasaṃvaro nāma. Ājīvapārisuddhipi ettheva samodhānaṃ gacchati.

Iti ayaṃ pañcavidhopi saṃvaro, yā ca pāpabhīrukānaṃ kulaputtānaṃ sampattavatthuto virati, sabbampetaṃ saṃvarasīlanti veditabbaṃ. Avītikkamo sīlanti samādinnasīlassa kāyikavācasiko anatikkamo. Idaṃ tāva kiṃ sīlanti pañhassa vissajjanaṃ.

Ru-Yi Translation

As to "restraint is virtue" — here, restraint should be understood as fivefold: restraint by the Pātimokkha, restraint by mindfulness, restraint by knowledge, restraint by patience, and restraint by energy. Herein, "he is possessed, fully possessed, of this restraint by the Pātimokkha" (Vibh. 511) — this is restraint by the Pātimokkha. "He guards the eye faculty; he enters upon restraint of the eye faculty" (DN I.213) — this is restraint by mindfulness.

"Whatever streams there are in the world" — Ajita, said the Blessed One — "mindfulness is their restraining; the restraint of the streams I declare; by understanding these are obstructed." (Sn 1041)

This is restraint by knowledge. The proper use of the requisites is included here as well. And this — one who is patient, who has come by the method beginning, "enduring cold, enduring heat..." (MN I.24; AN 6.58) — this is called restraint by patience. And this — one who does not tolerate an arisen sensual thought, who has come by the same method (MN I.26; AN 6.58) — this is called restraint by energy. Purity of livelihood is included here as well.

Thus this fivefold restraint, together with whatever abstinence there is — on the part of clansmen who fear wrongdoing — from an occasion for transgression that has presented itself: all this should be understood as restraint-virtue. As to "non-transgression is virtue" — it is the bodily and verbal non-transgression on the part of one who has undertaken the precepts of virtue. This, for now, is the answer to the question "what is virtue."

Reading note — Ru-Yi

Notice the method, not just the content: rather than argue for his own definition of "virtue," Buddhaghosa builds it almost entirely out of citations. The fourfold analysis itself — volition, mental factor, restraint, non-transgression — is quoted verbatim from the Paṭisambhidāmagga, a source the chapter expects its reader to already recognize as settled canonical authority. Each of the five kinds of restraint is then anchored to a separate scriptural passage: the Vibhaṅga for restraint by the code of monastic rules, the Dīgha Nikāya for restraint by mindfulness at the sense doors, the Suttanipāta's verse to the wanderer Ajita for restraint by knowledge, and stock formulas shared by the Majjhima and Aṅguttara Nikāyas for restraint by patience and by energy. None of these five sources originally presents itself as part of a fivefold scheme — each simply describes one kind of restraint on its own. The fivefold saṃvara taught here is Buddhaghosa's synthesis of five independent canonical passages under a single interpretive heading, which is the recurring method of the Pāli commentarial genre generally: authority accumulates through the density and precision of citation, not through claims to originality.

Key vocabulary — for Pāli learners
saṃvaram.

restraint, control — saṃ- (together/thoroughly) + vṛ ("to cover, hold back"). This whole section is Buddhaghosa dividing this one word into five kinds.

pātimokkhant.

the code of monastic training rules recited fortnightly by the Saṅgha.

khantif.

patience, forbearance, endurance.

vīriyant.

energy, effort, exertion — one of the five spiritual faculties (indriya) elsewhere in the text.

ājīvam.

livelihood — as in sammā-ājīva, "right livelihood," the fifth factor of the Noble Eightfold Path.

In What Sense Is It Virtue?

The chapter turns to the second question, tracing "sīla" to a verbal root meaning "to compose, to conduct oneself," and noting that grammarians recognize two related senses of that root.

Pali Source

Avasesesu kenaṭṭhena sīlanti sīlanaṭṭhena sīlaṃ. Kimidaṃ sīlanaṃ nāma. Samādhānaṃ vā, kāyakammādīnaṃ susīlyavasena avippakiṇṇatāti attho. Upadhāraṇaṃ vā, kusalānaṃ dhammānaṃ patiṭṭhānavasena ādhārabhāvoti attho. Etadeva hettha atthadvayaṃ saddalakkhaṇavidū anujānanti. Aññe pana siraṭṭho sīlattho, sītalaṭṭho sīlatthoti evamādināpi nayenettha atthaṃ vaṇṇayanti.

Ru-Yi Translation

As to "in what sense is it virtue" — among the remaining questions — it is virtue in the sense of "composing" (sīlana). What, then, is this "composing"? Either it is a coordinating, meaning the non-scatteredness of bodily action and the rest, by way of their being well-disciplined; or it is a supporting, meaning the state of being a foundation, by way of grounding wholesome states. This twofold meaning alone is what those skilled in grammar accept here. Others, however, explain the meaning by yet other methods — such as, the meaning of sīla is "head" (foremost), or the meaning of sīla is "coolness."

Key vocabulary — for Pāli learners
sīlanant.

"composing, conducting oneself" — the abstract noun the chapter derives sīla from. A classic example of Pāli commentarial etymology: explaining a word by tracing it to a verbal root, not by citing a dictionary.

samādhānant.

coordinating, composing together. Note for learners: this looks like samādhi (concentration) but is a different derivative — a common false-friend trap in Pāli compounds.

patiṭṭhā / patiṭṭhāna

foundation, footing, support — the same root behind sīle patiṭṭhāya, "established in virtue," from the chapter's opening verse.

Characteristic, Function, Manifestation, Proximate Cause

The chapter now delivers on the third question from its opening matrix, taking each of the four terms in turn — each introduced by a verse, then unpacked in prose.

Pali Source

Idāni kānassa lakkhaṇarasapaccupaṭṭhānapadaṭṭhānānīti ettha –

Sīlanaṃ lakkhaṇaṃ tassa, bhinnassāpi anekadhā;
sanidassanattaṃ rūpassa, yathā bhinnassanekadhā.

Yathā hi nīlapītādibhedena anekadhā bhinnassāpi rūpāyatanassa sanidassanattaṃ lakkhaṇaṃ, nīlādibhedena bhinnassāpi sanidassanabhāvānatikkamanato. Tathā sīlassa cetanādibhedena anekadhā bhinnassāpi yadetaṃ kāyakammādīnaṃ samādhānavasena kusalānañca dhammānaṃ patiṭṭhānavasena vuttaṃ sīlanaṃ, tadeva lakkhaṇaṃ, cetanādibhedena bhinnassāpi samādhānapatiṭṭhānabhāvānatikkamanato. Evaṃ lakkhaṇassa panassa –

Dussīlyaviddhaṃsanatā, anavajjaguṇo tathā;
kiccasampattiatthena, raso nāma pavuccati.

Tasmā idaṃ sīlaṃ nāma kiccaṭṭhena rasena dussīlyaviddhaṃsanarasaṃ, sampattiatthena rasena anavajjarasanti veditabbaṃ. Lakkhaṇādīsu hi kiccameva sampatti vā rasoti vuccati.

Soceyyapaccupaṭṭhānaṃ, tayidaṃ tassa viññuhi;
ottappañca hirī ceva, padaṭṭhānanti vaṇṇitaṃ.

Tayidaṃ sīlaṃ kāyasoceyyaṃ vacīsoceyyaṃ manosoceyyanti (AN 3.121) evaṃ vuttasoceyyapaccupaṭṭhānaṃ, soceyyabhāvena paccupaṭṭhāti gahaṇabhāvaṃ gacchati. Hirottappañca panassa viññūhi padaṭṭhānanti vaṇṇitaṃ, āsannakāraṇanti attho. Hirottappe hi sati sīlaṃ uppajjati ceva tiṭṭhati ca. Asati neva uppajjati, na tiṭṭhatīti. Evaṃ sīlassa lakkhaṇarasapaccupaṭṭhānapadaṭṭhānāni veditabbāni.

Ru-Yi Translation

Now, as to "what are its characteristic, function, manifestation, and proximate cause" —

Composing is its characteristic, even though divided in many ways — just as visibility is the characteristic of visible form, though that too is divided in many ways.

For just as visibility is the characteristic of the visible-form base, even though that base is divided in many ways by the distinction of blue, yellow, and the rest — because, however divided, it never goes beyond the state of being visible — so too with virtue: even though divided in many ways by the distinction of volition and the rest, that very "composing," spoken of as the coordinating of bodily action and the rest and as the grounding of wholesome states, is its characteristic — because, however divided, it never goes beyond the state of coordinating and grounding. Its characteristic having been explained thus —

The destruction of bad conduct, and likewise its flawless quality, in the sense of function and of achievement — this is called its function.

Therefore this virtue should be understood: in the sense of function, its function is the destruction of bad conduct; in the sense of achievement, its function is flawlessness. For among characteristic and the rest, it is precisely the function, or the achievement, that is called the "function."

Purity is declared by the wise to be its manifestation; and shame and moral dread are said to be its proximate cause.

This virtue has as its manifestation the purity spoken of thus: "bodily purity, verbal purity, mental purity" (AN 3.121) — it comes to be apprehended in the state of purity, as its manifestation. And shame and moral dread are declared by the wise to be its proximate cause, meaning its immediate cause. For when shame and moral dread are present, virtue arises and endures; when they are absent, it neither arises nor endures. Thus should the characteristic, function, manifestation, and proximate cause of virtue be understood.

Reading note — Ru-Yi

This is the chapter making good on a promise from its own opening matrix, and it does so with the standard Abhidhamma move of anchoring an abstract "characteristic" to a concrete, familiar case: visible form (rūpāyatana) stays visible-form no matter how many colors it divides into, so a characteristic can be single even while its bearer multiplies — the same reasoning is then applied, unchanged, to virtue. Watch for this exact pattern of "characteristic, function, manifestation, proximate cause," each introduced by verse and unpacked in prose, to recur for essentially every phenomenon the rest of this text goes on to define.

Naming shame (hirī) and moral dread (ottappa) as virtue's proximate cause is not incidental. Elsewhere in the canon this pair is called the "two guardians of the world" (lokapāladhamma) — the passage is quietly asserting that virtue does not sustain itself; it depends on these two more basic qualities to arise and to endure at all, exactly as the closing line states outright.

Key vocabulary — for Pāli learners
rūpāyatanant.

"the visible-form base" — one of the twelve āyatanas (sense bases) in Abhidhamma; used here purely as a familiar comparison case, not as this passage's real subject.

hirī / ottappa

a fixed pair: moral shame (recoiling from wrongdoing out of self-respect) and moral dread (fearing wrongdoing's consequences). Almost always cited together, never one without the other.

soceyyant.

purity, cleanliness — from suci, "clean, pure."

The Benefits of Virtue

The fourth question turns from analysis to inspiration: a canonical prose passage on the five benefits a virtuous householder enjoys, followed by ten verses in praise of virtue unlike anything so far in this chapter.

Pali Source

Kimānisaṃsaṃ sīlanti avippaṭisārādianekaguṇapaṭilābhānisaṃsaṃ. Vuttañhetaṃ — "avippaṭisāratthāni kho, ānanda, kusalāni sīlāni avippaṭisārānisaṃsānī"ti (AN 11.1).

Aparampi vuttaṃ "pañcime gahapatayo ānisaṃsā sīlavato sīlasampadāya. Katame pañca? Idha gahapatayo sīlavā sīlasampanno appamādādhikaraṇaṃ mahantaṃ bhogakkhandhaṃ adhigacchati, ayaṃ paṭhamo ānisaṃso sīlavato sīlasampadāya. Puna caparaṃ gahapatayo sīlavato sīlasampannassa kalyāṇo kittisaddo abbhuggacchati, ayaṃ dutiyo ānisaṃso sīlavato sīlasampadāya. Puna caparaṃ gahapatayo sīlavā sīlasampanno yaññadeva parisaṃ upasaṅkamati yadi khattiyaparisaṃ yadi brāhmaṇaparisaṃ yadi gahapatiparisaṃ yadi samaṇaparisaṃ, visārado upasaṅkamati amaṅkubhūto, ayaṃ tatiyo ānisaṃso sīlavato sīlasampadāya. Puna caparaṃ gahapatayo sīlavā sīlasampanno asammūḷho kālaṃ karoti, ayaṃ catuttho ānisaṃso sīlavato sīlasampadāya. Puna caparaṃ gahapatayo sīlavā sīlasampanno kāyassa bhedā paraṃ maraṇā sugatiṃ saggaṃ lokaṃ upapajjati, ayaṃ pañcamo ānisaṃso sīlavato sīlasampadāyā"ti (DN II.150; AN 5.213; Mahāvagga 285).

Aparepi "ākaṅkheyya ce, bhikkhave, bhikkhu sabrahmacārīnaṃ piyo ca assaṃ manāpo ca garu ca bhāvanīyo cāti, sīlesvevassa paripūrakārī"tiādinā (MN I.65) nayena piyamanāpatādayo āsavakkhayapariyosānā anekā sīlānisaṃsā vuttā. Evaṃ avippaṭisārādianekaguṇānisaṃsaṃ sīlaṃ. Apica —

Sāsane kulaputtānaṃ, patiṭṭhā natthi yaṃ vinā;
ānisaṃsaparicchedaṃ, tassa sīlassa ko vade.

Na gaṅgā yamunā cāpi, sarabhū vā sarasvatī;
ninnagā vāciravatī, mahī vāpi mahānadī.
Sakkuṇanti visodhetuṃ, taṃ malaṃ idha pāṇinaṃ;
visodhayati sattānaṃ, yaṃ ve sīlajalaṃ malaṃ.

Na taṃ sajaladā vātā, na cāpi haricandanaṃ;
neva hārā na maṇayo, na candakiraṇaṅkurā.
Samayantīdha sattānaṃ, pariḷāhaṃ surakkhitaṃ;
yaṃ sameti idaṃ ariyaṃ, sīlaṃ accantasītalaṃ.

Sīlagandhasamo gandho, kuto nāma bhavissati;
yo samaṃ anuvāte ca, paṭivāte ca vāyati.

Saggārohaṇasopānaṃ, aññaṃ sīlasamaṃ kuto;
dvāraṃ vā pana nibbāna-nagarassa pavesane.

Sobhantevaṃ na rājāno, muttāmaṇivibhūsitā;
yathā sobhanti yatino, sīlabhūsanabhūsitā.

Attānuvādādibhayaṃ, viddhaṃsayati sabbaso;
janeti kittihāsañca, sīlaṃ sīlavataṃ sadā.

Guṇānaṃ mūlabhūtassa, dosānaṃ balaghātino;
iti sīlassa viññeyyaṃ, ānisaṃsakathāmukhanti.

Ru-Yi Translation

As to "what are the benefits of virtue" — its benefit is the attainment of many good qualities, beginning with freedom from remorse. For this was said: "Wholesome virtues, Ānanda, have freedom from remorse as their purpose, freedom from remorse as their benefit" (AN 11.1).

It was also further said: "Householders, there are these five benefits for one who is virtuous, through the accomplishment of virtue. What five? Here, householders, one who is virtuous, accomplished in virtue, gains a great mass of wealth through diligence — this is the first benefit for one who is virtuous, through the accomplishment of virtue. Further, a good reputation spreads about one who is virtuous, accomplished in virtue — this is the second benefit. Further, one who is virtuous, accomplished in virtue, approaches any assembly whatsoever — whether of nobles, of brahmins, of householders, or of ascetics — confidently and without embarrassment — this is the third benefit. Further, one who is virtuous, accomplished in virtue, dies unconfused — this is the fourth benefit. Further, one who is virtuous, accomplished in virtue, on the breakup of the body, after death, is reborn in a good destination, in a heavenly world — this is the fifth benefit for one who is virtuous, through the accomplishment of virtue" (DN II.150; AN 5.213; Mahāvagga 285).

By the method beginning, "If, monks, a monk should wish: 'May I be dear and pleasing to my companions in the holy life, respected and esteemed by them' — let him then be one who fulfills the precepts of virtue" (MN I.65), many more benefits of virtue are stated — being dear and pleasing, and so on, culminating in the destruction of the taints. Thus virtue has as its benefit the attainment of many good qualities, beginning with freedom from remorse. Moreover —

Without it there is no foundation at all for clansmen in this Dispensation — who, then, could declare the full extent of that virtue's benefits?

Not the Ganges, nor the Yamunā, not the Sarabhū, nor the Sarasvatī, not the river Aciravatī, nor the Mahī, nor any great river, can cleanse that stain here in living beings — it is the water of virtue alone that truly cleanses beings of that stain.

Not those moisture-laden breezes, nor sandalwood, nor garlands, nor gems, nor the young rays of the moon, can allay here the deep-seated fever of beings — it is this noble virtue, utterly cool, that allays it.

Where indeed could there be a fragrance to equal the fragrance of virtue — which blows equally with the wind, and against it?

Where else is there another staircase for the ascent to heaven equal to virtue — or a gateway for entering the city of nibbāna?

Kings adorned with pearls and gems do not shine as the self-controlled shine, adorned with the ornament of virtue.

Virtue always destroys entirely the fear that begins with self-reproach, for the virtuous, and always gives rise to renown and joy.

Thus should be known this introductory account of the benefits of virtue — the root of all good qualities, and the destroyer of the very strength of faults.

Reading note — Ru-Yi

Notice the shift in register. Every question so far has been answered in flat analytical prose, but "benefits" pulls in a canonical citation repeated nearly word for word across the Dīgha, Aṅguttara, and the Vinaya's Mahāvagga alike — evidence of how standardized this five-item list already was, long before Buddhaghosa assembled this chapter — and then opens into ten couplets of devotional verse unlike anything preceding it. Pāli commentarial prose does this often: definitional questions get flat analysis, but a question about a thing's worth gets verse, as though the genre itself is marking that this part is meant to be felt, not only parsed.

The image of virtue's fragrance traveling "equally with the wind, and against it" trades on an implicit contrast with ordinary scent, which only carries downwind — the verse never spells this out, assuming the reader already knows the comparison it is quietly overturning.

How Many Kinds of Virtue? — The Full List

The chapter's fifth question opens with a rapid-fire list naming every classification scheme it is about to explain — from onefold up through fivefold — before explaining even one of them. This "name them all first, explain them one by one after" structure is itself the method the chapter's own title announces: niddesa means "detailed exposition," and this is the exposition unfolding exactly as promised.

Pali Source

Idāni yaṃ vuttaṃ katividhaṃ cetaṃ sīlanti, tatridaṃ vissajjanaṃ. Sabbameva tāva idaṃ sīlaṃ attano sīlanalakkhaṇena ekavidhaṃ.

Ru-Yi Translation

Now, as to what was said — "and how many kinds does this virtue have" — here is the answer. First of all, this virtue in its entirety is onefold by way of its own characteristic of "composing."

It is twofold by way of performance and avoidance; likewise by way of good-conduct virtue and fundamentals-of-the-holy-life virtue, by way of abstinence and non-abstinence, by way of dependent and independent, by way of time-limited and life-long, by way of limited and unlimited, and by way of mundane and supramundane.

It is threefold by way of inferior, medium, and superior; likewise by way of self-dominated, world-dominated, and Dhamma-dominated; by way of misapprehended, unmisapprehended, and tranquilized; by way of purified, unpurified, and doubtful; and by way of the trainee's, the adept's, and neither-trainee's-nor-adept's.

It is fourfold by way of that which partakes of decline, that which partakes of stability, that which partakes of distinction, and that which partakes of penetration; likewise by way of the virtue of monks, of nuns, of the not-fully-ordained, and of householders; by way of virtue that is natural, customary, a matter of one's nature, or caused by a prior condition; and by way of restraint by the Pātimokkha, restraint of the faculties, purification of livelihood, and virtue connected with the requisites.

It is fivefold by way of limited-purification virtue and the rest. For this was said in the Paṭisambhidā: "There are five virtues: limited-purification virtue, unlimited-purification virtue, fulfilled-purification virtue, unmisapprehended-purification virtue, tranquilized-purification virtue" (Paṭis I.37). Likewise by way of abandoning, abstinence, volition, restraint, and non-transgression.

The Twofold Schemes, Explained

Having listed all seven twofold schemes at once, the chapter now works through each in turn — performance and avoidance, good conduct and the fundamentals of the holy life, abstinence and non-abstinence, dependence and independence, time-limited and life-long, limited and unlimited, and finally mundane and supramundane.

Ru-Yi Translation

Herein, in the case of the onefold division, the meaning should be understood by the method already stated. In the case of the twofold division: whatever fulfillment of a training precept laid down by the Blessed One as "this should be done" — that is performance. Whatever non-doing of what has been prohibited as "this should not be done" — that is avoidance. Here is the verbal meaning of these terms: "performance" is so called because through it people conduct themselves, proceeding by way of fulfilling the training precepts; "avoidance" is so called because by means of it people protect and guard what has been forbidden. Performance is accomplished by both faith and energy; avoidance is accomplished by faith alone. Thus it is twofold by way of performance and avoidance.

In the second pair: "good conduct" means the highest proper conduct. Good conduct itself, as an adjective, is "pertaining to good conduct"; or, what is laid down with reference to good conduct is "pertaining to good conduct" — a designation for the remaining virtue apart from the eightfold livelihood-precept set. "Being the starting point of the path that constitutes the holy life" is "fundamentals of the holy life" — a designation for the eightfold livelihood-precept virtue itself, since that virtue is the starting point of the path and must be purified at the very outset. Hence it is said: "for his bodily action, verbal action, and livelihood are already, beforehand, thoroughly purified" (MN III.431). Or again: whatever training precepts are called "minor and lesser-minor" is good-conduct virtue; the remainder is fundamentals-of-the-holy-life virtue. Or: what is included in the twofold monastic-code analysis is fundamentals-of-the-holy-life virtue, while what is included in the Khandhaka observances is good-conduct virtue — and through the accomplishment of the latter, the former succeeds. Hence it is said: "monks, that a monk should fulfill the fundamentals-of-the-holy-life state without having fulfilled the good-conduct state — this is not possible" (AN 5.21). Thus it is twofold by way of good-conduct virtue and fundamentals-of-the-holy-life virtue.

In the third pair: mere abstinence from killing living beings and the rest is abstinence-virtue. The remainder — volition and so on — is non-abstinence-virtue. Thus it is twofold by way of abstinence and non-abstinence.

In the fourth pair: "dependence" means there are two dependences — dependence on craving and dependence on views. Whatever is engaged in by one wishing for a favorable rebirth, thinking, "by this virtue may I become a god, or one among the gods" (DN III.320; MN I.186; AN 5.206, 7.50) — this is dependent on craving. Whatever is engaged in through the view that "purity comes by virtue alone" — this is dependent on views. But whatever is supramundane, together with the mundane virtue that serves as its requisite, is independent. Thus it is twofold by way of dependent and independent.

In the fifth pair: virtue undertaken after fixing a determination of time is time-limited. That undertaken and thereafter maintained for life is life-long. Thus it is twofold by way of time-limited and life-long.

In the sixth pair: virtue whose limit is determined by gain, fame, relatives, limb, or life is called "limited"; the opposite is "unlimited." And this was said in the Paṭisambhidā: "What is that virtue which is limited? There is virtue limited by gain, virtue limited by fame, virtue limited by relatives, virtue limited by limb, virtue limited by life. What is that virtue limited by gain? Here someone, for the sake of gain, because of gain, for the reason of gain, transgresses a training precept as undertaken — this is that virtue limited by gain" (Paṭis I.38). By this same method the others should be elaborated. And in the answer concerning the unlimited, too, it was said: "What is that virtue not limited by gain? Here someone, for the sake of gain, because of gain, for the reason of gain, does not even give rise to a thought of transgressing a training precept as undertaken — how much less would he transgress it! This is that virtue not limited by gain" (Paṭis I.38). By this same method the others should be elaborated. Thus it is twofold by way of limited and unlimited.

In the seventh pair: all virtue that is subject to the taints is mundane; that free of the taints is supramundane. Herein, the mundane brings about distinction of existence, and also serves as a requisite for escape from existence. As it is said: "The discipline is for the purpose of restraint; restraint is for the purpose of freedom from remorse; freedom from remorse is for the purpose of gladness; gladness is for the purpose of joy; joy is for the purpose of tranquility; tranquility is for the purpose of pleasure; pleasure is for the purpose of concentration; concentration is for the purpose of the knowledge and vision of things as they really are; the knowledge and vision of things as they really are is for the purpose of disenchantment; disenchantment is for the purpose of dispassion; dispassion is for the purpose of liberation; liberation is for the purpose of the knowledge and vision of liberation; the knowledge and vision of liberation is for the purpose of final nibbāna through non-clinging. For this purpose is the talk, for this purpose is the counsel, for this purpose is the support, for this purpose is the lending of ear — namely, the liberation of the mind through non-clinging" (Paṭis II.366). The supramundane brings about escape from existence, and is also the ground for reviewing knowledge. Thus it is twofold by way of mundane and supramundane.

Reading note — Ru-Yi

The seventh pair's chain — discipline to restraint to freedom from remorse to gladness to joy to tranquility to pleasure to concentration to knowledge-and-vision to disenchantment to dispassion to liberation — is worth reading slowly: each link is stated as existing for the sake of the next, a single argument that virtue is not an end in itself but the first domino in a causal sequence ending in liberation. The chapter is answering "why bother with the mundane kind of virtue at all?" by embedding sīla in a chain it does not control the end of.

The Threefold Schemes, Explained

Six more threefold schemes follow the same pattern — each restarts from scratch with its own basis of division, even where it reuses labels (like "inferior, medium, superior") from a scheme already given.

Ru-Yi Translation

Among the triads: in the first triad, virtue brought about by inferior desire, mind, energy, or investigation is inferior; that brought about by medium desire and the rest is medium; by superior, superior. Or: virtue undertaken out of desire for fame is inferior; out of desire for the fruit of merit, medium; undertaken thinking simply "this is to be done," in dependence on the noble state, is superior. Or: virtue tainted by such self-exaltation and disparagement of others, thinking, "I am accomplished in virtue, but these other monks are of bad conduct, of evil nature," is inferior; untainted, mundane virtue is medium; supramundane virtue is superior. Or: virtue engaged in, by the power of craving, for the sake of future existence and enjoyment, is inferior; that engaged in for the sake of one's own liberation is medium; that engaged in for the sake of the liberation of all beings — the virtue of the perfections — is superior. Thus it is threefold by way of inferior, medium, and superior.

In the second triad: virtue engaged in by one who wishes to abandon what is unbecoming to himself, treating himself as authority, out of regard for himself, is self-dominated. That engaged in by one who wishes to avoid the world's censure, treating the world as authority, out of regard for the world, is world-dominated. That engaged in by one who wishes to honor the greatness of the Dhamma, treating the Dhamma as authority, out of regard for the Dhamma, is Dhamma-dominated. Thus it is threefold by way of self-dominated, world-dominated, and Dhamma-dominated.

In the third triad: that which was called "dependent" among the pairs above — being misapprehended through craving and views — is misapprehended. That which is a requisite of the path for the virtuous ordinary person, and that associated with the path for trainees, is unmisapprehended. That associated with the fruit, for both trainees and adepts, is tranquilized. Thus it is threefold by way of misapprehended, unmisapprehended, and tranquilized.

In the fourth triad: that fulfilled by one who commits no offense, or, having committed one, has since made amends — that is purified. That of one who has committed an offense and made no amends is unpurified. The virtue of one who is in doubt about the grounds, the offense, or the transgression is called doubtful virtue. Herein, the meditator should purify unpurified virtue; and, without indulging the ground of doubt, should dispel the doubt in what is doubtful, thinking, "thus it will be comfortable for me." Thus it is threefold by way of purified, unpurified, and doubtful.

In the fifth triad: virtue associated with the four noble paths and the first three fruits of the ascetic life is the trainee's virtue. That associated with the fruit of Arahantship is the adept's virtue. The rest is neither the trainee's nor the adept's. Thus it is threefold by way of the trainee's, the adept's, and neither.

But in the Paṭisambhidā, since even the ordinary nature of various beings in the world is loosely called "sīla" — as when people say, "this one is of a happy nature, this one of a suffering nature, this one of a quarrelsome nature, this one of a vain nature" — it is therefore said, in that sense: "There are three virtues: wholesome virtue, unwholesome virtue, and indeterminate virtue" (Paṭis I.39). Thus it is also said to be threefold by way of wholesome and the rest. But here, the unwholesome does not accord with even one of the characteristics and so on of the virtue meant in this technical sense, so it is not brought in here — its threefold nature, therefore, should be understood only by the method already given.

Reading note — Ru-Yi

Watch how many times "inferior, medium, superior" gets reused across this whole question with a completely different basis each time — by mental factor, by motive, by moral taint, by object of craving — and how the second through fifth triads each mint an entirely new set of three labels. The chapter is not building one taxonomy; it is showing that "how many kinds" has no single correct answer, only as many answers as there are legitimate criteria for dividing the same thing. That, itself, is a very Abhidhamma stance to take: a phenomenon's "kind" is always relative to the lens applied to it.

The closing remark on kusala/akusala/abyākata (wholesome/unwholesome/indeterminate) is a small but pointed methodological aside: this is Abhidhamma's single most fundamental three-way classification, applied to every mental state without exception — and the chapter explicitly declines to use it here, on the ground that "unwholesome virtue" is a contradiction in terms for the technical sense of sīla it has spent this entire question defining. The Paṭisambhidā's looser, everyday use of "sīla" to mean simply "a person's nature or habit" (as in "a quarrelsome sort") is acknowledged, then set firmly aside.

āpattif.
