The Benefits of Developing Wisdom

This page carries Ru-Yi's translation of the Visuddhimagga's chapter 23, Paññābhāvanānisaṃsaniddesa ("The Description of the Benefits of Developing Wisdom") — the whole treatise's final chapter. It names and explains four benefits of the wisdom developed across chapters fourteen through twenty-two: the destruction of the web of defilements; the tasting of the noble fruit's own flavor, defended against the view that "fruit" is merely the fetters' bare absence; the rare capacity to attain the profound attainment of cessation, explored through an extensive catechism, the story of the elder Mahānāga surviving a fire while absorbed in cessation, and the simile of two travelers on an unfamiliar road; and the accomplishment of worthiness to receive offerings, worked out across every grade of noble person from the three kinds of stream-enterer to the various arahants. The chapter closes with its own formal colophon — and then, in a separate epilogue outside the numbered chapters, Buddhaghosa's own personal reflections and his famous self-description, closing this site's complete translation of all twenty-three chapters of the Visuddhimagga.

The Question Answered: Four Benefits of Developing Wisdom

The treatise's final chapter opens by answering a question implicitly hanging over everything since chapter fourteen first began the long ascent through the aggregates, bases, elements, and the rest: what, after all this, is the actual benefit of developing wisdom? Buddhaghosa's answer is characteristically honest about scale before it is specific — wisdom's development yields benefits running into the hundreds, not fully expressible even at great length — before naming, in brief, the four benefits this closing chapter will take up one by one: the destruction of the various defilements, the tasting of the flavor of the noble fruit, the capacity to attain the attainment of cessation, and the accomplishment of worthiness to receive offerings and the rest.

Pali Source

Yaṃ pana vuttaṃ "paññābhāvanāya ko ānisaṃso"ti, tattha vadāma. Ayañhi paññābhāvanā nāma anekasatānisaṃsā. Tassā dīghenāpi addhunā na sukaraṃ vitthārato ānisaṃsaṃ pakāsetuṃ. Saṅkhepato panassā nānākilesaviddhaṃsanaṃ, ariyaphalarasānubhavanaṃ, nirodhasamāpattisamāpajjanasamatthatā, āhuneyyabhāvādisiddhīti ayamānisaṃso veditabbo.

Ru-Yi Translation

Now, as to what was asked, "what is the benefit of the development of wisdom?" — to this we reply. This development of wisdom has benefits running into the hundreds. Even over a long stretch of time, it is not easy to make its benefit known in full detail. In brief, however, its benefit should be understood as this: the destruction of the various defilements, the tasting of the flavor of the noble fruit, the capacity to attain the attainment of cessation, and the accomplishment of worthiness to receive offerings and the rest.

Reading note — Ru-Yi

This opening paragraph performs the same function chapter fourteen's own opening performed at the start of the entire "wisdom" section: it announces, in a single compact sentence, the complete table of contents for everything still to come — four benefits, each of which will receive its own dedicated subhead before this final chapter, and the entire multi-year translation project, draws to a close. It is worth noticing the candor of "even over a long stretch of time, it is not easy to make its benefit known in full detail" — after twenty-two chapters of exhaustive, uncompressed detail on virtually every topic this treatise has touched, Buddhaghosa still insists that wisdom's full benefit exceeds what even a treatise of this length could fully capture. The four benefits named here are not arbitrary: they trace a natural arc from the purely negative (destroying defilements) through two positive experiential accomplishments (tasting the fruit, and the rare capacity for cessation-attainment) to a final social and spiritual status (worthiness to receive offerings) — moving, in effect, from what wisdom removes to what it makes a person actually be.

Key vocabulary — for Pāli learners
ānisaṃsa

benefit, advantage — the chapter's own title-word (Paññābhāvanānisaṃsaniddesa, "the description of the benefits of developing wisdom"), naming the entire closing chapter's single unifying theme across all four subheads to follow.

nānākilesaviddhaṃsana

"the destruction of the various defilements" — nānā (various) + kilesa (defilement) + viddhaṃsana (destroying, from vi + dhvaṃsati); the first of the four benefits, taken up immediately in the very next subhead.

nirodhasamāpattisamāpajjanasamatthatā

"the capacity to attain the attainment of cessation" — nirodha-samāpatti (the attainment of the cessation of perception and feeling) + samāpajjana (attaining) + samatthatā (capacity, ability); the third benefit, and the subject of this chapter's own longest and most technically celebrated subhead.

The First Benefit: Destroying the Web of Defilements

The first benefit is now distinguished into its mundane and supramundane forms — the destruction of defilements already narrated, in mundane form, from the very determining of mind-and-matter (chapter eighteen's own opening topic) onward, and, in supramundane form, at the actual moment of the noble path, exactly as chapter twenty-two's own great subhead worked out in exhaustive, category-by-category detail. Three verses close the subhead, comparing developed wisdom's destruction of the defilement-web to a thunderbolt's terrifying force falling on a mountain, a wildfire born of the wind sweeping through a forest, and the sun's blazing disk dispelling darkness — an ancient evil, long fallen upon beings and the source of every kind of harm.

Pali Source

Tattha yaṃ nāmarūpaparicchedato paṭṭhāya sakkāyadiṭṭhādīnaṃ vasena nānākilesaviddhaṃsanaṃ vuttaṃ, ayaṃ lokikāya paññābhāvanāya ānisaṃso. Yaṃ ariyamaggakkhaṇe saṃyojanādīnaṃ vasena nānākilesaviddhaṃsanaṃ vuttaṃ, ayaṃ lokuttarāya paññābhāvanāya ānisaṃsoti veditabbo.

"Bhīmavegānupatitā, asanīva siluccaye; Vāyuvegasamuṭṭhito, araññamiva pāvako.

Andhakāraṃ viya ravi, satejujjalamaṇḍalo; Dīgharattānupatitaṃ, sabbānatthavidhāyakaṃ.

Kilesajālaṃ paññā hi, viddhaṃsayati bhāvitā; Sandiṭṭhikamato jaññā, ānisaṃsamimaṃ idha."

Ru-Yi Translation

Herein, that "destruction of the various defilements" spoken of, by way of personality-view and the rest, from the determining of mind-and-matter onward — this should be understood as the benefit of mundane development of wisdom. That "destruction of the various defilements" spoken of, by way of the fetters and the rest, at the moment of the noble path — this should be understood as the benefit of supramundane development of wisdom.

"Falling with terrifying force, like a thunderbolt upon a mountain; arisen with the force of the wind, like a wildfire in a forest;

like the sun, with its blazing, radiant disk, dispelling darkness — [thus does developed wisdom destroy] the net of defilements, fallen upon beings for a long time, the cause of every kind of harm.

Truly, developed wisdom destroys the net of defilements. One should know this benefit here as directly visible."

Reading note — Ru-Yi

The mundane/supramundane division given here is a compact recapitulation of the entire "body" of wisdom's five purifications this project has just finished translating: "from the determining of mind-and-matter onward" points back to chapter eighteen's opening subhead, while "at the moment of the noble path" points to chapter twenty-two's own exhaustive fetter-by-fetter mapping. Rather than repeating any of that detail, Buddhaghosa simply gestures back to it — a fitting economy for a closing chapter whose job is synthesis, not fresh exposition.

The three similes in the closing verse are worth noticing for their escalating scale and their final, telling qualifier "sandiṭṭhikamato" — "as directly visible," meaning this benefit is not a promise about some future or unseen result but something the practitioner can verify for themselves, here and now, in their own diminished defilements. A thunderbolt striking a mountainside, a wind-driven wildfire consuming a forest, and the sun's own disk dispelling darkness are each, in their own register, images of overwhelming, near-instantaneous force meeting something that had seemed permanent or immovable — the "net of defilements... fallen upon beings for a long time" — and simply undoing it. The phrase "the cause of every kind of harm" (sabbānatthavidhāyakaṃ) is a fittingly severe verdict on defilement to close out a project that has, since chapter eighteen, spent so much space cataloguing exactly what those harms are.

Key vocabulary — for Pāli learners
asani

thunderbolt, lightning — the same word already met in chapter twenty-two's own samuccheda-pahāna simile (a tree struck by a thunderbolt, asanivicakkābhihata), here reused as the opening image for developed wisdom's own destructive force.

sandiṭṭhika

"directly visible," verifiable here and now — saṃ (with) + diṭṭhi (seeing) + ka (suffix); one of the traditional six qualities of the Dhamma recited in the standard formula (svākkhāto... sandiṭṭhiko...), here applied specifically to this first benefit of wisdom's development.

The Second Benefit: Tasting the Fruit — Not Mere Abandonment

The second benefit opens: not only the destruction of defilements, but the actual tasting of the flavor of the noble fruit — the fruits of stream-entry and the rest — is itself a benefit of developed wisdom, experienced in two ways, within the path's own cognitive process and, afterward, by way of fruition-attainment. Against a view current in Buddhaghosa's own time — that "fruit" names nothing more than the bare abandonment of fetters, with no further positive state to speak of — a Paṭisambhidāmagga citation is produced to prove that fruition is a genuine, positively-arising knowledge in its own right, arising precisely because the very effort that produced the path has now been calmed.

Pali Source

Ariyaphalarasānubhavananti na kevalañca kilesaviddhaṃsanaññeva, ariyaphalarasānubhavanampi paññābhāvanāya ānisaṃso. Ariyaphalanti hi sotāpattiphalādi sāmaññaphalaṃ vuccati. Tassa dvīhākārehi rasānubhavanaṃ hoti. Maggavīthiyañca phalasamāpattivasena ca pavattiyaṃ. Tatrāssa maggavīthiyaṃ pavatti dassitāyeva.

Apica ye "saṃyojanappahānamattameva phalaṃ nāma, na koci añño dhammo atthī"ti vadanti, tesaṃ anunayatthaṃ idaṃ suttampi dassetabbaṃ – "kathaṃ payogapaṭippassaddhipaññā phale ñāṇaṃ? Sotāpattimaggakkhaṇe dassanaṭṭhena sammādiṭṭhi micchādiṭṭhiyā vuṭṭhāti, tadanuvattakakilesehi ca khandhehi ca vuṭṭhāti, bahiddhā ca sabbanimittehi vuṭṭhāti. Tampayogapaṭippassaddhattā uppajjati sammādiṭṭhi, maggassetaṃ phala"nti vitthāretabbaṃ.

"Cattāro maggā apariyāpannā, cattāri ca sāmaññaphalāni, ime dhammā appamāṇārammaṇā". "Mahaggato dhammo appamāṇassa dhammassa anantarapaccayena paccayo"ti evamādīnipi cettha sādhakāni.

Ru-Yi Translation

"The tasting of the flavor of the noble fruit": not only is the destruction of defilements a benefit of the development of wisdom; the tasting of the flavor of the noble fruit is also a benefit. For "noble fruit" refers to the fruit of recluseship, beginning with the fruit of stream-entry. The tasting of its flavor occurs in two ways — within the path's own cognitive process, and by way of fruition-attainment, in its subsequent occurrence. Of these, its occurrence within the path's own cognitive process has already been shown.

Moreover, for the sake of favoring those who say, "fruit is simply the bare abandonment of fetters; there is no other, further state at all," this scriptural passage, too, should be shown: "how is understanding through the calming of effort knowledge in the fruit? At the moment of the path of stream-entry, right view, in the sense of vision, emerges from wrong view, emerges from the defilements and aggregates that follow along with it, and emerges externally from all signs. Because of the calming of that very effort, right view arises; this is the fruit of the path" — this should be explained in detail.

"The four paths that are unincluded [in the round of existence], and the four fruits of recluseship — these states have the unlimited as their object." "A sublime state is a condition, by way of the proximity-condition, for an unlimited state" — such passages as these, too, are confirmatory here.

Reading note — Ru-Yi

The rival view being refuted here — that "fruit" is nothing but the fetters' bare absence, with no further positive mental event to name — is a genuine and reasonable-sounding position: if a path's whole job is eliminating fetters, why posit a separate "fruit-consciousness" as some additional thing, rather than simply calling the post-path state "fetter-free" and leaving it at that? Buddhaghosa's answer does not argue the point abstractly but simply produces the Paṭisambhidāmagga's own technical formula, which explicitly names fruition as "understanding through the calming of effort" (payogapaṭippassaddhipaññā) — a positively arising cognitive event, distinct from the path itself, that occurs precisely because the path's own effort has now ceased. The two further citations (from the Dhammasaṅgaṇī and the Paṭṭhāna, the Abhidhamma's own opening and seventh books) reinforce this from a different angle, by listing the four fruits of recluseship alongside the four paths themselves as things with "the unlimited," i.e. Nibbāna, genuinely as their object — a status a mere absence could not coherently be said to have.

Key vocabulary — for Pāli learners
sāmaññaphala

"the fruit of recluseship" — sāmañña (the state of being a recluse/renunciant, sāmaṇa) + phala (fruit); the standard canonical term (familiar from the Sāmaññaphala Sutta) for what the noble path actually produces, here specified as the four fruits from stream-entry through arahantship.

payogapaṭippassaddhipaññā

"understanding through the calming of effort" — payoga (effort, application) + paṭippassaddhi (calming, tranquilizing) + paññā (understanding); the Paṭisambhidāmagga's own precise technical name for fruition-knowledge, distinguishing it as a positive cognitive event arising once the path's own active effort has subsided.

apariyāpanna

"unincluded" [in the round of existence] — a (not) + pariyāpanna (included, comprised); the standard Abhidhamma term for the supramundane paths and fruits, marking them as falling entirely outside the threefold mundane classification of sense-sphere, fine-material, and immaterial existence.

Nine Questions on Fruition-Attainment; What It Is, and Who Attains It

To make fruition-attainment's own occurrence fully clear, Buddhaghosa poses a systematic nine-question catechism — a method this project has already met in various forms throughout the treatise, here applied with particular thoroughness to a state many practitioners will never directly verify for themselves. The first two questions are answered at once: fruition-attainment is simply the noble fruit's own full absorption upon cessation (Nibbāna); and, while no ordinary worldling can attain it, lacking the prior achievement a noble person already has, every single noble person attains it — but only their own specific fruit, never a fruit above or below their current attainment, a point settled by directly quoting the Paṭisambhidāmagga's own list of exactly what each stage of gotrabhū-like preparation aims to unlock.

Pali Source

Phalasamāpattiyaṃ pavattidassanatthaṃ panassa idaṃ pañhākammaṃ – kā phalasamāpatti, ke taṃ samāpajjanti, ke na samāpajjanti, kasmā samāpajjanti, kathañcassā samāpajjanaṃ hoti, kathaṃ ṭhānaṃ, kathaṃ vuṭṭhānaṃ, kiṃ phalassa anantaraṃ, kassa ca phalaṃ anantaranti?

Tattha kā phalasamāpattīti yā ariyaphalassa nirodhe appanā.

Ke taṃ samāpajjanti, ke na samāpajjantīti sabbepi puthujjanā na samāpajjanti. Kasmā? Anadhigatattā. Ariyā pana sabbepi samāpajjanti. Kasmā? Adhigatattā. Uparimā pana heṭṭhimaṃ na samāpajjanti, puggalantarabhāvupagamanena paṭippassaddhattā. Heṭṭhimā ca uparimaṃ, anadhigatattā. Attano attanoyeva pana phalaṃ samāpajjantīti idamettha sanniṭṭhānaṃ.

Keci pana "sotāpannasakadāgāminopi na samāpajjanti. Uparimā dveyeva samāpajjantī"ti vadanti. Idañca tesaṃ kāraṇaṃ, ete hi samādhismiṃ paripūrakārinoti. Taṃ puthujjanassāpi attanā paṭiladdhalokiyasamādhisamāpajjanato akāraṇameva. Kiñcettha kāraṇākāraṇacintāya. Nanu pāḷiyaṃyeva vuttaṃ – "katame dasa gotrabhudhammā vipassanāvasena uppajjanti? Sotāpattimaggapaṭilābhatthāya uppādaṃ pavattaṃ…pe… upāyāsaṃ bahiddhā saṅkhāranimittaṃ abhibhuyyatīti gotrabhu. Sotāpattiphalasamāpattatthāya sakadāgāmimaggaṃ…pe… arahattaphalasamāpattatthāya… suññatavihārasamāpattatthāya… animittavihārasamāpattatthāya uppādaṃ…pe… bahiddhā saṅkhāranimittaṃ abhibhuyyatīti gotrabhū"ti. Tasmā sabbepi ariyā attano attano phalaṃ samāpajjantīti niṭṭhamettha gantabbaṃ.

Ru-Yi Translation

Now, for the sake of showing fruition-attainment's own occurrence, this catechism is undertaken: what is fruition-attainment? Who attains it, and who does not? Why do they attain it? How does its attainment occur? How is it sustained? How does one emerge from it? What immediately precedes the fruit? And of what is the fruit itself the immediate successor?

Herein, "what is fruition-attainment?" — it is the noble fruit's own full absorption upon cessation.

"Who attains it, and who does not?" — no worldling at all attains it. Why? Because it has not been achieved by them. But every noble person does attain it. Why? Because it has been achieved by them. Those at a higher stage, however, do not attain the fruit of a lower stage, since it has been calmed through their having advanced to a different kind of person. Nor do those at a lower stage attain the fruit of a higher stage, since it has not yet been achieved by them. Rather, each attains only their own particular fruit — this is the conclusion to be reached here.

But some say, "even the stream-enterer and the once-returner do not attain it; only the two higher ones attain it." And this is their reason: these two are the ones who have fulfilled concentration. But this is no reason at all, since even a worldling attains the mundane concentration he himself has acquired. And, moreover, what need is there here for reflection on reasons and non-reasons? Is it not stated in the very text itself: "which are the ten states of change-of-lineage that arise by way of insight? For the purpose of obtaining the path of stream-entry, it overcomes, with respect to what is external, arising, occurrence... distress, the sign of formations — thus is it change-of-lineage. For the purpose of attaining the fruition of stream-entry, [it overcomes]... the path of once-return... for the purpose of attaining the fruition of arahantship... for the purpose of attaining the dwelling in voidness... for the purpose of attaining the dwelling in the signless, it overcomes arising... the sign of formations, externally — thus is it change-of-lineage"? Therefore, the conclusion to be reached here is that every single noble person attains their own particular fruit.

Reading note — Ru-Yi

The debate recorded here — whether a stream-enterer and once-returner can genuinely attain fruition-attainment, or whether this rarefied practice is reserved for the two highest noble persons alone — is a good example of Buddhaghosa's characteristic method of settling a doctrinal dispute: rather than arguing purely from first principles, he goes straight to a canonical citation that already answers the question definitively. The cited Paṭisambhidāmagga passage lists ten distinct purposes for which "change-of-lineage" (gotrabhū, or here a broader term covering similar preparatory turning-toward moments) can arise — including explicitly "for the purpose of attaining the fruition of stream-entry" — which settles the matter beyond argument, since it would make no sense for the canon to name a purpose that no one could actually pursue.

The rejected argument — "these two [higher noble persons] are the ones who have fulfilled concentration" — deserves a moment's sympathy even in its rejection, since it reflects a reasonable-sounding intuition: fruition-attainment does require real concentrative skill, and non-returners and arahants have indeed eliminated far more of the coarse defilements that would otherwise disturb concentration. But Buddhaghosa's counter-example is decisive and almost deflating in its simplicity: even an ordinary, unenlightened worldling can attain the mundane concentration they have personally developed, so "having fulfilled concentration" cannot be the relevant bar for a noble person attaining their own already-verified supramundane fruit.

Key vocabulary — for Pāli learners
appanā

full absorption — from appeti (to fix firmly, to apply); the technical term for the mind's complete, sustained fixing upon its object, used here to define fruition-attainment as the fruit-consciousness's own absorption upon Nibbāna, paralleling how jhāna is defined as absorption upon a meditation object.

puggalantarabhāvupagamana

"having advanced to the state of a different person" — puggala (person) + antara (different, other) + bhāva (state) + upagamana (arriving at, advancing to); explains why a higher noble person cannot attain a lower fruit — they have, in effect, become a categorically different kind of person for whom the lower attainment is no longer relevant.

Why Fruition Is Attained, and the Actual Sequence of Attaining It

The third question — why does a noble person attain fruition at all, when the defilements it addresses are already gone? — is answered by comparison: exactly as a king enjoys the pleasure of sovereignty and a deity enjoys celestial pleasure, a noble person, having determined "I shall experience noble, supramundane happiness," attains fruition-attainment simply for the sake of a pleasant abiding in this very life, at whatever moment they wish. The fourth question, how its attainment actually occurs, receives a twofold answer straight from the Majjhima Nikāya: through non-attention to any object other than Nibbāna, and through attention to Nibbāna itself — followed by the precise sequence this produces, in which a noble disciple retiring alone contemplates the rise and fall of formations until, immediately after the change-of-lineage knowledge that takes formations as its final object, the mind plunges into cessation by way of fruition-attainment — and, crucially, even a trainee's mind at this moment produces only fruit, never a fresh path.

Pali Source

Kasmā samāpajjantīti diṭṭhadhammasukhavihāratthaṃ. Yathā hi rājā rajjasukhaṃ, devatā dibbasukhaṃ anubhavanti, evaṃ ariyā "ariyaṃ lokuttarasukhaṃ anubhavissāmā"ti addhānapparicchedaṃ katvā icchiticchitakkhaṇe phalasamāpattiṃ samāpajjanti.

Kathañcassā samāpajjanaṃ hoti, kathaṃ ṭhānaṃ, kathaṃ vuṭṭhānanti dvīhi tāva ākārehi assā samāpajjanaṃ hoti – nibbānato aññassa ārammaṇassa amanasikārā nibbānassa ca manasikārā. Yathāha – "dve kho, āvuso, paccayā animittāya cetovimuttiyā samāpattiyā sabbanimittānañca amanasikāro, animittāya ca dhātuyā manasikāro"ti.

Ayampanettha samāpajjanakkamo. Phalasamāpattatthikena hi ariyasāvakena rahogatena paṭisallīnena udayabbayādivasena saṅkhārā vipassitabbā. Tassa pavattānupubbavipassanassa saṅkhārārammaṇagotrabhuñāṇānantarā phalasamāpattivasena nirodhe cittaṃ appeti. Phalasamāpattininnatāya cettha sekkhassāpi phalameva uppajjati, na maggo.

Ye pana vadanti "sotāpanno 'phalasamāpattiṃ samāpajjissāmī'ti vipassanaṃ paṭṭhapetvā sakadāgāmī hoti. Idameva pana gahetabbaṃ – sekkhassāpi phalameva uppajjati, na maggo. Phalañcassa sace anena paṭhamajjhāniko maggo adhigato hoti. Paṭhamajjhānikameva uppajjati. Ru-Yi Translation

"Why do they attain it?" — for the sake of a pleasant abiding in this very life. For, just as a king experiences the pleasure of sovereignty, and a deity experiences celestial pleasure, so too the noble ones, having determined, "we shall experience noble, supramundane happiness," attain fruition-attainment at whatever moment they wish.

"How does its attainment occur, how is it sustained, how does one emerge from it?" — its attainment, first, occurs in two ways: through non-attention to any object other than Nibbāna, and through attention to Nibbāna. As it is said: "friend, there are two conditions for the signless deliverance of mind by way of attainment: non-attention to all signs, and attention to the signless element."

Now, here is the sequence of its attainment. For a noble disciple desirous of fruition-attainment, having gone into solitude and withdrawn, should contemplate the formations by way of their rise and fall and the rest. For one whose insight thus proceeds in due succession, immediately after the change-of-lineage knowledge that has the formations as its object, the mind, by way of fruition-attainment, becomes absorbed in cessation. And here, because of the inclination toward fruition-attainment, even for a trainee, only fruit arises, never the path.

But those who say, "a stream-enterer, having set out on insight thinking 'I shall attain fruition-attainment,' becomes a once-returner; and a once-returner becomes a non-returner" — these should be told: "if this were so, a non-returner would become an arahant, an arahant a paccekabuddha, and a paccekabuddha a Buddha. Therefore this is nothing at all," and this, too, should not be accepted merely because it has already been refuted in this manner by the text itself. Rather, precisely this should be accepted: even for a trainee, only fruit arises, never the path. And that fruit, if the path attained by him was accompanied by the first meditative absorption, then only that accompanied by the first absorption arises. If it was accompanied by one or another of the second and further absorptions, then only that accompanied by one or another of the second and further absorptions arises. Thus, first, does its attainment occur.

Reading note — Ru-Yi

The king-and-deity simile answering "why attain it?" deserves a moment's reflection for what it implies about the noble life more broadly: fruition-attainment is presented here not as some grim, joyless discipline but as the noble person's own legitimate, freely chosen source of the highest available pleasure — "supramundane happiness" available on demand, at whatever moment is wished. This directly answers a question a careful reader might have been quietly asking since chapter one: what does an arahant, with craving entirely extinguished, actually do with their remaining time in this life? Part of the answer, this passage suggests, is precisely this — resting in fruition-attainment's own available happiness, exactly as a king might retreat to enjoy the pleasures proper to sovereignty.

The rejected "escalator" view — that repeatedly attempting fruition-attainment might itself gradually promote a stream-enterer to a once-returner, and so on up the ladder — is dismantled with a wonderfully economical reductio: if simply attempting fruition-attainment could bump a practitioner up one level, there would be nothing in principle stopping this same mechanism from eventually promoting an arahant all the way to full Buddhahood, which is absurd on its face, since Buddhahood requires an entirely different, vastly longer path of its own (the pāramī-fulfilling career already treated in this project's earlier chapters on the six recollections and the immeasurables). The correct picture, instead, is reassuringly modest: fruition-attainment simply reproduces, again and again, exactly the fruit a practitioner has already attained — never a fruit above it, and, matching the specific jhāna-factor composition their own original path possessed, never a fruit of some different meditative flavor either.

How Fruition Is Sustained and Emerged From; What Precedes and Follows It

The remaining questions are answered in swift succession, each backed by its own Majjhima Nikāya citation. Fruition-attainment is sustained through three conditions — non-attention to all signs, attention to the signless element, and a prior determination of when to emerge — and is emerged from through two conditions, the reverse of the entry-conditions. What immediately precedes a given fruit varies with context (sometimes the path, sometimes an earlier fruit, sometimes change-of-lineage, sometimes even the base of neither-perception-nor-non-perception for one emerging from cessation-attainment), and everything except a path-produced fruit counts as occurring "by way of fruition-attainment." A closing set of verses caps the subhead, likening the fruit's own flavor to something sweeter than honey, tasted by the wise through developing the noble fruit's own understanding.

Reading note — Ru-Yi

The precise entry/persistence/emergence formula given across this and the preceding section — two conditions to enter, three to sustain (the extra one being the prior determination of a return-time), two to emerge — will recur, essentially unchanged, in the very next subhead's treatment of the far rarer and more famous attainment of cessation, and it is worth holding onto this simpler version as a template before the more elaborate one arrives. The closing verses' central image — the fruit's flavor "sweet in every degree... like honey" — deserves to be read as something more than ornamental praise: the phrase "spitting-out of the flesh-bait of the world" (vantalokāmisa) is a deliberately visceral image, comparing worldly pleasure to bait a fish has already swallowed and must disgorge, making the fruit's own sweetness not a naive escape from pleasure altogether but a replacement of one kind of pleasure (crude, hook-laden, ultimately harmful) with another (pure, unsurpassed, genuinely nourishing) — a fitting final image for a benefit whose entire point, as the previous section established, is a freely available "pleasant abiding" a noble person may return to at will.

The Third Benefit: Eleven Questions on the Attainment of Cessation

The chapter's third and most celebrated benefit now opens: not merely fruition-attainment, but the capacity to attain the far rarer attainment of cessation — the complete, temporary stopping of consciousness and its mental factors — is itself a benefit of developed wisdom. An even more extensive catechism than the last is posed, eleven questions in all, before the first two are answered: cessation-attainment is the non-occurrence of consciousness and its associated mental factors, brought about in graduated stages; and, contrary to what one might expect, it is attained by neither ordinary worldlings nor even by stream-enterers, once-returners, or non-returners who practice insight alone without the eight mundane absorptions — only a non-returner or an arahant who has also mastered all eight mundane attainments can accomplish it, since a very specific, five-part technical qualification, quoted next from the Paṭisambhidāmagga's own summary-list, belongs to no one else.

Reading note — Ru-Yi

The scope of exclusion named here deserves to be read slowly, since it is more sweeping than a first pass might suggest: not just ordinary worldlings, but stream-enterers and once-returners of any kind, "bare-insight" (sukkhavipassaka) non-returners who reached their attainment through insight alone without mastering the eight jhānas, and even arahants themselves are excluded unless they too happen to have mastered the eight mundane absorptions. This means cessation-attainment is not simply a function of how far one has progressed along the four noble paths — an arahant, the highest of all noble persons, may nonetheless be entirely incapable of it, if their own path to arahantship proceeded through insight alone. What matters instead is a specific combination of the very highest fruit-stage development (third or fourth path) together with complete mastery of mundane jhāna up through the base of neither-perception-nor-non-perception — two achievements that do not automatically come bundled together, and whose combination is rare enough to be singled out as its own distinct kind of benefit.

The five-part technical formula quoted here — two powers, three tranquilized formations, sixteen exercises of knowledge, nine exercises of concentration — is itself only named at this point, not yet explained; Buddhaghosa signals as much in the next paragraph by simply reproducing, without adding his own commentary, the Paṭisambhidāmagga's own detailed gloss on each element. This is a mark of considerable respect for the source material: rather than paraphrasing or condensing what the canonical text already states with total precision, the commentary defers entirely to the original wording.

Two Powers and Three Tranquilized Formations

Rather than paraphrase, Buddhaghosa simply reproduces the Paṭisambhidāmagga's own detailed exposition of the five-part formula, beginning with its first two elements: the two powers of serenity and insight, each defined through its own specific unshakeability — serenity's power shown through each successive jhāna's freedom from being shaken by its own specific opposing factor, all the way up through the base of neither-perception-nor-non-perception's freedom from the perception of nothingness; and insight's power shown through an exactly parallel progression of contemplations, from impermanence through relinquishment, each unshaken by its own specific opposite perception. The three tranquilized formations are then named: verbal formations (initial and sustained application of mind) tranquilized by the second jhāna, bodily formations (in-and-out breathing) tranquilized by the fourth jhāna, and mental formations (perception and feeling) tranquilized only by cessation-attainment itself.

Sixteen Exercises of Knowledge, Nine of Concentration, and Five Masteries

The formula's remaining three elements complete the citation: sixteen "exercises of knowledge" — the same seven insight-contemplations already named, followed by nine further named states running from the path of stream-entry through the fruition-attainment of arahantship, treating each named accomplishment along the entire noble path as its own kind of "exercise"; nine "exercises of concentration" — the eight mundane jhānic attainments themselves, together with the specific fivefold jhāna-factor cluster (initial application, sustained application, rapture, pleasure, one-pointedness) needed to first obtain each one; and finally the five masteries — over adverting, attaining, resolving upon a duration, emerging, and reviewing — each defined by the identical formula of being able to perform the function "wherever wished, for however long wished, to whatever extent wished," entirely without sluggishness.

Why Only Non-Returners and Arahants: The Unabandoned Fifth Cord of Sense-Pleasure

A follow-up question sharpens the earlier exclusion: the formula just cited specified sixteen "exercises of knowledge," but a non-returner, having not yet attained arahantship, can only claim fourteen of the sixteen — so why does a non-returner still qualify, while a once-returner (with only twelve) and a stream-enterer (with only ten) do not? The answer turns on a single unabandoned fetter: sensual desire for the five cords of sense-pleasure, still present, though weakened, in the once-returner and, though not yet eliminated at all in the deepest sense, unresolved for the stream-enterer as well — leaving their power of serenity permanently incomplete, and an incomplete serenity-power simply cannot support an attainment that specifically requires both powers functioning at full strength. The non-returner alone has eliminated this fetter entirely, giving a fully complete power of serenity capable of the task — confirmed by a direct citation from the Paṭṭhāna itself, specifying that it is precisely the non-returner's own emergence from cessation that the great treatise has in mind.

Where, Why, and How Cessation-Attainment Occurs

Three more questions follow in quick succession. Cessation can only be attained in the five-aggregate (sense-sphere or fine-material) planes of existence, never in the four-aggregate (purely immaterial) planes, since the immaterial planes lack the very first jhāna a meditator must pass through on the way there — though some hold this is instead simply because the immaterial planes lack the physical basis (heart-basis) cessation itself requires. It is attained purely out of weariness with the constant occurring and re-occurring of formations, and a wish to dwell, however temporarily, having reached cessation itself, in genuine ease. And it is attained by one of three possible combinations of serenity and insight, applied with unequal emphasis: development by serenity alone reaches only the base of neither-perception-nor-non-perception; development by insight alone reaches only fruition-attainment; only development by both together, with the preliminary duties properly performed, reaches cessation itself.

Reading note — Ru-Yi

The three-way branching given in answer to "how does it occur" is a genuinely illuminating piece of comparative analysis, since it places cessation-attainment within a wider map of what serenity and insight can each accomplish alone versus together: serenity-only practice, however far pushed, tops out at the base of neither-perception-nor-non-perception — the highest mundane jhāna, but still only a mundane jhāna; insight-only practice tops out at fruition-attainment — genuinely supramundane, but not cessation; only the combination of both, correctly sequenced and accompanied by specific preliminary duties the very next paragraphs will detail, breaks through to cessation-attainment itself. This confirms, from a fresh angle, the "yoked pair" (yuganaddha) ideal already central to chapter twenty-two's own vuṭṭhānabalasamāyoga subhead — cessation-attainment turns out to be, in effect, the single most demonstrative proof available that serenity and insight, perfected together rather than separately, can reach further than either one alone ever could.

The stated motivation — weariness with "the occurring and the diversity of formations" and a wish to "dwell in ease" — is worth reading alongside the earlier motivation given for fruition-attainment (a freely chosen pleasant abiding, likened to royal or celestial pleasure). Cessation-attainment's own motivation is subtly different in register: less a positive pleasure actively sought and more a genuine respite actively needed, a deliberate, temporary stepping-outside of the ceaseless arising-and-passing this entire treatise has spent twenty-two chapters teaching a meditator to see with ever-increasing clarity. It is, in a sense, the ultimate practical payoff of having seen impermanence so thoroughly that even a brief holiday from formations altogether becomes both intelligible and desirable.

The Detailed Procedure Begins: Sitting, Absorption, and Three Kinds of Insight

Having given the summary answer, Buddhaghosa now supplies the full, practical detail. A monk wishing to attain cessation, his meal-duties finished, his hands and feet well washed, sits in a secluded place on a well-prepared seat, folding his legs crosswise, holding his body erect, and establishing mindfulness in front of him; he then attains the first meditative absorption, emerges from it, and contemplates the very formations just experienced as impermanent, suffering, and not-self — but with a specific, calibrated intensity, since insight comes in three distinct grades suited to three distinct purposes: insight for comprehending formations may be dull or sharp either way, since it merely serves as the path's proximate cause regardless; insight for fruition-attainment must be sharp, resembling path-development itself; but insight for cessation-attainment must be neither too dull nor too sharp — precisely calibrated for this one specific, demanding purpose.

Reading note — Ru-Yi

The threefold classification of insight given here is a genuinely useful piece of practical calibration, easy to overlook amid the treatise's more abstract doctrinal analysis: not every occasion for insight calls for the same intensity, and knowing which of the three purposes one is actually pursuing changes what "correct" practice looks like in the moment. A meditator working simply toward the path itself need not worry about over- or under-sharpening their insight, since either will serve as an adequate proximate cause; a meditator specifically pursuing fruition-attainment should push insight as sharp as possible, since this state resembles the path's own development; but a meditator aiming at cessation-attainment must find a middle register — sharp enough to genuinely progress, yet restrained enough not to simply tip over into a fresh fruition-attainment or a fresh path-moment instead of the intended cessation.

The physical preparations described — finishing one's meal, washing hands and feet, sitting cross-legged with an erect body and mindfulness established in front — are worth noting as identical, almost word for word, to the standard opening formula for entering any jhāna throughout the earlier meditation chapters of this project, confirming that cessation-attainment, however extraordinary its eventual destination, begins from exactly the same ordinary posture and preliminary care as any other meditative undertaking.

Ascending Through the Jhānas; the First Preliminary Duty and Mahānāga Thera's Fire

The meditator repeats this same pattern of attaining and contemplating through the second, third, and fourth jhānas and onward through the base of infinite space and the base of infinite consciousness, until, having emerged from the base of nothingness, he performs four essential preliminary duties before attempting the base of neither-perception-nor-non-perception itself. The first of these — protecting whatever belongings are not bound to his own person, such that they come to no harm from fire, water, wind, thieves, or rats during the seven days he intends to remain in cessation — is illustrated by the story of the elder Mahānāga, who, having entered cessation-attainment in a lay supporter's rest-house without making this determination, sat unharmed at the center of a raging fire that consumed the entire building around him, emerging afterward, once the villagers had extinguished the flames and paid their homage, to fly off to Piyaṅgu Island rather than remain and be lionized.

Reading note — Ru-Yi

The Mahānāga story is one of the most vivid narrative episodes in this entire final chapter, and it repays being read for more than its dramatic surface: the villagers' scornful cry "a lazy ascetic!" (alasasamaṇo), hurled at a monk sitting motionless while his own shelter burns around him, is precisely what an outside observer with no knowledge of cessation-attainment would reasonably conclude — and the story's quiet point is that the elder's own stillness looked, to the uninformed, exactly like negligence rather than the deepest possible form of diligence. The elder's own reaction on emerging — not pride at having survived a fire unharmed, but faint embarrassment at having become "conspicuous" (pākaṭa), followed immediately by flight to a remote island — is a small but telling character detail, consistent with a broader pattern in the Pali commentarial tradition of arahants preferring anonymity to public acclaim, however miraculous the circumstances.

The closing distinction between bound and unbound possessions deserves attention as a genuinely practical piece of guidance: a monk's own robes and the very seat he sits on are automatically protected by the power of the attainment itself, requiring no separate determination — the citation about the venerable Sañjīva and, by association, the venerable Sāriputta (both said to have displayed "the expansion of concentration," samādhivipphārā iddhi, a specific kind of psychic power arising directly from deep concentration rather than from deliberate resolve) confirms that this automatic protection is a recognized, named phenomenon in its own right, distinct from the deliberate determination required for anything not physically attached to the meditator's own body.

The Second and Third Duties: Awaiting the Saṅgha's and the Teacher's Summons

The second and third preliminary duties are, strictly speaking, not the actual waiting itself but the specific mental act of resolving in advance to respond the moment a summons comes — the meditator determines that, should the Saṅgha wish to perform some formal business requiring his presence during his seven days in cessation, or should the Teacher establish a training rule or deliver a Dhamma-talk prompted by some relevant occasion, he will emerge the very instant any monk comes to fetch him. Both duties are so weighty that, having made this determination, a monk in cessation will indeed spontaneously emerge the moment a messenger, standing within earshot, simply reports that the Saṅgha or the Teacher is calling for him — without needing to be summoned by name or physically disturbed.

Reading note — Ru-Yi

The careful distinction drawn here — "the actual waiting is not this monk's own preliminary duty; rather, adverting to that waiting is his preliminary duty" — is a subtle but important piece of technical precision easy to miss on a quick reading. It is not that the meditator must somehow remain alert to the Saṅgha's needs while fully absorbed in cessation (a contradiction in terms, since cessation-attainment stops consciousness entirely); rather, a single act of mental determination made before entering the attainment is enough to guarantee automatic emergence the instant a specific triggering condition occurs, without any conscious monitoring taking place during the attainment itself. This is structurally identical to the "prior determination" (pubbe ca abhisaṅkhāro) already established as one of the three conditions sustaining ordinary fruition-attainment — cessation-attainment simply extends the same mechanism to cover institutional and pastoral emergencies as well as a simple self-chosen return-time.

The parallel structure of the two duties — Saṅgha and Teacher — and their shared closing formula ("so weighty, indeed, is...") together convey something important about the social embeddedness even the most solitary and extraordinary meditative attainment must respect: a monk capable of entering a state most beings will never even glimpse is nonetheless expected to remain answerable, without delay, to the ordinary institutional life of the monastic community and to the Buddha's own teaching authority — cessation-attainment is treated here not as an escape from communal responsibility but as something to be undertaken responsibly within it.

The Fourth Duty: Determining One's Own Lifespan, and Why Only Two More Moments

The fourth and final preliminary duty is the one genuinely indispensable one: a monk must skillfully determine, before entering cessation, that his own life-formations will indeed continue for the intended seven days, since — unlike the first three duties, which may safely be skipped without disaster — entering cessation without first checking that death will not intervene within that period risks a catastrophe cessation-attainment cannot itself prevent: because death simply does not occur within cessation, a meditator whose life-span would have ended partway through the seven days is instead forced to emerge from the attainment prematurely, part-way through, the moment that critical juncture arrives. Having then performed all four duties, the meditator attains the base of nothingness, emerges, attains the base of neither-perception-nor-non-perception, and, after at most two further moments of consciousness proceed, becomes wholly without consciousness, touching cessation itself — a strict two-moment ceiling explained by the specific character of the preliminary work: it is the yoking together of serenity and insight across all eight attainments in due order that itself constitutes the actual "application" bringing about graduated cessation, not the base of neither-perception-nor-non-perception considered as an attainment in its own right.

Reading note — Ru-Yi

This paragraph's opening observation — that the fourth duty alone, among the four, is strictly mandatory — is worth flagging clearly, since it is easy to lose amid the parallel structure of the four duties as a set: skipping the first duty risks only property damage (as Mahānāga's story illustrated, without any harm to the elder himself); skipping the second or third duty risks only a delayed, rather than instant, response to an institutional summons; but skipping the fourth duty risks the single genuinely irreversible catastrophe cessation-attainment cannot self-correct for. The reasoning is precise and slightly chilling in its clarity: cessation-attainment stops consciousness so completely that even the ordinary biological process of dying cannot occur within it — meaning a meditator who enters cessation without checking their remaining lifespan does not risk dying while absorbed, but rather is automatically ejected from the attainment the instant death would otherwise have occurred, emerging back into consciousness specifically in order to die in the ordinary way, having lost whatever remaining portion of the intended seven days was cut short.

The technical explanation for the strict two-moment ceiling deserves careful attention as one of the more genuinely difficult pieces of Abhidhamma reasoning in the whole treatise: the point is that the entire graduated eight-attainment ascent, taken together as a single unified "application" (payoga), is what is actually working toward cessation throughout — meaning the base of neither-perception-nor-non-perception, reached at the very top of that ascent, is not being pursued here as a freestanding goal in its own right (as it would be for an ordinary meditator simply practicing the formless attainments) but purely as the final launching-point for cessation itself. Because the entire ascent's "application" is aimed at cessation rather than at that base considered in isolation, consciousness simply cannot stabilize and continue at that level for more than a couple of moments before the application it was always serving completes itself.

The Two Travelers on an Unfamiliar Road; How the Attainment Endures

A monk who skips the preliminary duty and attempts the base of neither-perception-nor-non-perception directly finds himself unable to become wholly without consciousness, falling back instead into the base of nothingness — illustrated by a traveler on an unfamiliar road who, reaching a deep water-filled ravine or a rock scorched by fierce sunlight, and having failed to first tie up his loose garment, retreats to the near bank or the near side rather than risk soaking his belongings or burning his feet; only a traveler who has walked the road before, and who ties his garment securely and grips it firmly in hand beforehand, crosses the ravine or crosses the hot rock and continues on. Once cessation is thus properly reached, its sustaining is explained simply: it endures precisely for as long as the pre-determined time-limit has not yet arrived, and for as long as none of the three interrupting emergencies — an untimely death, the Saṅgha's summons, the Teacher's summons — has occurred.

Reading note — Ru-Yi

The two-travelers simile deserves to be read as a companion piece to the earlier canal-crossing simile from chapter twenty-two's very own opening subhead, since both use the image of a hazardous crossing to illustrate a mental threshold, but with an instructively different emphasis: where the canal-crossing simile emphasized the continuous, unreleased grip carrying a meditator across from conformity through gotrabhū to the path itself, this simile emphasizes preparation as the decisive variable — the exact same destination (the far side of the ravine, or safe passage across the hot rock) is reachable or unreachable depending entirely on whether one preliminary step (tying up one's garment) was taken beforehand, with no difference at all in the traveler's underlying capability or courage. An untrained meditator attempting the base of neither-perception-nor-non-perception without the fourfold preliminary duty is not lacking in concentrative skill — he may have climbed through every jhāna below flawlessly — but simply has not "tied up his garment," and so is turned back at the very last step, exactly like the first traveler's futile attempts at the ravine and the rock.

The sustaining conditions named in the final sentence quietly complete a picture that has been building across the preceding several sections: cessation-attainment endures for as long as none of exactly three specific interrupting emergencies has arisen, corresponding precisely to duties two, three, and four of the fourfold preliminary preparation (the first duty, protecting unbound possessions, does not itself interrupt the attainment even if violated — as Mahānāga's own story demonstrated, since his rest-hall burned around him without forcing him to emerge). This confirms that the four duties are not simply four equally-weighted checklist items but serve two genuinely distinct functions: protecting external property, and — for the latter three — actually governing how long the attainment itself can continue uninterrupted.

Emergence, the Mind Inclined to Seclusion, and the Difference Between Death and Cessation

The remaining questions close out this celebrated subhead. Emergence from cessation occurs in only two ways — for a non-returner, by the arising of the fruit of non-return; for an arahant, by the arising of the fruit of arahantship — and one who has emerged finds their mind inclined, sloped, and directed toward seclusion, exactly as the Buddha himself told the householder Visākha. The difference between a dead person and one who has attained cessation is then drawn with exquisite precision, straight from the Majjhima Nikāya: both share ceased and tranquilized bodily, verbal, and mental formations, but the dead person's vitality is exhausted, their heat settled, and their faculties broken up, while the one in cessation has vitality unexhausted, heat unsettled, and faculties unbroken. And to the final, most abstract question — is cessation-attainment conditioned, unconditioned, mundane, supramundane, produced, or unproduced? — Buddhaghosa answers that none of the usual predicates strictly apply, since cessation-attainment has no positive essential nature of its own to characterize in that way; the only thing that can be truthfully said is that it is "accomplished," precisely by virtue of being genuinely attained by the one who attains it.

Yathāha – "yvāyaṃ, āvuso, mato kālaṅkato, tassa kāyasaṅkhārā niruddhā paṭippassaddhā, vacīsaṅkhārā… cittasaṅkhārā niruddhā paṭippassaddhā, āyu parikkhīṇo, usmā vūpasantā, indriyāni paribhinnāni. Yo cāyaṃ bhikkhu saññāvedayitanirodhaṃ samāpanno, tassapi kāyasaṅkhārā niruddhā paṭippassaddhā, vacīsaṅkhārā… cittasaṅkhārā niruddhā paṭippassaddhā, āyu aparikkhīṇo, usmā avūpasantā, indriyāni aparibhinnānī"ti.

Nirodhasamāpatti saṅkhatātiādipucchāyaṃ pana saṅkhatātipi asaṅkhatātipi lokiyātipi lokuttarātipi na vattabbā. Kasmā? Sabhāvato natthitāya. Yasmā panassā samāpajjantassa vasena samāpannā nāma hoti, tasmā nipphannāti vattuṃ vaṭṭati, no anipphannā.

"Iti santaṃ samāpattiṃ, imaṃ ariyanisevitaṃ; Diṭṭheva dhamme nibbānamitisaṅkhaṃ upāgataṃ.

Bhāvetvā ariyaṃ paññaṃ, samāpajjanti paṇḍitā. Yasmā tasmā imissāpi, samāpattisamatthatā; Ariyamaggesu paññāya, ānisaṃsoti vuccatīti."

Ru-Yi Translation

"How does one emerge from it?" — for a non-returner, by the arising of the fruit of non-return; for an arahant, by the arising of the fruit of arahantship. Thus does emergence occur in two ways.

"What is the mind of one who has emerged inclined toward?" — it is inclined toward Nibbāna. For this was said: "friend Visākha, for a monk who has emerged from the attainment of the cessation of perception and feeling, the mind is inclined toward seclusion, sloped toward seclusion, directed toward seclusion."

"What is the difference between one who is dead and one who has attained it?" — this meaning, too, has already been stated in the text itself. As it is said: "friend, in the case of one who is dead, who has completed his time: his bodily formations have ceased and been tranquilized; his verbal formations... his mental formations have ceased and been tranquilized; his vitality is exhausted; his heat has settled; his faculties are broken up. But in the case of this monk who has attained the cessation of perception and feeling: his bodily formations, too, have ceased and been tranquilized; his verbal formations... his mental formations, too, have ceased and been tranquilized; yet his vitality is not exhausted; his heat has not settled; his faculties are unbroken."

As for the question beginning, "is the attainment of cessation conditioned...?" — it should not be said to be conditioned, nor unconditioned, nor mundane, nor supramundane. Why? Because, by way of its own essential nature, it does not exist [as a positive state to be so characterized]. But, since it comes to be called "attained" precisely by virtue of the one who attains it, it is proper to say that it is "accomplished" — not "unaccomplished."

"Thus, this peaceful attainment, resorted to by the noble ones, which has, in this very life, come to be reckoned as Nibbāna —

having developed the noble understanding, the wise attain it. Therefore, this capacity for attainment, too, with respect to wisdom in the noble paths, is said to be a benefit."

Reading note — Ru-Yi

The dead-versus-attained comparison is among the most precise pieces of phenomenological description in the entire treatise, and its power lies precisely in how much the two states share before the crucial difference is named: both a corpse and a body in cessation-attainment have bodily, verbal, and mental formations equally ceased and tranquilized — from the outside, by any ordinary observation, the two could look identical. The difference is confined to three specific, almost clinical markers — vitality (āyu), heat (usmā), and the integrity of the sense-faculties (indriyāni) — each present, unexhausted, and unbroken in the meditator, and each correspondingly absent, exhausted, or broken in the corpse. This passage functions as a kind of built-in safety mechanism within the tradition itself: it gives anyone caring for a monk in cessation-attainment (as the villagers unknowingly did for Mahānāga) a concrete, checkable basis for distinguishing profound meditative absorption from death, long before any of the more dramatic external evidence (survival through fire, spontaneous emergence at a predetermined time) might become available.

The final question's answer is genuinely one of the more philosophically careful moments in the whole Visuddhimagga: rather than forcing cessation-attainment into any of the standard fourfold classification already used everywhere else in this treatise (conditioned/unconditioned, mundane/supramundane — the very same categories, incidentally, already invoked and firmly resolved for Nibbāna itself back in chapter sixteen), Buddhaghosa simply declines to apply any of them, on the grounds that cessation-attainment, unlike Nibbāna, has no independent essential nature (sabhāva) of its own to characterize — it is, after all, defined negatively throughout this entire subhead as a non-occurrence of mental events, not a positive thing in its own right. The single permissible predicate offered instead — "accomplished" (nipphannā), simply because it genuinely is attained by whoever attains it — is a fittingly minimal, almost deflationary conclusion to what has otherwise been this chapter's single most elaborate and technically demanding subhead.

vivekaninna, vivekapoṇa, vivekapabbhāra

"inclined toward, sloped toward, directed toward seclusion" — three near-synonymous terms (ninna/poṇa/pabbhāra all meaning a downward or directed slope) piled up for emphasis, from the Buddha's own reply to the householder Visākha describing the mind's disposition immediately after emerging from cessation.

āyu, usmā, indriya

nipphanna

"accomplished," produced — ni (down, thoroughly) + phanna (from phalati, to result, come about); the single predicate Buddhaghosa is willing to apply to cessation-attainment, chosen specifically because the attainment is defined by the fact of its being genuinely reached, not by any independent essential nature of its own.

The Fourth Benefit: Worthiness to Receive Offerings

The fourth and final benefit is now named: developed wisdom accomplishes worthiness to receive offerings and the further honors that accompany it. Simply by virtue of having developed this fourfold wisdom, without further distinction between its various grades, a person of developed wisdom becomes worthy of hospitality, worthy of gifts, worthy of veneration, worthy of reverential salutation — an unsurpassed field of merit for the world, together with its gods.

Pali Source

Āhuneyyabhāvādisiddhīti na kevalañca nirodhasamāpattiyā samāpajjanasamatthatāva, ayaṃ pana āhuneyyabhāvādisiddhipi imissā lokuttarapaññābhāvanāya ānisaṃsoti veditabbo. Avisesena hi catubbidhāyapi etissā bhāvitattā bhāvitapañño puggalo sadevakassa lokassa āhuneyyo hoti pāhuneyyo dakkhiṇeyyo añjalīkaraṇīyo anuttaraṃ puññakkhettaṃ lokassa.

Ru-Yi Translation

"The accomplishment of worthiness to receive offerings and the rest": not only is the capacity to attain the attainment of cessation a benefit; this accomplishment of worthiness to receive offerings and the rest, too, should be understood as a benefit of this supramundane development of wisdom. For, without further distinction, simply by virtue of this fourfold wisdom having been developed, a person of developed wisdom becomes, for the world together with its gods, one worthy of hospitality, worthy of gifts, worthy of veneration, worthy of reverential salutation — an unsurpassed field of merit for the world.

Reading note — Ru-Yi

This general statement — worthiness accrues "without further distinction, simply by virtue of this fourfold wisdom having been developed" — is a deliberately leveling opening to a subhead that is about to draw exceptionally fine distinctions among noble persons: whatever further gradations of sharpness, faculty, and specific attainment the following paragraphs distinguish, all four grades of noble wisdom already qualify their possessor for the identical fourfold honor named here. The four terms used — āhuneyya, pāhuneyya, dakkhiṇeyya, añjalikaraṇīya — together form a standard, frequently recited canonical formula praising the qualities of the Saṅgha, and their appearance here confirms that this formula's real referent, throughout the canon, is precisely the noble persons whose fourfold classification this subhead now proceeds to work out in full technical detail.

Key vocabulary — for Pāli learners
āhuneyya, pāhuneyya, dakkhiṇeyya, añjalikaraṇīya

worthy of hospitality, worthy as a guest, worthy of gifts, worthy of reverential salutation — four terms forming the standard canonical formula (recited throughout the Nikāyas in praise of the Saṅgha) for describing a noble person's status as a supreme field of merit for the world.

puññakkhetta

"a field of merit" — puñña (merit, wholesome kamma) + khetta (field); the standard agricultural metaphor for a worthy recipient of gifts and offerings, whose own purity of mind is what makes an offering to them especially fruitful, exactly as fertile soil makes a planted seed especially productive.

Three Grades of Stream-Enterer, and the Once-Returner

Beyond this general accomplishment, specific distinctions follow according to the sharpness of faculty and insight each noble person brought to their attainment. Having developed the wisdom of the first path with dull faculties and weak insight, a person is called "at most seven times more" (sattakkhattuparama), destined to wander among fortunate rebirths at most seven more times before making an end of suffering. With middling faculties and insight, one is called a "clan-to-clan" (kolaṃkola) stream-enterer, wandering through no more than two or three further clan-lineages. And with sharp faculties and insight, one is called a "single-seed" (ekabījī) stream-enterer, producing only a single further human existence before ending suffering. Having developed the second path's wisdom, one simply becomes a once-returner, coming back to this world only once more before making an end of suffering.

Pali Source

Visesato panettha paṭhamamaggapaññaṃ tāva bhāvetvā mandāya vipassanāya āgato mudindriyopi sattakkhattuparamo nāma hoti, sattasugatibhave saṃsaritvā dukkhassantaṃ karoti. Majjhimāya vipassanāya āgato majjhimindriyo kolaṃkolo nāma hoti, dve vā tīṇi vā kulāni sandhāvitvā saṃsaritvā dukkhassantaṃ karoti. Tikkhāya vipassanāya āgato tikkhindriyo ekabījī nāma hoti, ekaññeva mānusakaṃ bhavaṃ nibbattetvā dukkhassantaṃ karoti.

Dutiyamaggapaññaṃ bhāvetvā sakadāgāmī nāma hoti, sakideva imaṃ lokaṃ āgantvā dukkhassantaṃ karoti.

Ru-Yi Translation

More specifically here: having developed, first, the wisdom of the first path, one who has arrived by way of dull insight, with dull faculties too, is called "at most seven times more"; having wandered on and circled through seven fortunate states of existence, he makes an end of suffering. One who has arrived by way of middling insight, with middling faculties, is called "clan-to-clan"; having wandered on and circled through two or three [further] clans, he makes an end of suffering. One who has arrived by way of sharp insight, with sharp faculties, is called "single-seed"; having produced only a single human existence, he makes an end of suffering.

Having developed the wisdom of the second path, one is called a once-returner; having come back to this world only once, he makes an end of suffering.

Reading note — Ru-Yi

This threefold subdivision of the stream-enterer — the first time this project's translation has encountered these three specific technical names in full — reveals that "stream-enterer" is not itself a single undifferentiated category but spans a real range of remaining distance to travel, entirely determined by the sharpness of faculty and insight the practitioner brought to that very first breakthrough. It is worth noting explicitly what does and does not vary across the three: all three have equally and completely eliminated the very same three lower fetters (personality-view, doubt, misapprehension of rules and observances, as established back in chapter twenty-two) — none is "more of a stream-enterer" than the others in terms of what has been abandoned. What varies is purely the remaining distance to full liberation, measured in maximum possible further rebirths: seven, versus two or three, versus exactly one.

The name "clan-to-clan" (kolaṃkola) deserves a moment's clarification, since its literal meaning (kula, "clan" or "family," reduplicated) is not self-explanatory in translation: it refers to being reborn into two or three further family lineages — not necessarily within the same realm or even the same general social status — before the final liberating breakthrough, a slightly less restrictive designation than the seven-rebirth ceiling of the "at most seven times more" stream-enterer, but a meaningfully less advanced position than the "single-seed" stream-enterer's guarantee of just one further human birth.

sattakkhattuparama

"at most seven times more" — satta (seven) + khattuṃ (times) + parama (utmost, at most); the stream-enterer of dullest faculties, guaranteed final liberation within at most seven further rebirths among fortunate destinies.

kolaṃkola

"clan-to-clan" — kola/kula (clan, family) reduplicated; the middling-faculty stream-enterer, passing through no more than two or three further family lineages before liberation.

ekabījī

"single-seed" — eka (one) + bīja (seed) + ī (possessive suffix); the sharpest-faculty stream-enterer, producing only one further human existence, like a single seed yielding exactly one more sprouting, before making an end of suffering.

Five Kinds of Non-Returner

The third path's wisdom produces the non-returner, who, by the specific degree of faculty involved, divides into five distinct destinies, each attaining final Nibbāna in the Pure Abodes rather than returning to this world: one who attains final Nibbāna in the interval, having been reborn in some Pure Abode plane, before reaching the midpoint of that life-span; one who attains it just past the midpoint; one who generates the higher path without exertion, effortlessly; one who generates it with exertion, through deliberate effort; and one whose stream flows upward, rising through successive Pure Abode planes all the way to the highest, Akaniṭṭha, before finally attaining Nibbāna there.

Pali Source

Tatiyamaggapaññaṃ bhāvetvā anāgāmī nāma hoti. So indriyavemattatāvasena antarāparinibbāyī, upahaccaparinibbāyī, asaṅkhāraparinibbāyī, sasaṅkhāraparinibbāyī, uddhaṃsoto akaniṭṭhagāmīti pañcadhā. Idha vihāyaniṭṭho hoti. Tattha antarāparinibbāyīti yattha katthaci suddhāvāsabhave upapajjitvā āyuvemajjhaṃ appatvāva parinibbāyati. Upahaccaparinibbāyīti āyuvemajjhaṃ atikkamitvā parinibbāyati. Asaṅkhāraparinibbāyīti asaṅkhārena appayogena uparimaggaṃ nibbatteti. Sasaṅkhāraparinibbāyīti sasaṅkhārena sappayogena uparimaggaṃ nibbatteti. Uddhaṃsoto akaniṭṭhagāmīti yatthupapanno, tato uddhaṃ yāva akaniṭṭhabhavā āruyha tattha parinibbāyati.

Ru-Yi Translation

Having developed the wisdom of the third path, one is called a non-returner. By way of the variation in faculty, he is fivefold: one who attains final Nibbāna in the interval; one who attains final Nibbāna just past [the midpoint]; one who attains final Nibbāna without exertion; one who attains final Nibbāna with exertion; and one whose stream flows upward, going to the Akaniṭṭha [plane]. His final destiny is completed here, [in the Pure Abodes, never returning to this world]. Herein, "one who attains final Nibbāna in the interval" means one who, having been reborn in whatever Pure Abode plane, attains final Nibbāna without yet having reached the midpoint of that life-span. "One who attains final Nibbāna just past [the midpoint]" means one who attains final Nibbāna having passed the midpoint of the life-span. "One who attains final Nibbāna without exertion" means one who generates the higher path without exertion, without deliberate effort. "One who attains final Nibbāna with exertion" means one who generates the higher path with exertion, with deliberate effort. "One whose stream flows upward, going to Akaniṭṭha" means one who, having been reborn in whatever plane, rises upward from there all the way to the plane of Akaniṭṭha, and attains final Nibbāna there.

Reading note — Ru-Yi

This fivefold non-returner scheme rewards being visualized as a set of distinct trajectories through the five Pure Abode planes (Suddhāvāsa) reserved exclusively for non-returners — planes this project's own earlier chapters on the cosmological structure of existence have already located at the very summit of the fine-material realm. The first two varieties differ only in timing within a single rebirth (attaining the final breakthrough to arahantship before or after the midpoint of that Pure Abode life-span); the middle two differ in manner rather than timing (whether the final push to arahantship requires deliberate effort or arises spontaneously); and the fifth and most distinctive variety involves an entirely different trajectory altogether — not settling into a single Pure Abode plane at all, but rising progressively upward through several of them in succession until reaching Akaniṭṭha, the very highest, before the final breakthrough occurs there.

None of these five variations reflects any difference in what has actually been abandoned — every non-returner, by definition, has fully eliminated all five lower fetters, exactly as chapter twenty-two's own fetter-mapping established. What varies here is purely the remaining trajectory toward the fourth and final path, a genuinely fine-grained taxonomy that testifies to how seriously the tradition took the project of mapping every possible remaining variation in the noble ones' progress toward complete liberation, right down to relatively minor questions of timing and effort within an already-guaranteed final outcome.

suddhāvāsa

the Pure Abodes — suddha (pure) + āvāsa (abode, dwelling); five fine-material realms reserved exclusively for non-returners, where all five varieties named in this passage complete their final journey to arahantship without ever again returning to the sense-desire world.

asaṅkhāraparinibbāyī, sasaṅkhāraparinibbāyī

one who attains final Nibbāna without exertion, and with exertion — a (without) / sa (with) + saṅkhāra (deliberate effort, formation) + parinibbāyī (one who attains final Nibbāna); distinguishes whether the final path to arahantship arises spontaneously or requires deliberate striving.

Akaniṭṭha

the highest of the five Pure Abode planes — a (not) + kaniṭṭha (youngest, lowest); literally "not the lowest" or, by extension, "the highest," the final destination of the "upward-streaming" non-returner before attaining final Nibbāna there.

The Various Arahants; Untangling the Tangle

The fourth and highest path's wisdom, finally, produces the arahant — who, depending on their own individual development, may be called "faith-liberated," "wisdom-liberated," "liberated in both ways," a master of the threefold higher knowledge, a possessor of the six direct-knowledges, or one who has attained the distinct analytical knowledges — every one of them a great destroyer of the taints. It is at the moment of this fourth path that the great tangle of craving is finally untangled; at the moment of its fruit, the tangle now fully untangled, the arahant becomes the foremost recipient of offerings for the world together with its gods. A closing verse then sums up the entire subhead: since the noble ones' wisdom yields so many varied benefits, the discerning person should take delight in its cultivation.

Pali Source

Catutthamaggapaññaṃ bhāvetvā koci saddhāvimutto hoti, koci paññāvimutto hoti, koci ubhatobhāgavimutto hoti, koci tevijjo, koci chaḷabhiñño, koci paṭisambhidappabhedappatto mahākhīṇāsavo. Yaṃ sandhāya vuttaṃ "maggakkhaṇe panesa taṃ jaṭaṃ vijaṭeti nāma. Phalakkhaṇe vijaṭitajaṭo sadevakassa lokassa aggadakkhiṇeyyo hotī"ti.

"Evaṃ anekānisaṃsā, ariyapaññāya bhāvanā; Yasmā tasmā kareyyātha, ratiṃ tattha vicakkhaṇo."

Ru-Yi Translation

Having developed the wisdom of the fourth path, one becomes, some, faith-liberated; some, wisdom-liberated; some, liberated in both ways; some, possessed of the threefold higher knowledge; some, possessed of the six direct-knowledges; some, one who has attained the distinct analytical knowledges — every one of them a great destroyer of the taints. With reference to which it was said: "at the moment of the path, this one is said to untangle that tangle; at the moment of the fruit, the tangle now untangled, he becomes the foremost recipient of offerings for the world together with its gods."

"Thus manifold are the benefits of the development of noble wisdom; therefore, the discerning person should take delight in it."

Reading note — Ru-Yi

The six-fold list of arahant-types given here is not a hierarchy of superior and inferior arahantship — every one of them has, in the exact same complete sense, destroyed every taint — but a classification by which additional accomplishments, beyond the bare fact of liberation itself, a given arahant happens to also possess. "Faith-liberated" and "wisdom-liberated" describe two different dominant approaches on the path to that same liberation (already glimpsed briefly in this project's chapter twenty-one, among the seven kinds of noble persons); "liberated in both ways" adds mastery of the immaterial jhānas to liberation by wisdom; "the threefold higher knowledge" (recollection of past lives, the divine eye, and the knowledge of the destruction of the taints) and "the six direct-knowledges" (adding psychic powers and the divine ear to that same threefold set) name specific supernormal accomplishments already catalogued in this project's own chapters twelve and thirteen; and "the distinct analytical knowledges" refers to the four paṭisambhidā already defined, with their own conditions for attainment, back in chapter fourteen's opening subhead on the varieties of wisdom.

The "untangling the tangle" (jaṭaṃ vijaṭeti) image deserves to be recognized as a direct echo of a specific, famous canonical verse — the Buddha's own reply to a deva's question about who can untangle the tangle of craving that binds beings both within and without — and its reappearance here, at the very close of the fourth benefit's own subhead, ties this chapter's entire technical apparatus back to one of the tradition's oldest and most memorable images for what liberation actually accomplishes: not an escape to some other place, but the patient, complete disentangling of a knot that had seemed, from the inside, simply too complicated ever to fully undo.

Chapter Twenty-Three's Own Close: The Verse That Framed the Whole Treatise

With the fourth benefit now fully explained, the chapter — and, with it, the entire twenty-three-chapter numbered body of the Visuddhimagga itself — draws to its formal close by returning to a single verse: the same verse, Buddhaghosa notes, that framed the whole treatise from its very beginning, on virtue, concentration, and wisdom. Having now explained this verse in full, by way of virtue, concentration, and wisdom, the development of wisdom together with all its benefits stands fully illuminated, and the chapter closes with the treatise's own formal colophon naming this the twenty-third chapter.

Ettāvatā ca –

"Sīle patiṭṭhāya naro sapañño, cittaṃ paññañca bhāvayaṃ; Ātāpī nipako bhikkhu, so imaṃ vijaṭaye jaṭanti." –

Imissā gāthāya sīlasamādhipaññāmukhena desite visuddhimagge sānisaṃsā paññābhāvanā paridīpitā hotīti.

Paññābhāvanānisaṃsaniddeso nāma

Ru-Yi Translation

By this much —

"Established in virtue, a person of wisdom, developing mind and wisdom, ardent and prudent, a monk — he untangles this tangle." —

by way of this verse, in the Path of Purification taught by way of the heading of virtue, concentration, and wisdom, the development of wisdom, together with its benefits, has been fully illuminated.

Thus, in the Path of Purification, composed for the joy of good people,
in the section on the development of wisdom,
the discussion called "The Description of the Benefits of Developing Wisdom"
constitutes the twenty-third chapter.

Reading note — Ru-Yi

This single closing verse is one of the most quietly significant sentences in the entire treatise, because it is the very verse the Visuddhimagga opened with — the same "established in virtue, a person of wisdom... he untangles this tangle" that this project's own very first chapter translated at the start of the whole undertaking, drawn originally from the Saṃyutta Nikāya's own account of a deva's question to the Buddha about who can untangle the tangle both within and without. Buddhaghosa's decision to structure his entire twenty-three-chapter treatise as nothing more than an extended commentary on this single four-line verse — virtue in chapters one and two, concentration in chapters three through thirteen, and wisdom in chapters fourteen through this very chapter twenty-three — is made fully explicit only here, at the last possible moment, in the sentence "by way of this verse... the Path of Purification taught by way of the heading of virtue, concentration, and wisdom... has been fully illuminated." Every chapter this project has translated, across many months, has in a real sense simply been unpacking four lines of poetry in ever-increasing technical detail.

The formal colophon marking this as "the twenty-third chapter" would, in many other multi-chapter Pali works, be the very last line of the text. It is worth pausing here, before continuing, to recognize what has just been completed: this is the end of the Visuddhimagga's own numbered chapter-sequence, the structure this entire translation project has followed since chapter one. But Buddhaghosa's own text does not end here — a further, separate section follows immediately in the source, outside the twenty-three-chapter numbering altogether, which the source itself titles "Nigamanakathā," the concluding discussion — an epilogue in the author's own voice, closing out not just this chapter but the entire treatise as a physical, historical work of literature. That epilogue, including Buddhaghosa's own famous self-description, is translated in full in the sections that follow.

The Epilogue Begins: Recalling the Promise Made at the Start

Outside the numbered chapter-sequence altogether, Buddhaghosa now speaks in his own voice for the treatise's true close. He repeats, once more, the very same verse just quoted, and then recalls the verses with which he himself had originally set the whole undertaking in motion — verses promising to expound the meaning of virtue and the rest, faithfully, for the sake of those who, having gained the rare opportunity of going-forth in the Conqueror's dispensation, wish for purification but do not truly know it despite their efforts, and inviting all who desire purity to listen with careful attention. He then declares, in his own voice, that this promise has now been discharged — the exposition of the meaning of virtue and the rest, following exactly the method of the commentaries of the five Nikāyas, gathering their substance together and freed from every fault of confused admixture, has indeed been spoken.

Pali Source

Ettāvatā ca –

"Sīle patiṭṭhāya naro sapañño, cittaṃ paññañca bhāvayaṃ; Ātāpī nipako bhikkhu, so imaṃ vijaṭaye jaṭa"nti. –

Imaṃ gāthaṃ nikkhipitvā yadavocumha –

"Imissā dāni gāthāya, kathitāya mahesinā; Vaṇṇayanto yathābhūtaṃ, atthaṃ sīlādibhedanaṃ.

Sudullabhaṃ labhitvāna, pabbajjaṃ jinasāsane; Sīlādisaṅgahaṃ khemaṃ, ujuṃ maggaṃ visuddhiyā.

Yathābhūtaṃ ajānantā, suddhikāmāpi ye idha; Visuddhiṃ nādhigacchanti, vāyamantāpi yogino.

Tesaṃ pāmojjakaraṇaṃ, suvisuddhavinicchayaṃ; Mahāvihāravāsīnaṃ, desanānayanissitaṃ.

Visuddhimaggaṃ bhāsissaṃ, taṃ me sakkacca bhāsato; Visuddhikāmā sabbepi, nisāmayatha sādhavo"ti.

Svāyaṃ bhāsito hoti.

Tattha ca –

Tesaṃ sīlādibhedānaṃ, atthānaṃ yo vinicchayo; Pañcannampi nikāyānaṃ, vutto aṭṭhakathānaye.

Samāharitvā taṃ sabbaṃ, yebhuyyena sanicchayo; Sabbasaṅkaradosehi, mutto yasmā pakāsito.

Tasmā visuddhikāmehi, suddhapaññehi yogihi; Visuddhimagge etasmiṃ, karaṇīyova ādaroti.

Ru-Yi Translation

By this much —

"Established in virtue, a person of wisdom, developing mind and wisdom, ardent and prudent, a monk — he untangles this tangle." —

having set down this verse, this is what we said —

"Now, expounding, as it truly is, the meaning of the divisions beginning with virtue, of this very verse spoken by the Great Sage,

and, for the sake of those who, having obtained a going-forth, exceedingly hard to obtain, in the Conqueror's dispensation — a going-forth that gathers together virtue and the rest, secure and a straight path to purification —

yet, not knowing it as it truly is, though they here desire purity and are meditators making the effort, do not attain purification,

for the sake of producing joy in them, [I shall speak] a well-purified determination, resting on the method of exposition of the dwellers of the Great Monastery,

I shall speak the Path of Purification. As I speak it carefully, may all of you who desire purity, good people, listen attentively." —

Thus was it spoken.

And here —

that determination of the meanings of those divisions beginning with virtue, as stated in the method of the commentaries of all five Nikāyas,

having gathered all of that together, is, for the most part, a determination that, because it has been made known freed from every fault of confused admixture,

therefore, by meditators who desire purity, whose wisdom is pure, careful attention should indeed be paid to this very Path of Purification.

Reading note — Ru-Yi

This is a genuinely moving piece of authorial bookending, and it deserves to be recognized as such: Buddhaghosa is not simply summarizing what has been covered — he is literally quoting his own opening promise back to himself and the reader, verse for verse, in order to formally certify that the promise has been kept. The original verses, composed before a single chapter of doctrine had been expounded, described a very specific, sympathetic target audience: not scholars seeking an intellectual survey, but sincere renunciants who have "obtained a going-forth, exceedingly hard to obtain," and who are already "making the effort" yet still failing to reach purification simply because they "do not know it as it truly is." The entire twenty-three-chapter treatise, on this telling, was never primarily a work of scholarship for its own sake, but a practical rescue mission for earnest practitioners stuck exactly where clarity, not more effort, was what they actually needed.

The declaration of method — "gathered together... from the commentaries of all five Nikāyas... freed from every fault of confused admixture" — is a significant historical and doctrinal claim in its own right, asserting that nothing in this vast treatise represents Buddhaghosa's own private innovation or a mixture of incompatible traditions, but a careful synthesis of the existing Sinhalese commentarial tradition (the Mahāvihāra's own accumulated exegesis on all five Nikāyas) into a single, internally consistent whole. This claim of fidelity to inherited tradition, rather than personal originality, is entirely of a piece with the "copyright discipline" this translation project has itself observed throughout — working directly from primary and traditional sources, never introducing external modern interpretive frameworks.

Key vocabulary — for Pāli learners
sudullabhaṃ pabbajjaṃ

"a going-forth exceedingly hard to obtain" — su (very) + dullabha (hard to obtain) + pabbajjā (the going-forth into monastic life); a phrase emphasizing the rarity and preciousness of monastic ordination itself, the starting-point this epilogue identifies as the treatise's original motivating concern.

pañca nikāyā

the five [Nikāya] collections — the Dīgha, Majjhima, Saṃyutta, Aṅguttara, and Khuddaka Nikāyas, whose entire body of accumulated commentarial tradition (aṭṭhakathā) Buddhaghosa here claims to have synthesized into this single treatise.

sabbasaṅkaradosa

"every fault of confused admixture" — sabba (every) + saṅkara (mixing, confusion, mongrelization) + dosa (fault); the specific defect Buddhaghosa claims his synthesis has avoided — an incoherent jumbling-together of incompatible commentarial traditions or schools.

A Dedication: The Elder Saṅghapāla, and the Merit of This Work

A formal dedicatory verse-sequence follows, naming the specific circumstances of the treatise's composition: it was undertaken at the request (ajjhesana) of the elder Saṅghapāla — a monk of the foremost lineage of the Theriya (Elder) tradition, a dweller of the Mahāvihāra, a monk of pure conduct devoted to strict practice, skilled in monastic discipline, and adorned with such qualities as patience, gentleness, and loving-kindness. Buddhaghosa dedicates whatever accumulation of merit he has gained through undertaking this work, out of a wish for the enduring stability of the true Dhamma, to the wellbeing and happiness of all living beings.

Pali Source

Vibhajjavādiseṭṭhānaṃ, theriyānaṃ yasassinaṃ; Mahāvihāravāsīnaṃ, vaṃsajassa vibhāvino.

Bhadantasaṅghapālassa, sucisallekhavuttino; Vinayācārayuttassa, yuttassa paṭipattiyaṃ.

Khantisoraccamettādi-guṇabhūsitacetaso; Ajjhesanaṃ gahetvāna, karontena imaṃ mayā.

Saddhammaṭṭhitikāmena, yo patto puññasañcayo; Tassa tejena sabbepi, sukhamedhantu pāṇino.

Ru-Yi Translation

Of the foremost of the Vibhajjavādins, of the renowned Theriyas, of the dwellers of the Great Monastery, of a discerning one born into that lineage —

the venerable Saṅghapāla, of pure conduct, devoted to strict practice, endowed with monastic discipline and proper conduct, one who is fitting in his practice,

whose mind is adorned with such qualities as patience, gentleness, and loving-kindness — having received his request, and, by me, making this [treatise] —

whatever accumulation of merit has been attained, by one desiring the enduring stability of the true Dhamma, by the power of that, may all living beings flourish in happiness.

Reading note — Ru-Yi

These four verses supply a rare and historically valuable piece of concrete information amid a treatise otherwise almost entirely focused on doctrine and practice rather than its own circumstances of composition: the Visuddhimagga, we learn here directly from its own author, was not a purely self-motivated undertaking but was composed at the specific request of a named individual, the elder Saṅghapāla, described with considerable warmth and specificity — "of pure conduct," "devoted to strict practice" (sallekha, an ascetic term already familiar from this project's own earlier treatment of the dhutaṅga practices in chapter two), "skilled in monastic discipline," and possessed of patience, gentleness, and loving-kindness. The identification of both Saṅghapāla and, by clear implication, Buddhaghosa himself as belonging to "the foremost of the Vibhajjavādins, the renowned Theriyas, the dwellers of the Great Monastery" is a significant piece of historical self-positioning, situating the entire work within a specific lineage (the "Analysts," an early self-designation of the Theravāda tradition) and a specific institution (the Mahāvihāra monastery in Anurādhapura, Sri Lanka) whose own commentarial tradition this treatise claims, as the previous section stated, to faithfully synthesize.

The dedication of merit in the final verse — "may all living beings flourish in happiness" — follows a pattern this translation project has itself observed at the close of numerous other works in the broader canon and commentarial tradition: the wholesome kamma generated by a significant undertaking is not kept for the author's own private benefit alone but is explicitly shared outward, for the benefit of all beings, motivated here specifically "by one desiring the enduring stability of the true Dhamma" — a wish that this very treatise, composed for that purpose some fifteen centuries ago, has been remarkably, verifiably fulfilled by, given that it remains, to this day, one of the single most-read and most-taught works in the entire Theravāda tradition.

Key vocabulary — for Pāli learners
Vibhajjavādin

"an Analyst," a proponent of analytical teaching — vibhajja (having analyzed, distinguished) + vāda (doctrine, teaching); an early self-designation of the tradition ancestral to today's Theravāda, emphasizing careful, distinction-drawing exposition rather than sweeping, undifferentiated claims — a description this entire treatise, with its relentless careful subdivision of every topic, exemplifies at every turn.

ajjhesana

a request, an invitation — from adhi + iṣ (to request, invoke); the formal term for Saṅghapāla's own request that set this entire treatise in motion, a detail of composition-history this project's own reading of the text would otherwise have had no way of knowing.

sallekhavutti

"devoted to strict/ascetic practice" — sallekha (scraping away, austerity — the same term underlying the thirteen dhutaṅga ascetic practices this project's chapter two translated in detail) + vutti (conduct, manner of living); describes the elder Saṅghapāla's own personal practice.

The Treatise's Length, and a Wish for All Beings

A brief further verse-pair records the completed treatise's own measured length — fifty-eight recitation-sections by the reckoning of the text, finished without obstruction — before closing with a final wish that, exactly as this treatise has now reached completion, so too may all of the world's wholesome aspirations be fulfilled, swiftly and without hindrance, for every being.

Pali Source

Visuddhimaggo eso ca, antarāyaṃ vinā idha; Niṭṭhito aṭṭhapaññāsa-bhāṇavārāya pāḷiyā.

Yathā tatheva lokassa, sabbe kalyāṇanissitā; Anantarāyā ijjhantu, sīghaṃ sīghaṃ manorathāti.

Ru-Yi Translation

And this Path of Purification, here, without obstruction, is finished, at fifty-eight recitation-sections by the reckoning of the text.

Just so, and in the very same way, may all of the world's aspirations grounded in what is wholesome be fulfilled, without obstruction, swiftly, swiftly.

Reading note — Ru-Yi

The specific figure given here — fifty-eight bhāṇavāras, traditional "recitation-sections" each representing roughly the amount of text a reciter could deliver in a single sustained oral performance — is a small but concrete reminder of the entirely different technological and cultural conditions under which this treatise was originally composed, memorized, and transmitted: not as a book to be silently read alone, as this very webpage now allows, but as a work meant to be recited aloud, section by section, within a living oral tradition of monastic teaching and memorization that predates, by many centuries, its eventual committal to any written form.

The closing wish — that the world's wholesome aspirations be fulfilled "swiftly, swiftly" (sīghaṃ sīghaṃ), the doubled word itself conveying urgency — is a graceful, generous final note before the treatise's single most personal and historically distinctive passage: Buddhaghosa's own self-description, given in the very next section, which the tradition has preserved and transmitted alongside the treatise itself for fifteen centuries as one of the only sources of biographical information about its celebrated author.

Key vocabulary — for Pāli learners
bhāṇavāra

a recitation-section — bhāṇa (recitation, speaking) + vāra (turn, section); the traditional unit for measuring a text's length by how much could be recited aloud in one sustained session, here recording the entire Visuddhimagga's own length as fifty-eight such sections.

manoratha

an aspiration, a wish — manas (mind) + ratha (chariot); literally "a chariot of the mind," a vivid image for a heartfelt wish or aspiration one's mind travels toward, here extended in blessing to all beings in the treatise's own closing lines.

Buddhaghosa's Own Self-Description

The treatise now offers what is, alongside a handful of remarks scattered across his other commentarial works, one of the very few pieces of information the author of the Visuddhimagga ever gives about himself — a single, extraordinarily long and ornately constructed Pali sentence, composed entirely in the third person, describing the qualities of the elder who made this work, and closing with the name by which he came to be known: Buddhaghosa, "the Voice of the Buddha." This is among the most-cited passages in the whole of Pali literature, and it is translated here with particular care, working through its long chain of descriptive compounds one by one rather than compressing them.

Pali Source

Parama visuddha saddhā buddhi vīriya paṭimaṇḍitena sīlācārajjava maddavādiguṇasamudayasamuditena sakasamaya samayantaragahanajjhogāhaṇasamatthena paññāveyyattiyasamannāgatena tipiṭakapariyattibhede sāṭṭhakathe satthusāsane appaṭihatañāṇappabhāvena mahāveyyākaraṇena karaṇasampattijanitasukhaviniggatamadhurodāravacanalāvaṇṇayuttena yuttamuttavādinā vādīvarena mahākavinā chaḷabhiññāpaṭisambhidādi bhedaguṇapaṭimaṇḍite uttarimanussadhamme appaṭihatabuddhīnaṃ theravaṃsappadīpānaṃ therānaṃ mahāvihāravāsīnaṃ vaṃsālaṅkārabhūtena vipulavisuddhabuddhinā buddhaghosoti garūhi gahitanāmadheyyena therena mudantakhedakavattabbena kato visuddhimaggo nāma.

Ru-Yi Translation

This work called the Path of Purification was made by the elder — adorned with supremely pure faith, intelligence, and energy; possessed of an accumulation of virtues beginning with moral virtue, good conduct, uprightness, and gentleness; capable of penetrating into the thicket of his own school's doctrine and of other schools' doctrines; endowed with clarity of wisdom; possessed of an unobstructed power of knowledge with respect to the Teacher's dispensation, in its division into the Three Baskets of scripture together with their commentaries; a great grammarian; endowed with the charm of sweet, noble speech, born of an accomplishment of the organs of articulation and issuing forth with ease; a speaker of fitting and eloquent words; a foremost debater; a great poet; the very ornament, become manifest, of the lineage of the elders — those elders of unobstructed intelligence with regard to the superhuman states, adorned with such distinct qualities as the six direct-knowledges and the analytical knowledges, lamps of the elder-lineage, dwellers of the Great Monastery; of vast and pure intelligence; who received, from his own teachers, the name "Buddhaghosa" — by this elder, who is to be spoken of with both joy and toil.

Reading note — Ru-Yi

This single sentence, syntactically, is a chain of instrumental-case compounds all converging on one final noun, "therena" ("by the elder") — a construction typical of the ornate, high-register praśasti style used across South Asian literary traditions for formal self- or patron-description, and it repays being unpacked piece by piece rather than absorbed as a single blur of praise. Notice how the qualities move through a clear, deliberate sequence: personal virtues first (faith, intelligence, energy, moral conduct, gentleness), then scholarly and doctrinal command (penetrating both his own and rival schools' teachings, unobstructed knowledge of the entire Tipiṭaka with its commentaries), then a cluster of specifically literary and rhetorical accomplishments (a great grammarian, a speaker of sweet and noble words, a foremost debater, a great poet) that might surprise a reader expecting only meditative or doctrinal self-praise, and finally a return to the specifically monastic and spiritual — membership in, and status as "the ornament of," the lineage of the Mahāvihāra's own elders, themselves already described as adorned with the six direct-knowledges and the analytical knowledges.

The closing phrase — "who is to be spoken of with both joy and toil" (mudantakhedakavattabbena) — is genuinely difficult to render with full confidence, and honesty about that difficulty seems more valuable here than false certainty: the compound appears to combine mudā (joy, gladness) with kheda (weariness, exhaustion, toil), suggesting that the elder who accomplished this work should be remembered with a blend of delighted admiration and a due acknowledgment of the sheer labor the undertaking required — a fittingly human final note to attach to a treatise that has, across twenty-three chapters, catalogued the entire path to liberation in such exhaustive, uncompressed detail. Whatever the precise nuance, the name given in the sentence's final clause — Buddhaghosa, "Voice of the Buddha" — is the name under which this entire work, and its author's entire remaining commentarial corpus, has been known and studied ever since.

Key vocabulary — for Pāli learners
Buddhaghosa

"Voice of the Buddha" — buddha (the Awakened One) + ghosa (voice, sound, proclamation); the name given to the treatise's author by his own teachers, according to this very passage, and the name under which this entire commentarial corpus has been transmitted and studied for roughly fifteen centuries.

sakasamaya, samayantara

one's own school's doctrine, and other schools' doctrine — saka (one's own) / samaya-antara (a different doctrinal system) + samaya (doctrine, tenet, from Sanskrit siddhānta-adjacent usage); together, "gahana," a thicket or jungle — describing the elder's capacity to navigate the complex, sometimes thorny terrain of doctrinal disputes both within and beyond his own tradition.

karaṇasampattijanitasukhaviniggata

"born of an accomplishment of the organs of articulation, and issuing forth with ease" — karaṇa (the physical organs/instruments of speech-articulation, a term from Indian phonetic theory) + sampatti (accomplishment, perfection) + sukha (ease) + viniggata (issued forth); a technical, almost clinical description of eloquence as arising from a physically perfected vocal apparatus rather than merely from cleverness of thought.

The Final Words: "The Treatise of the Path of Purification Is Finished"

The Visuddhimagga's very last verses close the entire work with a wish for its own endurance: may this treatise remain in the world, showing noble sons the way to purification through virtue and the rest, for as long as the name of the Buddha himself — the world's greatest, the Great Sage, of purified mind, such in his nature — continues to be spoken of among beings. Two final lines of prose then record the work's length once more and pronounce, in five words, the closing of the entire multi-year composition: the Visuddhimagga is finished.

Pali Source

Tāva tiṭṭhatu lokasmiṃ, lokanittharaṇesinaṃ; Dassento kulaputtānaṃ, nayaṃ sīlādisuddhiyā.

Yāva buddhoti nāmampi, suddhacittassa tādino; Lokamhi lokajeṭṭhassa, pavattati mahesinoti.

Iti sādhujanapāmojjatthāya katā visuddhimaggakathā,
Pāḷigaṇanāya pana sā aṭṭhapaññāsabhāṇavārā hotīti.
Visuddhimaggapakaraṇaṃ niṭṭhitaṃ.

Ru-Yi Translation

May this remain in the world, for so long, for those seeking to cross beyond the world, showing noble sons the way to purification through virtue and the rest —

for as long as even the name "Buddha," of the purified-minded one, such in his nature, the world's greatest, the Great Sage, continues to be spoken of in the world.

Thus is the discourse on the Path of Purification, composed for the joy of good people;
and, by the reckoning of the text, it comes to fifty-eight recitation-sections.
The Treatise of the Path of Purification is finished.

Reading note — Ru-Yi

The final wish — that this treatise endure for as long as the Buddha's own name continues to be spoken in the world — is, on the evidence of roughly fifteen centuries of continuous transmission, study, and practice across the entire Theravāda world, a wish that has been granted about as completely as any author could reasonably hope. The image of "those seeking to cross beyond the world" (lokanittharaṇesinaṃ) closes the treatise with the same crossing-imagery this project has met repeatedly across its final chapters — the canal-crossing simile of chapter twenty-two's opening, the far-shore imagery of its boat simile, the two-travelers-on-an-unfamiliar-road simile of this very chapter's own cessation-attainment subhead — confirming, one last time, that the entire Visuddhimagga has never been offering abstract philosophy for its own sake, but a practical map for an actual, difficult crossing every reader is implicitly invited to undertake themselves.

And with the five words of the closing colophon — "Visuddhimaggapakaraṇaṃ niṭṭhitaṃ," "the Treatise of the Path of Purification is finished" — Buddhaghosa's fifth-century masterwork reaches its own genuine end, a full 285 lines of source text after the twenty-third chapter itself began, and, for this website, the end of a translation project spanning twenty-three chapters and many months of work: virtue in chapters one and two, concentration in chapters three through thirteen, and wisdom, at last, in chapters fourteen through this closing chapter twenty-three — the whole path, from a monk's very first step in taking up the precepts to an arahant's own capacity to rest, at will, in the attainment of cessation itself, now rendered into English directly from the Pāli, in its entirety, for the first time on this site.
