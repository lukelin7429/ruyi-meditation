The Faculties and the Four Noble Truths

This page carries Ru-Yi's translation of the Visuddhimagga's chapter 16, Indriyasaccaniddesa ("The Discussion of the Faculties and the Truths") — completing the last individually-named categories of wisdom's "ground." The twenty-two faculties receive a compact treatment; the Four Noble Truths receive the chapter's full weight, worked through fourteen distinct analytical methods — from etymology and characteristic to a rigorous philosophical defense of Nibbāna's very existence, and an unflinching, graphic anatomy of birth's own suffering.

Twenty-Two Faculties: Why "Lordly," and in What Order

The sixteenth chapter turns from elements to faculties — twenty-two in all, spanning the five physical senses and mind, the two sexes, life itself, the five kinds of feeling, the five spiritual faculties of faith, energy, mindfulness, concentration, and wisdom, and three final supramundane faculties marking a practitioner's progress toward full liberation. A summarizing verse promises understanding by meaning, characteristic, order, division, function, and plane. The commentary opens by explaining "indriya" itself through a rich, five-fold etymological pun on "Inda" (Sakka, lord of the gods): these states bear the "mark" of the Lord's own supreme mastery, since wholesome and unwholesome kamma governs their sharpness or dullness the way a sovereign governs a domain; they were declared, seen, taught, and cultivated by the Buddha — the "spiritual lord" — through both direct experience and meditative development; and, above all, they exercise their own genuine dominance, since the eye and its companions demonstrably govern the sharpness or dullness of eye-consciousness and the rest within their own specific sphere.

Pali Source

Dhātūnaṃ anantaraṃ uddiṭṭhāni pana indriyānīti bāvīsatindriyāni – cakkhundriyaṃ sotindriyaṃ ghānindriyaṃ jivhindriyaṃ kāyindriyaṃ manindriyaṃ itthindriyaṃ purisindriyaṃ jīvitindriyaṃ sukhindriyaṃ dukkhindriyaṃ somanassindriyaṃ domanassindriyaṃ upekkhindriyaṃ saddhindriyaṃ vīriyindriyaṃ satindriyaṃ samādhindriyaṃ paññindriyaṃ anaññātaññassāmītindriyaṃ aññindriyaṃ aññātāvindriyanti. Tattha – atthato lakkhaṇādīhi, kamato ca vijāniyā; bhedābhedā tathā kiccā, bhūmito ca vinicchayaṃ.

Tattha cakkhādīnaṃ tāva cakkhatīti cakkhūtiādinā nayena attho pakāsito. Pacchimesu pana tīsu paṭhamaṃ pubbabhāge anaññātaṃ amataṃ padaṃ catusaccadhammaṃ vā jānissāmīti evaṃ paṭipannassa uppajjanato indriyaṭṭhasambhavato ca anaññātaññassāmītindriyanti vuttaṃ. Dutiyaṃ ājānanato indriyaṭṭhasambhavato ca aññindriyaṃ. Tatiyaṃ aññātāvino catūsu saccesu niṭṭhitaññāṇakiccassa khīṇāsavassa uppajjanato indriyaṭṭhasambhavato ca aññātāvindriyaṃ.

Ko pana nesaṃ indriyaṭṭho nāmāti? Indaliṅgaṭṭho indriyaṭṭho. Indadesitaṭṭho indriyaṭṭho. Indadiṭṭhaṭṭho indriyaṭṭho. Indasiṭṭhaṭṭho indriyaṭṭho. Indajuṭṭhaṭṭho indriyaṭṭho. So sabbopi idha yathāyogaṃ yujjati. Bhagavā hi sammāsambuddho paramissariyabhāvato indo. Kusalākusalañca kammaṃ, kammesu kassaci issariyābhāvato. Tenevettha kammasañjanitāni tāva indriyāni kusalākusalakammaṃ ulliṅgenti. Tena ca siṭṭhānīti indaliṅgaṭṭhena indasiṭṭhaṭṭhena ca indriyāni. Sabbāneva panetāni bhagavatā yathābhūtato pakāsitāni abhisambuddhāni cāti indadesitaṭṭhena indadiṭṭhaṭṭhena ca indriyāni. Teneva bhagavatā munindena kānici gocarāsevanāya kānici bhāvanāsevanāya sevitānīti indajuṭṭhaṭṭhenāpi indriyāni.

Apica ādhipaccasaṅkhātena issariyaṭṭhenāpi etāni indriyāni. Cakkhuviññāṇādippavattiyañhi cakkhādīnaṃ siddhaṃ ādhipaccaṃ, tasmiṃ tikkhe tikkhattā mande ca mandattāti. Ayaṃ tāvettha atthato vinicchayo.

Lakkhaṇādīhīti lakkhaṇarasapaccupaṭṭhānapadaṭṭhānehipi cakkhādīnaṃ vinicchayaṃ vijāniyāti attho. Tāni ca nesaṃ lakkhaṇādīni khandhaniddese vuttāneva. Paññindriyādīni hi cattāri atthato amohoyeva. Sesāni tattha sarūpeneva āgatāni.

Kamatoti ayampi desanākkamova. Tattha ajjhattadhamme pariññāya ariyabhūmipaṭilābho hotīti attabhāvapariyāpannāni cakkhundriyādīni paṭhamaṃ desitāni. So pana attabhāvo yaṃ dhammaṃ upādāya itthīti vā purisoti vā saṅkhaṃ gacchati, ayaṃ soti nidassanatthaṃ tato itthindriyaṃ purisindriyañca. So duvidhopi jīvitindriyapaṭibaddhavuttīti ñāpanatthaṃ tato jīvitindriyaṃ. Yāva tassa pavatti, tāva etesaṃ vedayitānaṃ anivatti. Yañca kiñci vedayitaṃ, sabbaṃ taṃ dukkhanti ñāpanatthaṃ tato sukhindriyādīni. Taṃnirodhatthaṃ pana ete dhammā bhāvetabbāti paṭipattidassanatthaṃ tato saddhādīni. Imāya paṭipattiyā esa dhammo paṭhamaṃ attani pātubhavatīti paṭipattiyā amoghabhāvadassanatthaṃ tato anaññātaññassāmītindriyaṃ. Tasseva phalattā tato anantaraṃ bhāvetabbato ca tato aññindriyaṃ. Tato paraṃ bhāvanāya imassa adhigamo, adhigate ca pana imasmiṃ natthi kiñci uttari karaṇīyanti ñāpanatthaṃ ante paramassāsabhūtaṃ aññātāvindriyaṃ desitanti ayamettha kamo.

Ru-Yi Translation

Immediately following the elements, the "faculties" that were listed are the twenty-two faculties: the eye-faculty, the ear-faculty, the nose-faculty, the tongue-faculty, the body-faculty, the mind-faculty, the femininity faculty, the masculinity faculty, the life faculty, the pleasure faculty, the pain faculty, the joy faculty, the displeasure faculty, the equanimity faculty, the faith faculty, the energy faculty, the mindfulness faculty, the concentration faculty, the wisdom faculty, the "I shall come to know the not-yet-known" faculty, the faculty of one who knows, and the faculty of one who has known. Herein: the determination should be known by meaning, characteristic, order, division, function, and plane.

Herein, for the eye and the rest, firstly, the meaning has already been made clear by the method: "it sees, therefore 'eye,'" and so on. As for the final three: the first is said to be the "I shall come to know the not-yet-known" faculty, since it arises in one who has thus entered upon the path, thinking, "I shall come to know the not-yet-known deathless state, or the doctrine of the four truths," in the preliminary stage — and by reason of its arising in the sense of "faculty." The second is the "faculty of one who knows," by reason of its actually knowing, and by reason of its arising in the sense of "faculty." The third is the "faculty of one who has known," by reason of its arising in one whose taints are exhausted, whose task of knowledge with respect to the four truths is complete, and one who has thus known — and by reason of its arising in the sense of "faculty."

But what, then, is this sense of "faculty"? It is the sense of bearing the Lord's own mark; the sense of being taught by the Lord; the sense of being seen by the Lord; the sense of being declared by the Lord; and the sense of being cultivated by the Lord. All of these apply here, each as is fitting. For the Blessed One, the Fully Awakened One, is "Lord" (Inda) by reason of his supreme sovereignty. And wholesome and unwholesome kamma, too, is a kind of lord, since no one else has any sovereignty over these faculties. Hence, the faculties produced by kamma bear the mark of wholesome or unwholesome kamma; and, having been so marked, they are "faculties" in the sense of bearing the Lord's mark, and in the sense of being declared by that Lord. And all of these were made known by the Blessed One exactly as they truly are, and were directly, fully awakened to by him — hence they are "faculties" in the sense of being taught by the Lord and seen by the Lord. And, by that same Blessed One, the Lord of Sages, some were cultivated by way of resorting to their domain, some by way of meditative development — hence they are "faculties" also in the sense of being cultivated by the Lord.

Moreover, these are "faculties" also in the sense of dominance, reckoned as sovereignty. For the eye and the rest have a demonstrated dominance in the occurrence of eye-consciousness and the rest, since, when the eye is sharp, eye-consciousness is sharp, and when it is dull, eye-consciousness is dull. This, firstly, is the determination by meaning here.

"By characteristic": the meaning is that the determination of the eye and the rest should also be understood by way of characteristic, function, manifestation, and proximate cause. And their characteristics and the rest are exactly as already stated in the discussion of the aggregates. For the four faculties beginning with the wisdom faculty are, in meaning, simply non-delusion. The rest have come down there under their own specific names.

"By order": this too is simply order of teaching. Herein, since it is by fully understanding internal states that the attainment of the noble planes comes about, the eye-faculty and the rest, comprised within one's own person, were taught first. And since that person, in dependence on some particular quality, comes to be reckoned "a woman" or "a man" — "this is such a one" — the femininity and masculinity faculties were taught next, to show this. And since both of these kinds of person occur in dependence on the life faculty, the life faculty was taught next. As long as it occurs, there is no cessation of these feelings — and, to make known that whatever is felt is suffering, the pleasure faculty and the rest were taught next. And, to show the practice by which these states are to be developed for the sake of their cessation, faith and the rest were taught next. And, to show that this practice does not fail, since this state first becomes manifest in oneself through this very practice, the "I shall come to know the not-yet-known" faculty was taught next. And, since it is the fruit of that very practice, and since it is to be developed immediately following it, the "faculty of one who knows" was taught next. And, to make known that its attainment comes about through further development beyond that, and that, once this is attained, there is nothing further left to be done, the "faculty of one who has known," the ultimate refuge, was taught last. This is the order here.

Reading note — Ru-Yi

The five-fold pun on "Inda" — applying the same root simultaneously to Sakka, to the Buddha, and to kamma itself — reveals that "faculty" in this technical sense is fundamentally about demonstrated sovereignty rather than mere presence: an eye is a "faculty" not simply because it exists, but because its own sharpness or dullness visibly governs the sharpness or dullness of the consciousness that depends on it, exactly as a king's own character shapes the character of everything under his rule.

The final three faculties' names — "I shall know the unknown," "one who knows," "one who has known" — trace a single practitioner's progress through exactly three temporal stances toward the same content (the four truths): anticipation before stream-entry, active knowing along the remaining three paths, and settled completion at Arahantship, showing that even a purely grammatical distinction of tense can be pressed into service as a precise map of spiritual attainment.

Key vocabulary — for Pāli learners
indriya

a faculty — from Inda (Sakka, chief of the gods); the twenty-two-fold scheme treated in this chapter, each item exercising a genuine, demonstrable dominance within its own specific domain of experience.

anaññātaññassāmītindriya

the "I shall come to know the not-yet-known" faculty — anaññāta (not yet known) + aññassāmi ("I shall know") + indriya; the faculty arising specifically at the moment of stream-entry, marking the practitioner's first direct penetration of the four truths.

issariyaṭṭha

the sense of sovereignty, dominance — issariya (lordship) + attha (sense, meaning); the culminating and most functionally important of the several senses of "indriya," describing the genuine governing influence a faculty exerts over its corresponding domain.

What Each Faculty Actually Does — and Which Plane of Existence It Belongs To

Only the life faculty admits of any internal division — material and immaterial varieties — while the remaining twenty-one are each single and undivided. Function receives careful, individualized treatment: the eye and its bodily companions each cause their corresponding consciousness to follow their own degree of sharpness or dullness; the mind faculty exercises mastery over its co-arisen states; the life faculty sustains them; the two sex faculties shape the specific marks, signs, occupation, and demeanor of masculinity or femininity; the four feeling faculties overpower co-arisen states with their own gross quality, while equanimity brings about a peaceful, refined, neutral quality instead; faith and its companions overcome their opposing defilements and bring associated states to a state of confidence and clarity; and the three final faculties each accomplish a specific, decisive task — abandoning the three lower fetters, attenuating the remaining defilements, and finally abandoning all eagerness in every task, becoming the very cause of being turned toward the deathless. The section closes with a plane-by-plane breakdown showing that seventeen faculties are restricted to the sense sphere alone, five span all four planes, one spans three, and the final three belong to the supramundane exclusively.

Ru-Yi Translation

"By division": only the life faculty has a division here. For it is of two kinds, material life faculty and immaterial life faculty. The rest are undivided. Thus should the determination by division be understood here.

"By function": if one asks, what is the function of the faculties? For the eye-faculty, firstly, since it is said, "the eye-base is a condition, by way of the faculty-condition, for the eye-consciousness-element and its associated states," its function is this: causing eye-consciousness and its associated states to follow its own condition of sharpness or dullness, by way of serving as a faculty-condition. So too for the ear, nose, tongue, and body. The mind-faculty's function, however, is exercising mastery over its co-arisen states. The life faculty's function is maintaining its co-arisen states. The femininity and masculinity faculties' function is producing the mode of the feminine or masculine mark, sign, occupation, and demeanor. The pleasure, pain, joy, and displeasure faculties' function is overpowering their co-arisen states and bringing about their own respective gross mode. The equanimity faculty's function is bringing about a peaceful, refined, neutral mode. Faith and the rest' function is overcoming their opposing defilements and bringing associated states to a state of confidence and the like. The "I shall come to know the not-yet-known" faculty's function is the abandoning of the three fetters, and bringing associated states to face that very abandoning. The "faculty of one who knows" function is the abandoning that attenuates sensual lust and ill will and the rest, and bringing its co-arisen states under its own control. The "faculty of one who has known" function is the abandoning of eagerness in every task, and being the condition for associated states' being turned toward the deathless. Thus should the determination by function be understood here.

"By plane": here, the eye, ear, nose, tongue, body, femininity, masculinity, pleasure, pain, and displeasure faculties are sense-sphere only. The mind, life, and equanimity faculties, together with the faith, energy, mindfulness, concentration, and wisdom faculties, are comprised within all four planes. The joy faculty is comprised within three planes, by way of the sense sphere, the fine-material sphere, and the supramundane. The final three faculties are supramundane only. Thus should the determination by plane also be understood here. For one who understands in this way: "the monk, full of a sense of urgency, established in restraint of the faculties, having fully understood the faculties, will make an end of suffering."

This is the detailed exposition of the faculties.

Reading note — Ru-Yi

The functional definitions given here consistently describe a faculty as exercising influence over its co-arisen companions, never as merely existing alongside them — the eye "causes eye-consciousness to follow its own sharpness or dullness," the mind faculty "exercises mastery," faith "brings associated states to confidence" — a vocabulary of active governance that runs through every single entry and gives the whole twenty-two-fold scheme its coherence as a genuine account of dominance, not just an inventory of parts.

The plane-distribution reveals a quiet asymmetry worth noticing: seventeen faculties never rise above the sense sphere at all, while only the five spiritual faculties plus mind, life, and equanimity span every plane of existence without exception — meaning that faith, energy, mindfulness, concentration, and wisdom are, structurally, the only qualities among all twenty-two capable of accompanying a being through every possible mode of existence, mundane or supramundane.

Key vocabulary — for Pāli learners
oḷārikākārānupāpana

bringing about a gross mode — oḷārika (gross, coarse) + ākāra (mode, manner) + anupāpana (causing to reach, bringing about); the shared function assigned to the four "vivid" feeling faculties (pleasure, pain, joy, displeasure), each overpowering its co-arisen states with its own pronounced quality.

amatābhimukhabhāva

a state of being turned toward the deathless — amata (the deathless, Nibbāna) + abhimukhabhāva (a state of facing toward); the specific effect the "faculty of one who has known" has on its associated states, orienting an Arahant's entire mental continuum toward Nibbāna.

indriyasaṃvara

restraint of the faculties — indriya (faculty) + saṃvara (restraint, guarding); the closing verse's practical application of this entire technical survey, naming the actual meditative discipline of guarding the sense faculties as the path to fully understanding them.

The Four Noble Truths: Sixteen True Aspects, and Why "Dukkha" Means What It Does

The chapter's second and by far its largest subject now opens: the Four Noble Truths — suffering, its origin, its cessation, and the way leading to its cessation. A dense triple verse promises understanding through no fewer than fourteen distinct methods. The first, division, shows that each truth carries exactly four true, unmistakable aspects of its own — suffering's aspects of oppressing, being conditioned, burning, and changing; origin's aspects of accumulating, being a source, binding, and impeding; cessation's aspects of escape, seclusion, being unconditioned, and being deathless; and the path's aspects of leading out, being a cause, seeing, and governing. The second method, etymology, works through each name letter by letter: "dukkha" combines "du" (contemptible) with "kha" (empty), since the first truth is genuinely contemptible, being the seat of countless calamities, and genuinely empty of any of the stability, beauty, or pleasure a fool imagines; "samudaya" combines "saṃ" (conjunction) with "u" (arising) and "aya" (cause), naming exactly what brings suffering into being, given the right supporting conditions; "nirodha" combines "ni" (absence) with "rodha" (prison), naming the utter absence of the prison-house of saṃsāra that occurs upon its attainment; and the fourth truth is simply "the way going to the cessation of suffering," since it faces cessation as its object and constitutes the actual path to reaching it. Finally, all four are called "noble truths" for four converging reasons: because the noble ones — the Buddha and his disciples — penetrate them; because they are the truths of the Noble One, the Tathāgata himself; because their full awakening establishes his very nobility; and because they are themselves genuinely true, unfailing, and never otherwise than they appear.

Moreover, they are also "noble truths" in the sense of "truths that are noble" — "noble" here meaning true, unfailing, and never deceiving. As it is said: "monks, these four noble truths are true, unfailing, never otherwise — therefore they are called 'noble truths.'" Thus, the determination by etymology should be understood here.

Reading note — Ru-Yi

The four-fold aspect scheme reveals a deliberate internal symmetry across the truths: suffering's "being conditioned" is mirrored by cessation's "being unconditioned," and origin's "binding" is mirrored by the path's "leading out" — the sixteen aspects are not sixteen independent facts but eight matched pairs of problem and corresponding solution, laid out so that memorizing one truth's aspects half-memorizes its counterpart's as well.

The four converging justifications for calling these truths "noble" — penetrated by the noble ones, belonging to the Noble One, constituting his nobility, and being themselves unfailingly true — show the word doing real philosophical work rather than functioning as mere honorific decoration: nobility here names a two-way relationship, where the Buddha's own attainment of nobility and the truths' own claim to unfailing accuracy turn out to be mutually defining and inseparable.

Why "Truth"? Like Fire's Own Nature, Never Otherwise Than It Is

Each truth receives its own precise characteristic, function, and manifestation — suffering marked by afflicting, functioning to burn, manifesting as occurrence; origin marked by producing, functioning to prevent interruption, manifesting as impediment; cessation marked by peace, functioning as the deathless, manifesting as signlessness; the path marked by leading out, functioning to abandon defilements, manifesting as emergence. The commentary then addresses the deepest question of all: in what sense, exactly, is each of these a "truth"? The answer is that each is genuinely and objectively so — not illusory like a magician's trick, not deceptive like a mirage, not utterly unreal like the imagined self of non-Buddhist teachers — but a real object for noble knowledge precisely because affliction genuinely does afflict, causation genuinely does cause, peace genuinely is peaceful, and the path genuinely does lead out, each in a manner as fixed and unfailing as fire's own nature to burn. A five-verse summary presses the same point through pure logical necessity: nothing but suffering can afflict, and suffering afflicts nothing but itself; nothing but craving can produce suffering, by the fixed law of causation; nothing but Nibbāna is peace, and nothing is peace but Nibbāna; nothing but the path leads out, and the path itself never fails to lead out — hence the wise call this very quality of being genuinely true and unmistaken, undivided across all four truths, their common "truth-meaning."

Why Suffering Comes First — and the Full Cast of Twelve, Three, One, and Eight

The order of teaching the four truths follows a carefully reasoned pedagogical logic: suffering comes first, being the most obvious and universally shared experience, easy for anyone to grasp; its origin follows immediately, to reveal the cause behind that already-recognized suffering; cessation comes third, to make known that when the cause ceases, the effect ceases too; and the path comes last, to show the actual means for attaining that cessation. An alternative account emphasizes psychological effect instead: suffering is taught first to rouse a healthy sense of urgency in beings too attached to the pleasures of existence; then origin, to show that this suffering neither arises uncaused nor is fashioned by some divine creator, but comes specifically from this identifiable source; then cessation, to give comfort to those now shaken by suffering and searching for a genuine way out; and finally the path, the means of actually reaching that cessation. With order settled, the commentary now catalogues exactly which specific doctrinal items the Buddha names within his own definition of each truth: twelve distinct items under suffering (birth, aging, death, sorrow, lamentation, pain, displeasure, despair, association with the unloved, separation from the loved, not getting what is wanted, and the five aggregates subject to clinging, in brief); three kinds of craving under origin; a single reality, Nibbāna, under cessation, however many synonyms it receives; and eight factors under the path.

Reading note — Ru-Yi

The two accounts of teaching-order given here — one purely logical (cause precedes effect, effect's cessation follows cause's cessation, means comes last), the other purely psychological (urgency, then causal explanation, then comfort, then practical guidance) — are offered side by side without forcing a choice between them, since both point to the identical sequence; the commentary seems content to let the reader see the same order justified twice, from two entirely different angles, rather than adjudicate which explanation is more fundamental.

The stark numerical asymmetry among the four truths' own internal item-counts — twelve for suffering, three for origin, one for cessation, eight for the path — is itself worth sitting with: suffering is the most elaborately subdivided because it is what ordinary experience actually presents in its full variety, while cessation collapses to a single item precisely because Nibbāna, however many synonyms are piled onto it, is not a compound of parts at all.

Birth: A Worm in Rotting Fish, and Seven Kinds of Suffering

The detailed treatment of suffering itself now begins with birth — a word the commentary first shows carries at least seven distinct canonical meanings (existence, a religious community, a conditioned characteristic, rebirth-linking, actual delivery, a family lineage, and noble virtue) before settling on the sense meant here: the very first appearance of the aggregates in any newly arisen being. Birth is shown to be suffering because it is the foundation for fully seven distinct kinds of suffering — the intrinsic pain of painful feeling itself; the suffering built into the very changeability of pleasant feeling; the suffering inherent in all conditioned formations simply by virtue of their constant rising and falling; suffering that is concealed, needing to be asked about before it becomes known, like an earache or the fever of lust; suffering that is plainly visible, like the pain of the thirty-two traditional forms of punishment; suffering spoken of only in an extended, figurative sense, such as birth and aging themselves; and suffering in the most direct, literal sense — the felt experience of pain itself. The commentary then plunges into an unflinching, graphically detailed account of exactly what a being suffers merely by being born: gestation in the narrow, foul-smelling space between stomach and bowel, no better than a worm hatching in rotting fish or festering porridge; the violent jostling endured whenever the mother stumbles, sits, or rises; the agony of childbirth itself, compared to a great elephant being forced through a keyhole; and the unbearable tenderness of newborn skin subjected to every touch, however gentle it is meant to be.

Ru-Yi Translation

This word "jāti" (birth) has many meanings. Thus, in "even one birth, even two births," it refers to existence. In "Visākhā, there are ascetics called the Nigaṇṭhas," it refers to a religious community. In "birth is included within two aggregates," it refers to the characteristic of the conditioned. In "the first mind that arose in the mother's womb, the first consciousness that appeared — from that comes his birth," it refers to rebirth-linking. In "the Bodhisatta, Ānanda, as soon as he was born," it refers to actual delivery. In "he was not reproached or reviled on account of his birth," it refers to family lineage. In "sister, since I was born with the noble birth," it refers to noble virtue.

Here, however, it refers to the aggregates occurring, for those born in a womb, from rebirth-linking up until emergence from the mother's womb; and, for other kinds of beings, simply to the aggregates present at rebirth-linking. But even this is only a figurative account. Non-figuratively, whatever aggregates first appear in beings being reborn in this or that place — the first appearance of those aggregates is what is called "birth."

This has the characteristic of the first coming-into-being in this or that existence. Its function is to hand over that existence. It is manifested as an emerging, here, from the previous existence — or, alternatively, as the state of being tormented by suffering.

Why, then, is this suffering? Because it is the basis for many kinds of suffering. For there are many kinds of suffering: intrinsic suffering, the suffering of change, the suffering of formations, concealed suffering, unconcealed suffering, suffering in an extended sense, and suffering in the direct sense.

Herein, bodily and mental painful feeling is called "intrinsic suffering," being suffering both by its own nature and by name. Pleasant feeling is "the suffering of change," being the cause for the arising of suffering through its own change. Equanimous feeling, together with the remaining formations of the three planes, is "the suffering of formations," being afflicted by constant rising and falling. Bodily and mental affliction such as an earache, a toothache, the fever born of lust, or the fever born of hatred, is called "concealed suffering," since it must be asked about before it is known, and its treatment is not evident — it is also called "unevident suffering." Affliction arising from such things as the thirty-two forms of punishment is called "unconcealed suffering," since it can be known without asking, and its treatment is evident — it is also called "evident suffering." Everything found in the analysis of the truth of suffering besides intrinsic suffering — birth and the rest — is "suffering in an extended sense," being the basis for this or that kind of suffering. Intrinsic suffering itself is called "suffering in the direct sense."

Now, this birth is the basis for the woeful suffering that the Blessed One himself made known, by way of simile, in the Discourse on the Fool and the Wise and elsewhere, and also for the suffering, arising even in a fortunate destiny in the human world, classified as beginning with the entering of the womb; hence it is suffering, being the basis for that suffering.

Herein, this is the suffering classified as beginning with the entering of the womb: this being, coming to be in the mother's womb, is not born among blue or white or red lotuses, but rather, in the region of the belly — below the stomach, above the bowel, between the abdominal lining and the spine, in an extremely cramped, thickly dark space, pervaded by a wind carrying the most disgusting odor of various kinds of putrid matter, an exceedingly repulsive region — comes to be like a worm born in rotting fish, rotting porridge, or a cesspool. Having come to be there, for ten months, cooked by the heat generated in the mother's womb as though in a covered pot, steamed like a dumpling, without any bending or stretching of limbs, it experiences the most extreme suffering. This, firstly, is the suffering that has its basis in the entering of the womb.

And the extreme suffering it experiences whenever the mother suddenly stumbles, walks, sits, rises, or turns — being dragged about, shaken, and jolted by such actions, like a lamb in the hands of a drunken lout or a young snake in the hands of a snake-charmer — and the intense suffering it experiences when the mother drinks something cold, as though reborn in a cold hell, or swallows hot gruel or rice, as though showered with burning embers, or swallows something salty or sour, as though subjected to the punishment of having caustic solution applied — this is the suffering that has its basis in the carrying of the womb.

And the suffering that arises for it, when the mother's womb has gone wrong, from such things as cutting and splitting, at a place of arising suffering unfit even for friends, ministers, and companions to witness — this is the suffering that has its basis in a miscarried pregnancy.

And the suffering that arises when the mother is giving birth, as it is turned about by kamma-born winds and forced along the birth-canal — a path as terrifying as a plunge into hell — being drawn out through the extremely constricted mouth of the womb as though through the hole of a key, and crushed as though by colliding mountains, like a great nāga or a hell-being — this is the suffering that has its basis in the actual delivery.

And the suffering that arises for the newborn, whose body is as delicate as a fresh wound, when its hands are taken hold of, when it is bathed, washed, and wiped with a cloth, and the like — suffering resembling being pierced and split by needle-points and razor-blades — this is the suffering that has its basis in emerging outside from the mother's womb.

And the suffering that arises afterward, in the course of life, for one who kills himself by pursuing self-mortification and self-torment through such practices as going naked, or who, out of anger, refuses to eat, or who hangs himself — this is the suffering that has its basis in self-inflicted violence. And the suffering that arises for one who undergoes killing, imprisonment, and the like at another's hands — this is the suffering that has its basis in violence inflicted by others.

Thus, this birth is simply the basis for the whole of this suffering.

Reading note — Ru-Yi

The seven-fold taxonomy of suffering — intrinsic, of change, of formations, concealed, unconcealed, extended, direct — quietly reorganizes everything the rest of this chapter is about to describe: birth, aging, death, and the rest are all classified as merely "extended" suffering, mere occasions or bases on which some deeper, more fundamental kind of suffering (painful feeling itself, or the sheer impermanence of formations) actually operates — a reminder that the elaborate catalogue to come is describing suffering's occasions, not suffering's ultimate substance.

The unrelenting, almost clinical detail of the womb-suffering passage — the cramped darkness, the stench, the crushing during birth, the needle-sharp pain of a newborn's first touch — is deliberately calibrated to counter precisely the sentimentality that ordinarily surrounds birth in human culture; the commentary refuses any comforting frame, insisting that birth's opening moments, examined honestly rather than nostalgically, are already saturated with the very suffering the entire teaching exists to address.

Verses on Birth — Then Aging and Death, Each With Its Own Anatomy

A cascade of verses drives home birth's role as suffering's universal precondition, moving systematically through every possible destiny: were a being not born into hell, it could never encounter that realm's unbearable burning; were it not born among animals, it could never suffer the driver's goad and whip; were it not born among ghosts, it could never know their peculiar hunger, thirst, wind, and heat; were it not born among asuras, it could never face that realm's crushing darkness and unbearable cold. Even the suffering of a long confinement in, and difficult emergence from, the mother's womb, comparable to a hellish cesspit, could never occur without birth — in short, no suffering anywhere exists apart from birth, which is why the sage names it first of all as suffering. Aging and death follow with their own compact treatments: aging, defined here as the aging of a single existence's own aggregates over time rather than the moment-to-moment aging of conditioned phenomena generally, is shown to be the basis for the loosening of limbs, deterioration of faculties, loss of youthful vigor, decline of strength, loss of memory and understanding, and the growing contempt of one's own family; death, defined as the actual severance of the life faculty within a single existence, is shown to be the basis for the mental anguish of the dying and the sheer physical agony of the body's joints and vital points being wrenched apart, an unbearable and irremediable suffering.

Reading note — Ru-Yi

The verse-sequence's method — systematically working through hell-beings, animals, ghosts, asuras, and finally the human womb, showing in each case that the specific suffering proper to that realm literally could not occur without birth — builds toward its own explicit conclusion by pure exhaustive enumeration: having checked every possible destiny and found birth indispensable to each one's suffering, the sage is entitled to the universal claim that no suffering anywhere exists apart from birth.

Aging's inclusion of "arriving at a foolish state" alongside physical decline is a quietly unsentimental addition: the verse does not treat old age as a period of accumulated wisdom compensating for bodily loss, but explicitly names cognitive and characterological decline — becoming foolish — as one more genuine loss aging inflicts, refusing the common cultural trope that elderly decline is offset by increased understanding.

Sorrow, Lamentation, Pain, Displeasure, Despair — and a Pot Cooking on the Fire

Five more members of the suffering-catalogue follow in quick succession, each precisely distinguished from its neighbors despite their obvious overlap. Sorrow is the inward mental burning of one struck by a relative's misfortune or the like — a poisoned dart piercing the heart, an iron spike heated in fire, straw catching flame again and again. Lamentation is the resulting outward, incoherent speech, its function being the proclaiming of both the departed's virtues and one's own distress, its manifestation a kind of frantic agitation. Bodily pain and mental displeasure receive their own compact definitions as physical affliction and psychological affliction respectively, each shown to intensify the other — mental anguish drives people to tear their hair, beat their breasts, roll on the ground, and even take their own lives. Despair, the last and most exhausted member of this cluster, is simply hatred itself, brought to an extreme pitch by overwhelming mental suffering, manifesting as a kind of collapse or dejection beyond even lamentation's expressive relief. The commentary closes this section with a memorable image of escalating intensity: sorrow is like a pot slowly cooking over a low flame, its contents sealed within; lamentation is like the same pot boiling over violently on a fierce flame, its contents spilling forcefully outward; and despair is like whatever remains trapped inside, unable to escape, continuing to cook down to nothing.

Reading note — Ru-Yi

Unwanted Company, Lost Loved Ones, Frustrated Wishes — and a Single Drop of the Ocean

Three more specific forms of suffering close out the enumeration before the entire twelve-item list collapses back into a single, unifying formula. Association with the unwanted is precisely defined as the conjoining of a being with disagreeable people or conditions, its manifestation being simply harm itself. Separation from the loved is its mirror image, the parting from agreeable people or conditions, its manifestation calamity itself. Not getting what one wants covers every futile longing for something inherently unattainable — most memorably, the wish that beings not be subject to birth, aging, sickness, and death at all — its manifestation the simple non-attainment of what was sought. All twelve items are then shown to converge in a single striking image: exactly as fire feeds on fuel, as targets draw arrows, as cattle attract biting flies, as ripe fields draw reapers, and as villages draw plundering bandits, so every one of birth's, aging's, and death's afflictions, indeed the entirety of this whole catalogue of suffering, arises nowhere but within the five aggregates subject to clinging themselves — which is why the Buddha, when compressing an otherwise inexhaustible subject that could not be fully described even across many aeons, gathered the whole ocean of suffering into a single drop by declaring, simply and finally, "in brief, the five aggregates subject to clinging are suffering."

Reading note — Ru-Yi

The Origin Is Craving; Its Cessation Is Taught as Craving's Own Cessation

The second truth's own definition receives brief word-commentary — craving is what leads to renewed existence, is bound up with delight and lust, and finds delight now here, now there, dividing into craving for sensual pleasure, for existence, and for non-existence, a threefold analysis to be treated in still greater depth in the treatise's later chapter on dependent origination. The third truth then poses a genuinely striking question: the definition of cessation given in the canon speaks only of the complete fading away and cessation of craving — so why is the cessation of suffering itself explained by reference to the cessation of its cause, rather than being defined directly? The answer reveals something important about how a Buddha teaches as opposed to how non-Buddhist teachers do: since suffering ceases only through the cessation of its cause, and never otherwise — exactly as a felled tree, if its root remains undamaged, will grow back again and again for as long as the underlying craving-tendency itself remains uprooted — the Tathāgatas, behaving like lions, address the actual cause even while eliminating and teaching about the effect; non-Buddhist teachers, behaving like dogs, address only the effect itself, through practices of self-mortification and the like, never touching the cause. All of the resulting terms — fading away, cessation, giving up, relinquishment, freedom, and non-attachment — turn out, in the ultimate sense, to be nothing but synonyms for Nibbāna itself, since it is in dependence on Nibbāna that craving both fades away and ceases.

Does Nibbāna Actually Exist? A Rigorous Philosophical Defense

Before cataloguing Nibbāna's positive qualities, the commentary takes on a genuinely serious philosophical challenge directly: does Nibbāna even exist, given that it can never be apprehended the way an ordinary object can, no more than a hare's horn? The answer is a firm no, on the grounds that Nibbāna is indeed apprehensible, but only by the appropriate means — cultivated through the corresponding practice, exactly as a mind-reader's own trained knowledge apprehends another's supramundane mind, something an untrained worldling could never detect. What ordinary fools fail to grasp cannot, on that basis alone, be declared not to exist. A second line of attack presses further: if Nibbāna genuinely did not exist, right practice itself would become sterile, incapable of ever bearing fruit — an absurd conclusion, since right practice manifestly does lead somewhere. A series of increasingly technical objections and rebuttals follows: is Nibbāna simply the "non-existence" of past and future defilements, given that they clearly do not exist? No, since present defilements would then have to be un-abolished at exactly the moment of path-attainment, an absurd result that would make the very attainment of Nibbāna with residue meaningless. Is Nibbāna, then, simply the destruction of lust and the rest, as certain canonical formulas suggest? No, since that would make Arahantship itself into a mere destruction-event, subject to time, conditioned, and even attainable without any right effort at all — consequences the tradition emphatically rejects, since it is specifically the noble path that brings about the destruction of defilements, a destruction which, once accomplished, permanently forecloses their further occurrence.

Reading note — Ru-Yi

The dialectic's core strategy is worth naming explicitly: every proposed reductive definition of Nibbāna as some kind of mere "non-existence" or "destruction-event" is rejected not because the proposal is meaningless, but because each one, followed through consistently, generates absurd or unacceptable consequences — Nibbāna becoming temporary, conditioned, attainable without effort, or even burning with the very fires it is supposed to extinguish — a method of refutation by consequence rather than by direct counter-assertion.

The final move — conceding that "destruction" is indeed used of Nibbāna, but only as a figurative extension from the noble path that brings it about, never as Nibbāna's own proper description — models a recurring interpretive principle in this commentarial tradition: canonical language that looks, on its surface, like a literal definition is sometimes better read as a practical shorthand pointing toward something the text itself insists is too subtle for direct, literal naming.

Unshared, Unborn, Deathless, Permanent: Nibbāna's Own True Qualities

Having cleared away every mistaken account of what Nibbāna is not, the commentary now lists what it genuinely is. It is unshared, since it is attained only by one actually possessing the path; unarisen, since it has no prior point of origin at all — and, importantly, this is not because it is somehow generated by the path either, since the path only reaches it, never produces it. Being without any point of origin, it is free from aging and death, and, being free from origin, aging, and death, it is permanent — a permanence carefully distinguished from the eternality wrongly attributed to atoms or the imagined self, since Nibbāna's permanence has genuine logical grounds (the absence of any cause for change) that those other claims entirely lack. It is formless, transcending the very nature of matter; it is the single, sole goal of all Buddhas and disciples alike, with no variation among them; and it carries two further names depending on the sense in which it is attained — Nibbāna "with residue remaining" when the defilements alone have been calmed while the resultant aggregates from prior kamma persist, and Nibbāna "without residue remaining" when even that residue, along with the very last consciousness, fails to arise again after the destruction of the origin. Attainable only through a knowledge born of unremitting effort, and directly confirmed by the Buddha's own omniscient word, Nibbāna is, in the ultimate sense, no mere absence at all — as the Buddha himself declared: "monks, there is an unborn, an unbecome, an unmade, an unconditioned."

The Eightfold Path, Freshly Defined: Eight Factors Arising Together in a Single Moment

Though all eight path factors were already explained in the discussion of the aggregates, the commentary now redefines each one specifically to bring out its distinctive quality as they all occur together, simultaneously, in the mind of a meditator actually penetrating the four truths. Right view is the wisdom-eye taking Nibbāna as object, uprooting the underlying tendency to ignorance, seeing rightly and illuminating the true nature of things. Right intention, arising for one already endowed with such view, directs the mind onto that same plane of Nibbāna and destroys wrong intention. Right speech, right action, and right livelihood are each, in turn, the specific abstinence that eradicates verbal misconduct, bodily misconduct, and wrong livelihood respectively, arising in one who has already seen and is already applying right intention. Right effort is the corresponding arousing of energy that cuts off laziness for one now established on that threefold foundation of virtue; right mindfulness is the resulting non-confusion of mind that shakes off wrong mindfulness; and right concentration, arising in a mind thus protected by unsurpassed mindfulness, is the one-pointedness that shatters wrong concentration entirely — the eight factors together constituting the complete definition of the fourth noble truth.

Two Kinds of Knowing the Truths — and the Four Wrong Views Each One Dispels

Knowledge of the four truths itself divides into two distinct kinds. Conforming knowledge, mundane and built up through repeated hearing and reflection, operates only with respect to cessation and the path, since suffering and its origin — being ever-present in ordinary experience — do not require this kind of anticipatory understanding in the same way. Penetrative knowledge, supramundane and taking Nibbāna alone as its object, penetrates all four truths simultaneously and at once, in a single functional stroke, exactly as one who sees the rising sun necessarily sees, in that very moment, the light that inevitably comes with it. Turning to mundane conforming knowledge's own specific function, each truth's contemplation is shown to overturn a particular, specifically matched wrong view: knowledge of suffering turns back the view of a real, persisting self within the aggregates; knowledge of the origin turns back the view of annihilation; knowledge of cessation turns back the view of eternalism; and knowledge of the path turns back the view that no genuine spiritual action or its result actually exists. Until all four truths are genuinely known, the verse insists, a person remains thoroughly confused about the world, its origin, its auspicious ending, and the way leading there.

Reading note — Ru-Yi

The sunrise simile for penetrative knowledge captures something genuinely precise about its unified character: one does not first see the sun and only later, in a separate act, notice the light it brings — seeing the sun and seeing its light are a single, indivisible perceptual event, and the commentary insists penetrative knowledge of the four truths works the very same way, comprehending suffering, origin, cessation, and path in one undivided functional stroke rather than four successive acts of understanding.

The two parallel four-way mappings — each truth's knowledge overturning first a specific wrong view (self, annihilation, eternalism, non-action), then a specific wrong practice (misperceiving impermanent aggregates as stable, mistaking a false cause, grasping a false liberation, mistaking a false path) — reveal that this entire fourfold analysis functions as a comprehensive diagnostic grid, addressing not one but two distinct dimensions of spiritual error at once: what one wrongly believes, and what one wrongly does as a result.

Absorbing the Thirty-Seven Requisites of Enlightenment Into the Four Truths

The commentary now shows how a much larger body of doctrine — the full thirty-seven requisites of enlightenment scattered across many different discourses — is entirely subsumed within these same four truths. Suffering absorbs everything except craving and the taintless states themselves; the origin absorbs all thirty-six varieties of craving; cessation remains a pure, unmixed single reality; but the path's own definition, given nominally as eight factors under the heading of right view and the rest, turns out to secretly contain the entire remaining catalogue. Under the banner of right view alone hide investigation-of-states as a basis of power, the wisdom faculty, the wisdom power, and the enlightenment factor of investigation of states. Under right intention hide the three kinds of wholesome thought. Right speech, action, and livelihood each stand in for their corresponding threefold group of good conduct. And, since all three of these virtue-factors are the noble, cherished virtue that must be received by the very hand of faith, they in turn bring with them the faith faculty, the faith power, and the basis of power called desire-to-act. Right effort conceals the fourfold right striving, the energy faculty, power, and enlightenment factor; right mindfulness conceals the fourfold establishment of mindfulness and its own three counterparts; and right concentration conceals the three kinds of concentration together with concentration's own faculty, power, and three further enlightenment factors of joy, tranquility, and equanimity — the entire architecture of the thirty-seven factors turning out to be nothing more than this same eightfold path, examined closely enough.

The commentary now offers a whole network of paired similes, each one mapping all four truths onto a single, coherent narrative scenario. Suffering is like a burden; its origin is like the taking up of that burden; cessation is like the setting down of that burden; the path is like the means of setting it down. Suffering is like a disease; its origin is like the cause of that disease; cessation is like the disease's cure; the path is like the medicine that brings about that cure. Suffering is like a famine; its origin is like a drought; cessation is like an abundant harvest; the path is like timely, plentiful rain. And the network extends further still, through four additional four-part chains: an enemy, the root of enmity, the uprooting of enmity, and the means to that uprooting; a poison tree, its root, the cutting of that root, and the means to that cutting; fear, the root of fear, fearlessness, and the means to attaining it; and, finally, the near shore, a great flood, the far shore, and the effort required to actually cross over — each fourfold chain offering yet another concrete, memorable lens onto the identical underlying structure of problem, cause, resolution, and method.

Reading note — Ru-Yi

Every one of these seven four-part similes shares an identical logical skeleton — an unwanted condition, its specific originating cause, the state achieved once that cause is removed, and the concrete means of removing it — and the sheer number and variety of concrete scenarios chosen (burden, disease, famine, enmity, poisoning, fear, a flood to be crossed) suggests a deliberate pedagogical strategy: whichever single image a particular listener happens to find most vivid or personally resonant, the same underlying four-truths structure will already be legible within it.

The final simile — near shore, great flood, far shore, and the effort to cross — quietly reframes the entire teaching as fundamentally about movement and effort rather than passive waiting: cessation (the far shore) does not simply arrive on its own once suffering (the flood) is acknowledged, but requires the specific, sustained exertion named as the path, without which the far shore, however real, remains permanently out of reach.

Suffering That Isn't a Truth, and a Truth That Isn't Suffering: The Four-Cornered Analysis

A final precision-tool sharpens the boundary between "suffering" as an ordinary category and "the noble truth of suffering" as a specific technical designation, applying the classical four-cornered logical analysis to each truth in turn: there is suffering that is not a noble truth, a noble truth that is not suffering, something that is both suffering and a noble truth, and something that is neither. Path-associated states and the fruits of the ascetic life, being impermanent and therefore subject to the suffering inherent in all formations, count as suffering — yet since they are not the specific object toward which the entire holy life is directed for the sake of full understanding, they do not count as the noble truth of suffering itself. Cessation, meanwhile, is a noble truth without in any sense being suffering. The remaining two truths, origin and path, can each be suffering by virtue of impermanence, while still remaining, in every respect apart from craving, both fully suffering and a genuine noble truth at once. And whatever exists specifically for the sake of fully understanding it, and for the sake of which the holy life is lived, is neither the ordinary category of suffering nor a noble truth in this restricted technical sense — a distinction that, once mastered for the truth of suffering, applies with only minor adjustment to origin, cessation, and the path in turn.

Reading note — Ru-Yi

The tetralemma's real payoff is exposing a subtle but consequential ambiguity hiding inside the ordinary word "suffering": one sense is purely descriptive (anything impermanent counts, including the path and its fruits), while the other is functional and restricted (only what the entire holy life exists specifically to help one fully understand counts as the "noble truth" of suffering) — and conflating these two senses would wrongly imply that the very path meant to end suffering is itself an instance of the problem it solves.

Excluding craving specifically from the aggregates' status as "suffering and noble truth at once" is a small but careful move: craving belongs definitionally to the second truth (origin), not the first, and the qualifier "apart from craving" prevents the aggregates' full identification with suffering from accidentally re-absorbing the very cause the second truth was created to isolate and name separately.

Suffering Without a Sufferer: The Emptiness at the Heart of All Four Truths

All four truths, examined in the ultimate sense, are shown to be genuinely empty — devoid of any experiencer, any agent, any liberated person, and any goer along the path. A tightly constructed verse drives the point home with almost mathematical precision: there is only suffering, but no sufferer to be found; there is only action, but no actual agent behind it; there does exist an extinguishing, but no extinguished person to speak of; there is a path, but no one who actually walks it. A second, complementary account of emptiness works instead through mutual exclusion between the truths themselves: suffering, origin, and path are each empty of the permanence, beauty, pleasure, and self wrongly imagined of them, while cessation, the deathless state, is empty specifically of these same first two — permanence and self — since it stands apart from them entirely. Cessation is also empty of the remaining three truths, and each of the other three is, in turn, empty of cessation. And, in a final refinement, effect is shown to be empty of its own cause, and cause empty of its own effect — origin containing no trace of the suffering it is not yet, and suffering containing no trace of the origin already spent in producing it — so that neither this treatise's own doctrine of dependent conditions, nor any rival theory of an inherent, built-in identity between cause and effect, survives unscathed.

Reading note — Ru-Yi

The closing rebuttal of both dependent-origination-style causal accounts and rival theories of inherent cause-effect identity, in a single stroke, is a notable act of even-handedness: the commentary does not merely defend its own causal framework against outside theories, but subjects its own vocabulary of cause and effect to exactly the same emptiness-analysis, refusing to let its own doctrine claim any special exemption from the very impermanence and non-self it applies to everything else.

One-Fold to Six-Fold: Reclassifying Each Truth by a Different Count

The commentary now applies to all four truths the same numerical-classification method used throughout the treatise for the aggregates, bases, and elements. Suffering, considered purely as mere occurrence, is one-fold; by name and form, two-fold; by the three realms of sensual, fine-material, and immaterial rebirth, three-fold; by the four kinds of nutriment, four-fold; by the five aggregates subject to clinging, five-fold. The origin, as the producing cause, is likewise one-fold; by association or dissociation with views, two-fold; by its three kinds — craving for sensual pleasure, existence, and non-existence — three-fold; by what is abandoned through each of the four paths, four-fold; by the five modes of delighting in form and the other aggregates, five-fold; and by the six classes of craving corresponding to the six sense objects, six-fold. Cessation, as the unconditioned element itself, is one-fold; but figuratively divides into with-residue and without-residue, two-fold; by the pacifying of the three kinds of existence, three-fold; by attainment through each of the four paths, four-fold; by the pacifying of the same five delights, five-fold; and by the exhaustion of the same six classes of craving, six-fold. The path, as what is to be developed, is one-fold; by the twin divisions of calm and insight, or of seeing and developing, two-fold; and, most substantially, three-fold by its grouping into the three aggregates of virtue, concentration, and wisdom.

Reading note — Ru-Yi

The reversed containment relation quoted from the Cūḷavedalla Sutta — not "the path contains the three aggregates" but "the path is contained within the three aggregates" — is a small grammatical inversion carrying real conceptual weight: the eightfold path, spread out across its own eight named factors, is shown to be the narrower, more specific thing, while virtue, concentration, and wisdom are the broader categories that happen, in this particular case, to be fully exhausted by the path's eight factors without remainder.

The parallel five-fold and six-fold classifications running through suffering, origin, and cessation — matched item for item (the five aggregates mirrored by five delights in them, the six sense-object cravings mirrored by six correspondingly exhausted cravings) — reveal that these three truths are not merely thematically related but structurally identical in their internal architecture, differing only in whether a given item is present, delighted in, or has had that delight extinguished.

Three Friends in a Garden: Why Concentration Needs Energy and Mindfulness to Stand Firm

A charming narrative simile explains a genuinely subtle technical point: why concentration, though arising together with effort and mindfulness as three co-equal path factors, is grouped under the "aggregate of concentration" by its own essential nature, while effort and mindfulness are grouped there only by function. Three friends enter a garden to enjoy a festival; one, seeing a magnificently flowering campaka tree, cannot reach its blossoms even by stretching out his hand; a second friend bends down and offers his back as a stepping-stone; and only once a third friend has offered his shoulder as well can the first finally climb up, using one friend's back and the other's shoulder together, and pick flowers freely. Concentration is like that first friend, incapable, by its own nature, of fixing one-pointedly upon its object; effort, like the friend who bends down, does the work of exertion; and mindfulness, like the friend who offers his shoulder, does the work of preventing distraction — only with both of their support can concentration actually succeed. An exactly parallel simile, drawn from a money-changer unable to turn a coin over using only the flat of his palm but able to examine every side of it once he uses his fingertips, explains why wisdom, similarly incapable by its own nature of settling decisively on an object as impermanent, painful, or not-self, depends on intention's own repeated "striking and turning" to make that determination possible at all.

Reading note — Ru-Yi

Both similes solve the identical structural puzzle — three factors arise together, yet only one is classified "by its own kind" within a given aggregate while the other two are classified merely "by function" — and the resolution in each case turns on a distinction between essential incapacity and functional support: concentration and wisdom are each intrinsically unable to perform their signature task alone, and what looks like a triad of equal partners turns out, on closer analysis, to be one essential capacity propped up by two enabling supports.

The correction of the canonical text's own grammatical case — reading a locative ("in the aggregate of virtue") as though it meant an instrumental ("by way of the aggregate of virtue") — is a small but telling instance of this commentarial tradition's willingness to gloss canonical wording against its most literal surface reading when the underlying sense clearly requires it, rather than forcing a strained literal interpretation onto a case-ending that doesn't quite fit the intended meaning.

Splitting a Hair a Hundred Times Over: How the Four Truths Resemble and Differ

The chapter's final analytical method compares the four truths against one another directly, finding them alike in three respects — all are equally real, all are equally empty of self, and all are equally difficult to penetrate, a difficulty the Buddha himself illustrated to Ānanda through an unforgettable image: harder than an archer's shooting arrow after arrow through a tiny keyhole from a distance without ever missing, harder even than splitting, with impossible precision, the very tip of a hair already split a hundred times over, is the penetration of these four truths exactly as they are. Yet the truths also differ sharply from one another along several distinct axes at once: suffering and origin are mundane, with taints, and hard to fathom, contrasted against cessation and path's supramundane, taintless, easily-graspable-by-comparison nature; suffering and cessation are, in a sense, "results," contrasted against origin and path's causal role; suffering and path share a paradoxical kinship as things whose good and bad fruits respectively still belong to a trainee's or worldling's ongoing practice, contrasted against origin and cessation's fixed, unconditioned or purely defiling character; and finally, suffering and origin are worldly, contrasted against cessation and path's transcendence of the world altogether. With this final comparison complete, the sixteenth chapter's exhaustive survey of the four noble truths — and, with it, the whole of wisdom's "ground" so far as faculties and truths are concerned — comes to its close.

Reading note — Ru-Yi

The hair-splitting-arrow image, drawn directly from the Buddha's own exchange with Ānanda, deliberately escalates past its own first, already extreme comparison — splitting a hundred-times-split hair-tip with another hair-tip — only to declare genuine penetration of the four truths harder still, refusing to let even its most exaggerated available image of precision fully capture the difficulty being described.

The six pairwise comparisons closing the chapter (first-second, third-fourth, first-third, second-fourth, first-fourth, second-third) exhaust every possible pairing among the four truths, and each pairing surfaces a genuinely different axis of both kinship and contrast — cause versus effect, object versus subject, conditioned versus unconditioned, wholesome versus unwholesome — demonstrating that the four truths cannot be fully mapped along any single dimension, but require this entire lattice of overlapping relations to be properly understood.

Indriyasaccaniddesa Complete

With the four noble truths now examined through fourteen distinct analytical methods — division, etymology, characteristic, meaning, the extraction of meaning, exact number, order, the determination of their component items, the function of knowledge, the doctrines subsumed within them, simile, the tetralemma, emptiness and numerical classification, and finally similarity and dissimilarity — the sixteenth chapter of the Visuddhimagga comes to its close. Together with its opening survey of the twenty-two faculties, this chapter completes the last of the individually-named categories within wisdom's "ground": aggregates, sense bases, and elements in chapters fourteen and fifteen; faculties and truths here. Only dependent origination, the sixth and final category named back in chapter fourteen's own architecture, remains to complete the ground entirely — after which purification of view, the first of the treatise's own central seven purifications, can properly begin.

The seventeenth chapter, the Paññābhūminiddesa ("Discussion of the Ground of Wisdom"), verified from the Visuddhimagga's own table of contents, takes up this final piece — the doctrine of dependent origination — in what promises to be one of the treatise's longest and most intricate chapters, tracing the causal chain from ignorance through to the whole mass of suffering, and completing, once and for all, the ground upon which the remaining purifications will be built.
