Cariyapitaka · Story 8

King Sivi’s Conduct

Sivi Cariyā — The Chapter With Akitti · The Perfection of Giving (8th of 10)

Setting
No external narrative frame; the Buddha speaks in the first person about his past life as King Sivi of Ariṭṭha
Speaker
The Buddha, recounting his life as King Sivi, with two further voices quoted in dialogue: Sakka, and the disguised beggar Sakka becomes
Form
Sixteen four-line verses of first-person narration and quoted dialogue
Length
2–3 minutes to read
Northern parallel
King Sivi's story is widely known across Buddhist Asia as the Sivi Jātaka, traditionally numbered 499 among the full collection of 547; this reading guide does not assert further specific textual correspondences beyond that widely cited number.
Difficulty
★★★☆☆ — a dialogue-driven story with a genuinely difficult subject

Why this text

This is probably the best-known story in the whole Cariyapitaka: a king who declares, seated comfortably in his own longhouse, that he would give away even his own eye if asked. Sakka, king of the gods, overhears the thought and decides to test it — and when the moment comes, Sivi doesn't just meet the test, he exceeds what was actually asked of him.

Reading guide

A private thought, overheard

The story opens with Sivi alone with his own reflection: he can think of no gift he has not already given, and even names the one thing that might seem unthinkable — his own eye — as something he would give ‘without wavering’. Sakka, seated in the assembly of gods, hears this and decides to find out if it is true.

A test staged carefully

Sakka does not simply appear and ask; he takes the form of a frail, blind old man, describes his own blindness in detail, praises the king's reputation for generosity by name, and asks for only one eye, offering a concession — ‘while you too get by with one’.

A request exceeded, not just met

Sivi's reaction is not reluctant compliance but open elation: ‘my wish has come true, my intention is fulfilled’. He then orders his attendant Sivaka to take not the one eye asked for, but both. The gift given exceeds what was actually requested.

A verdict on his own mind

The story closes not with an outcome but with a report on the king's own state of mind throughout: ‘while planning to give, while giving, and after I had given, my mind did not falter’, and, in the same denial-then-reason structure used elsewhere in this chapter, an explicit statement that this was not from any lack of care for his own eyes or himself, but ‘because omniscience is precious to me’.

Key terms

Sakka — king of the gods, who overhears Sivi's private reflection and disguises himself as a blind beggar to test it.
Sivaka — the attendant Sivi orders to carry out the physical act of removing his eyes — the king directs the gift, but does not perform the act himself.
dāna — “giving” — the perfection this story illustrates, the eighth of ten stories on this theme opening the collection.
Ariṭṭha — the city named as King Sivi's seat of rule.
Sivi Jātaka — the fuller version of this story in the separate Jātaka tradition, traditionally numbered 499 among the full collection of 547.

The text

The text in full: sixteen verses, including quoted dialogue between Sivi, Sakka, and the disguised beggar. Translation: Bhikkhu Sujato (CC0, SuttaCentral).

A private thought, overheard

§1“In the city called Ariṭṭha I was the aristocrat named Sivi. Seated in my finest longhouse, I then had the following thought:

§2‘Whatever human gifts there are, none may be found that I have not given. Even if someone were to ask for my eye, I would give it without wavering.’

Sakka decides to test him

§3Knowing my thoughts, Sakka the lord of gods, sitting in the assembly of gods said the following.

§4‘Seated in his finest longhouse, the mighty King Sivi is thinking of manifold gifts and cannot see any he would not give.

§5Is this is true or not? Come, let us test him. Wait a while until I know his mind.’

A beggar's request

§6Appearing as a frail old man, with grey hair and wrinkled skin, feeble and blind, he approached the king.

§7Then, stretching out his left and right arms, he raised his cupped palms to his head and said the following.

§8‘Great king, I have a request for you. Under your just rule the kingdom has flourished, and the renown of your love of giving has risen among gods and humans.

§9Both my eyes, by which I find my way, are ruined and become blind. Give me just one eye, while you too get by with one.’

The king's response

§10After hearing his words I was thrilled, my mind elated, so with cupped palms, inspired, I said the following.

§11‘Just now I was reflecting on this when I came here from the longhouse. Understanding my mind, you have come to ask for an eye.

§12Oh! My wish has come true! My intention is fulfilled! Today I shall give to a supplicant a fine gift never before given.’

The gift given

§13‘Come Sivaka, stand up, do not hesitate or waver. Pluck out even both eyes and give them to the supplicant.’

§14And then, urged on by me, Sivaka did as I said. Extracting both eyes, he presented them to the trembling supplicant.

§15‘While planning to give, while giving, and after I had given, my mind did not falter, because it was solely for awakening.

§16It was not because I disliked my eyes, or that I disliked myself, but because omniscience is precious to me, that is why I gave away my eyes.’”
