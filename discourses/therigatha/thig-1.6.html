Why this text

This verse compresses the goal of the entire path into four lines addressed to a nun whose name, Dhīrā, already means ‘steadfast’ or ‘wise’. Where earlier poems in this book described release or fullness, this one names the destination directly: cessation, and then extinguishment itself.

Reading guide

A name meaning steadiness

‘Dhīrā’ carries connotations of firmness and wisdom — a fitting name for a verse whose instructions move directly and without hesitation toward the path's furthest point.

Cessation described as blissful, not blank

The verse specifies what ‘touching cessation’ means: ‘the blissful settling of perception’ — not an absence or a void, but a settling described in terms of ease.

Two words for the same destination

The verse's second half names the goal twice, in different registers: ‘win extinguishment’, then immediately ‘the supreme sanctuary from the yoke’ — one term naming what is attained, the other naming what it protects against.

The yoke, named again

This is the third poem in this book to use the image of the ‘yoke’, after Thig 1.2 and Thig 1.4 — a recurring thread across these otherwise very different, very short poems.

Key terms

Dhīrā — a nun's name carrying connotations of ‘steadfast’ or ‘wise’.
nirodha — “cessation” — what Dhīrā is instructed to touch, described here as a blissful settling rather than a blank absence.
nibbāna — “extinguishment” — named directly in this verse as the goal to be won.
yoga-kkhema — “sanctuary from the yoke” — a compound term naming safety from the ties that bind a person to further rebirth.
saññā — “perception” — the faculty this verse says settles blissfully at the moment cessation is touched.

The text

The text in full: a single four-line verse, with no attribution. Translation: Bhikkhu Sujato (CC0, SuttaCentral).

§1Dhīrā, touch cessation, the blissful settling of perception. Win extinguishment, the supreme sanctuary from the yoke.
