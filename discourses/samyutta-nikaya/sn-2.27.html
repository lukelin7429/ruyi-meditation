Saṃyutta Nikāya · Discourse 2.27

With Nanda

Nandasutta

Setting
At Sāvatthī; no further narrative detail given
Speakers
The Buddha and the godling Nanda
Form
A four-line verse on impermanence, matched by the Buddha's reply with its final line changed
Length
~1 minute to read
Northern parallel
A rough counterpart may exist in the Chinese Saṃyukta-āgama (T99), though this reading guide does not assert a specific matching sutra number
Difficulty
★★★☆☆ — brief, but best read directly against SN 2.19 for what is shared and what is not

Why this discourse

Nanda's verse opens with its own distinct image of impermanence — time flies, nights pass by, the stages of life leave one by one — before closing on a line already heard from a different godling: ‘seeing this peril in death, do good deeds that bring you joy.’ The Buddha's reply again substitutes only the final line, exactly as he did at SN 2.19, producing a verse whose closing couplet is now word for word identical to that earlier discourse's own reply, though its opening differs entirely.

Reading guide

A new opening, a familiar closing

This discourse's first two lines are entirely different from SN 2.19's Uttarasutta — time flying and nights passing, rather than life's shortness and old age's lack of shelter — but its second couplet, ‘seeing this peril in death, do good deeds that bring you joy,’ is identical word for word.

A fourth mode of repetition in this collection

This book has already shown verses repeated whole across unrelated discourses (SN 2.13), and single lines substituted within a reply (SN 2.16, SN 2.19); this discourse adds a new variant — two different godlings independently arriving at the identical closing couplet while opening their verses differently.

The Buddha's identical substitution

The Buddha's own reply here is, in turn, word for word identical to his reply at SN 2.19: ‘a seeker of peace would drop the world's bait’, replacing ‘do good deeds that bring you joy’ exactly as before, regardless of which godling's opening lines precede it.

What the shared couplet suggests

That both this discourse and SN 2.19 converge on the same closing couplet, met by the same substituted reply, suggests this specific pairing — merit for a happy rebirth, redirected toward renunciation — functioned as something like a fixed formula the compilers could attach to more than one opening.

Two names, one lesson repeated for emphasis

Whether or not this repetition was deliberate on the compilers' part, its effect within the collection is to underscore the lesson through recurrence: readers encountering both discourses meet the same redirection from merit-making to renunciation twice, framed by two different godlings' distinct circumstances.

Key terms

accayanti ahorattā — “time flies, nights pass by” — this discourse's own distinct opening image, absent from SN 2.19.
jīvitassa vayoguṇā — “the stages of life leave us one by one” — the second line, likewise unique to this discourse's opening.
maraṇabhaya — “peril in death” — the phrase shared word for word with SN 2.19's closing couplet.
puññāni kayirātha — “do good deeds” — Nanda's original conclusion, identical to Uttara's at SN 2.19.
lokāmisa — “the world's bait” — the Buddha's substituted image, identical word for word to his reply at SN 2.19.

The text

The discourse in full: Nanda's verse and the Buddha's reply with its final line substituted. Translation: Bhikkhu Sujato (CC0, SuttaCentral).

§1Standing to one side, the godling Nanda spoke this verse in the Buddha’s presence:

§2“Time flies, nights pass by, the stages of life leave us one by one. Seeing this peril in death, do good deeds that bring you joy.”

§3“Time flies, nights pass by, the stages of life leave us one by one. Seeing this peril in death, a seeker of peace would drop the world’s bait.”
