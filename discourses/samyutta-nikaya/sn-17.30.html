Saṃyutta Nikāya · Discourse 17.30

A Mendicant With Defilements Ended

Bhikkhusutta

Setting
Sāvatthī
Speakers
The Buddha and Venerable Ānanda, in dialogue
Form
A direct claim, a follow-up question, and a careful clarifying answer
Length
~2 minutes to read
Northern parallel
A rough counterpart may exist in the Chinese Saṃyukta-āgama (T99), though this reading guide does not assert a specific matching sutra number
Difficulty
★★★★☆ — the vagga's doctrinally most refined discourse, closing on a distinction rather than a warning alone

Why this discourse

This discourse closes Tatiyavagga with its most surprising claim yet: possessions, honor, and popularity are an obstacle even for a mendicant who is perfected, with defilements ended — an arahant. Venerable Ānanda presses the Buddha on this directly: what could they possibly obstruct for someone already fully liberated? The Buddha's answer draws a precise line: he doesn't say possessions, honor, and popularity threaten an arahant's unshakable freedom of heart, which cannot be shaken by anything; he does say they can obstruct that same arahant's achievement of blissful meditative dwelling in this life, if they are diligent, keen, and resolute.

Reading guide

A claim that seems to contradict everything said about liberation

Every other discourse in this saṃyutta discusses danger for trainees, mendicants, or laypeople still capable of failure; opening with the same danger applying to someone with defilements ended forces the reader to ask what, exactly, could still be at risk for such a person.

Ānanda's question doing necessary work

Rather than let the Buddha's opening claim stand unexplained, the discourse has Ānanda ask directly what possessions, honor, and popularity obstruct for an arahant — a question the reader would otherwise be left to guess at.

A line drawn between liberation and its expression

The Buddha's answer distinguishes akuppā cetovimutti, the arahant's unshakable freedom of heart, which nothing can obstruct, from diṭṭhadhammasukhavihāra, blissful meditative dwelling in this present life, which can still be interfered with even after liberation is secure.

A condition attached even to this narrower danger

The obstruction applies specifically to an arahant who is appamatta, ātāpī, pahitatta — diligent, keen, and resolute — suggesting the danger interferes with the ongoing quality and depth of practice rather than with liberation's security, which the discourse treats as entirely unaffected.

A fitting close to the whole vagga's arc

Having tested trainees, mendicants, laypeople, and even hypothetical ascetics and brahmins, this closing discourse extends the same concern to its final and most difficult case — showing the danger's reach as remarkably thorough, while also showing precisely where that reach actually stops.

Key terms

arahaṁ khīṇāsavo — “perfected, with defilements ended” — the discourse's term for a fully liberated arahant, the subject of its opening claim.
akuppā cetovimutti — “unshakable freedom of heart” — the arahant's liberation itself, which the Buddha explicitly says possessions, honor, and popularity cannot obstruct.
diṭṭhadhammasukhavihāra — “blissful meditative dwelling in this life” — the specific attainment the Buddha says can still be obstructed, even for an arahant.
appamattassa ātāpino pahitattassa — “diligent, keen, and resolute” — the condition attached to the arahant for whom this obstruction applies.
uppannaṁ … pajahissāma — “we will give up [what has] arisen” — the discourse's closing training instruction, addressed to Ānanda and, through him, to the assembly.

The text

The discourse in full, including the closing training instruction. Translation: Bhikkhu Sujato (CC0, SuttaCentral).

§1At Sāvatthī. “Mendicants, possessions, honor, and popularity are an obstacle even for a mendicant who is perfected, with defilements ended.” When he said this, Venerable Ānanda said to the Buddha, “Sir, what do possessions, honor, and popularity obstruct for a mendicant with defilements ended?” “Ānanda, I don’t say that possessions, honor, and popularity obstruct the unshakable freedom of heart. But I do say that possessions, honor, and popularity obstruct the achievement of blissful meditations in this life for a meditator who is diligent, keen, and resolute. So grim are possessions, honor, and popularity—bitter and harsh, an obstacle to reaching the supreme sanctuary from the yoke. So you should train like this: ‘We will give up arisen possessions, honor, and popularity, and we won’t let them occupy our minds.’ That’s how you should train.”
