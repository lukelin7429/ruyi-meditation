Saṃyutta Nikāya · Discourse 22.56

Rounds of the Grasping Aggregates

Upādānaparivattasutta — Upayavagga

Setting
Sāvatthī
Speakers
The Buddha alone, addressing the assembled mendicants
Form
A precise technical definition of each aggregate, its origin, cessation, and the path to cessation, run through all five in turn
Length
~6 minutes to read
Northern parallel
A rough counterpart may exist in the Chinese Saṃyukta-āgama (T99), though this reading guide does not assert a specific matching sutra number
Difficulty
★★★★☆ — one of the most technically precise discourses in the book, worth reading slowly for its exact definitions

Why this discourse

This discourse makes the same withheld-until-understood claim SN 22.26 made earlier in the book, but frames the required understanding differently: as long as the Buddha did not truly understand the five grasping aggregates in “four rounds” (catuparivaṭṭa), he did not announce his awakening. The four rounds are directly knowing each aggregate itself, its origin, its cessation, and the practice leading to its cessation — the same shape as the four noble truths, now applied individually to each of the five aggregates. What makes this discourse unusually valuable is the precision of what follows: each aggregate receives its own exact definition and its own specific originating condition, not a generic formula repeated identically five times.

Reading guide

Four rounds, in the shape of the four noble truths

The discourse's structural announcement echoes SN 22.26's withheld-until-understood claim, but specifies the required understanding as four rounds per aggregate: directly knowing the aggregate itself, its origin, its cessation, and the practice leading to its cessation. This is the four noble truths' own shape — a thing, its arising, its ending, and the path — applied at a more granular level, once for each of the five aggregates rather than once for suffering as a whole.

Form: the four principal states and what derives from them

Form is defined precisely as the four principal states (mahābhūta — earth, water, fire, and air) and form derived from them. Its origin is nutriment (āhāra, rendered here as “fuel”); when fuel ceases, form ceases. This is a distinctive originating condition — not craving in general, but the specific physical process of nutriment.

Feeling, perception, and choices: six classes, one shared origin

Feeling, perception, and choices are each defined by six classes corresponding to the six sense doors (eye through mind), and each is said to originate from contact (phassa); when contact ceases, each of the three ceases. Three different aggregates, defined by different content, share the identical originating and ceasing condition — a precision not spelled out this way elsewhere in the book.

Consciousness: originating from name and form

Consciousness, likewise defined by six classes corresponding to the six sense doors, is given a distinctive originating condition of its own: name and form (nāmarūpa); when name and form cease, consciousness ceases. This directly recalls the mutual-conditioning relationship between consciousness and name-and-form explored at length in Book II's Nidānavagga, now stated in this discourse as a simple, direct dependency.

The identical path named for every aggregate

Despite the different originating conditions for each aggregate, the practice leading to each one's cessation is stated identically every time: the noble eightfold path, spelled out in full — right view, right purpose, right speech, right action, right livelihood, right effort, right mindfulness, and right immersion. The causes differ aggregate by aggregate; the remedy does not.

Key terms

catuparivaṭṭa — “four rounds” — the discourse's title concept, understanding each aggregate itself plus its origin, cessation, and path to cessation.
mahābhūta — “the four principal states” — earth, water, fire, and air, defining the aggregate of form together with what derives from them.
āhārasamudayā rūpasamudayo — “form originates from fuel” — form's specific originating condition, nutriment, distinct from the originating conditions given for the other aggregates.
phassasamudayā — “originates from contact” — the shared originating condition given identically for feeling, perception, and choices.
nāmarūpasamudayā viññāṇasamudayo — “consciousness originates from name and form” — consciousness's distinctive originating condition, recalling Book II's exploration of this mutual dependency.

The text

The discourse in full. Elided repetitions across the four-round definitions (perception and choices, each following the same pattern spelled out in full for form, feeling, and consciousness) are given exactly as bilara-data preserves them. Translation: Bhikkhu Sujato (CC0, SuttaCentral).

§1“Mendicants, there are these five grasping aggregates. What five? The grasping aggregates of form, feeling, perception, choices, and consciousness. As long as I didn’t truly understand these five grasping aggregates in four rounds, I didn’t announce my supreme perfect awakening in this world with its gods, Māras, and Divinities, this population with its ascetics and brahmins, its gods and humans. But when I did truly understand these five grasping aggregates in four rounds, I announced my supreme perfect awakening in this world with its gods, Māras, and Divinities, this population with its ascetics and brahmins, its gods and humans.

§2And how are there four rounds? I directly knew form, its origin, its cessation, and the practice that leads to its cessation.

§3consciousness, its origin, its cessation, and the practice that leads to its cessation.

§4And what is form? The four principal states, and form derived from the four principal states. This is called form. Form originates from fuel. When fuel ceases, form ceases. The practice that leads to the cessation of form is simply this noble eightfold path, that is: right view, right purpose, right speech, right action, right livelihood, right effort, right mindfulness, and right immersion.

§5Whatever ascetics and brahmins have directly known form in this way—and its origin, its cessation, and the practice that leads to its cessation—and are practicing for disillusionment, dispassion, and cessation regarding form: they are practicing well. Those who practice well have a footing in this teaching and training.

§6Those ascetics and brahmins who have directly known form in this way—and its origin, its cessation, and the practice that leads to its cessation—and due to disillusionment, dispassion, and cessation regarding form, are freed by not grasping: they are well freed. Those who are well freed are consummate ones. For consummate ones, there is no cycle of rebirths to be found.

§7And what is feeling? There are these six classes of feeling: feeling born of contact through the eye, ear, nose, tongue, body, and mind. This is called feeling. Feeling originates from contact. When contact ceases, feeling ceases. The practice that leads to the cessation of feelings is simply this noble eightfold path …

§8And what is perception? There are these six classes of perception: perceptions of sights, sounds, smells, tastes, touches, and ideas. This is called perception. Perception originates from contact. When contact ceases, perception ceases. The practice that leads to the cessation of perceptions is simply this noble eightfold path …

§9And what are choices? There are these six classes of intention: intention regarding sights, sounds, smells, tastes, touches, and ideas. These are called choices. Choices originate from contact. When contact ceases, choices cease. The practice that leads to the cessation of choices is simply this noble eightfold path …

§10And what is consciousness? There are these six classes of consciousness: eye, ear, nose, tongue, body, and mind consciousness. This is called consciousness. Consciousness originates from name and form. When name and form cease, consciousness ceases. The practice that leads to the cessation of consciousness is simply this noble eightfold path, that is: right view, right purpose, right speech, right action, right livelihood, right effort, right mindfulness, and right immersion.

§11Whatever ascetics and brahmins have directly known consciousness in this way—and its origin, its cessation, and the practice that leads to its cessation—and are practicing for disillusionment, dispassion, and cessation regarding consciousness: they are practicing well. Those who practice well have a footing in this teaching and training.

§12Those ascetics and brahmins who have directly known consciousness in this way—and its origin, its cessation, and the practice that leads to its cessation—and due to disillusionment, dispassion, and cessation regarding consciousness, are freed by not grasping: they are well freed. Those who are well freed are consummate ones. For consummate ones, there is no cycle of rebirths to be found.”
