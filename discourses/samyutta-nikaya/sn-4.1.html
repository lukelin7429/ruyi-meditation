Saṃyutta Nikāya · Discourse 4.1

Mortification

Tapokammasutta

Setting
At Uruvelā, at the root of the goatherd's banyan tree on the bank of the Nerañjarā River, in the days just after the Buddha's awakening
Speakers
The Buddha and Māra the Wicked
Form
A private reflection overheard, a challenging verse, and a rebuttal verse
Length
~1.5 minutes to read
Northern parallel
A rough counterpart may exist in the Chinese Saṃyukta-āgama (T99), though this reading guide does not assert a specific matching sutra number
Difficulty
★★★☆☆ — sets the pattern this entire saṃyutta will repeat with variations

Why this discourse

This discourse opens the Mārasaṃyutta at the earliest possible moment: just after the Buddha's awakening, still at the site of his enlightenment, privately relieved to be free of grueling ascetic mortification. Māra reads this thought and confronts him directly — you've abandoned the very practice that purifies, and only think yourself pure. The Buddha's reply does not defend mortification as once useful but now surpassed; it declares the practice pointless from the start, as futile as rowing a boat on dry land, with ethics, immersion, and wisdom the only path that actually led to purity.

Reading guide

A saṃyutta opening at the earliest possible moment

Unlike most discourses in this book, which give no indication of when in the Buddha's life they occur, this one is explicitly set in the immediate aftermath of awakening, at Uruvelā itself — establishing Māra as a figure who begins testing the Buddha from the very first days of his teaching career.

A private thought, overheard rather than spoken

The confrontation does not begin with a question or a vision; it begins with Māra reading the Buddha's own private relief at being ‘freed from that grueling work’, a detail establishing throughout this saṃyutta that Māra's challenges often respond to something internal rather than anything outwardly said or done.

An accusation aimed at the Buddha's own credibility

Māra's challenge is not a generic threat but a targeted claim about legitimacy: mortification is what actually purifies, and the Buddha, having abandoned it, is ‘impure but thinks himself pure’ — an attack on the very foundation of his claim to awakening, not merely an attempt to frighten or distract.

Mortification declared futile, not merely surpassed

The Buddha's rebuttal does not concede that austerities once had value now exceeded by a better path; the simile of oars and a rudder on dry land describes the entire practice as having been useless activity from the start, never actually moving toward the goal it claimed to serve.

A refrain this whole saṃyutta will repeat

The Buddha's closing line, ‘you're beaten, terminator’, and Māra's own reaction — realizing he has been recognized, and vanishing miserable and sad — establish a pattern of confrontation and defeat this collection repeats, with variation, across its following twenty-four discourses.

Key terms

tapokamma — “the practice of mortification” — the ascetic austerities Māra accuses the Buddha of wrongly abandoning, giving this discourse its title.
Māra — “Māra the Wicked” — the god of temptation, deceit, and death, this saṃyutta's central antagonist, here appearing for the first time.
antaka — “terminator” — an epithet for Māra as death itself, closing the Buddha's rebuttal verse.
sīla samādhi paññā — “ethics, immersion, and wisdom” — the threefold path the Buddha names as what actually led to ultimate purity.
Nerañjarā — “the Nerañjarā River” — the river at Uruvelā beside which the Buddha was newly awakened when this confrontation occurs.

The text

The discourse in full: the Buddha's private reflection, Māra's challenge, and the rebuttal verse. Translation: Bhikkhu Sujato (CC0, SuttaCentral).

§1So I have heard. At one time, when he was first awakened, the Buddha was staying in Uruvelā at the root of the goatherd’s banyan tree on the bank of the Nerañjarā River. Then as he was in private retreat this thought came to his mind, “I am truly freed from that grueling work! Thank goodness I’m freed from that pointless grueling work. Thank goodness I’m freed and have attained awakening.”

§2And then Māra the Wicked, knowing the Buddha’s train of thought, went up to him and addressed him in verse:

§3“You’ve left the practice of mortification by which sons of men purify themselves. You’re impure, but think yourself pure; you’ve strayed from the path of purity.”

§4Then the Buddha, knowing that this was Māra the Wicked, replied to him in verse:

§5“I realized that it’s pointless; all that endless mortification is as futile as oars and rudder on dry land. Ethics, immersion, and wisdom: by developing this path to awakening I attained ultimate purity. You’re beaten, terminator!”

§6Then Māra the Wicked, thinking, “The Buddha knows me! The Holy One knows me!” miserable and sad, vanished right there.
