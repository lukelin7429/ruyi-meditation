Saṃyutta Nikāya · Discourse 35.64

With Migajāla (2nd)

Dutiyamigajālasutta — Migajālavagga

Setting
Sāvatthī (continuing from SN 35.63)
Speakers
Migajāla requesting a teaching in brief; the Buddha answering; the narrator reporting Migajāla's later attainment
Form
A request for brevity, a shortened sixfold chain now closing on suffering's origin and cessation rather than SN 35.63's own “alone/with partner” framing, then a narrated epilogue
Length
~2 minutes to read
Northern parallel
A rough counterpart may exist in the Chinese Saṃyukta-āgama (T99), though this reading guide does not assert a specific matching sutra number
Difficulty
★★☆☆☆ — a familiar chain, but with an unusually specific narrated outcome

Why this discourse

Migajāla returns and asks the Buddha for a teaching “in brief” so that he can go practice alone, withdrawn, diligent, keen, and resolute. The Buddha repeats the same six-sense chain from SN 35.63, but shortened, and drops the “living alone/with a partner” framing entirely — closing instead on “relishing is the origin of suffering, I say” and its mirror, “when relishing ceases, suffering ceases, I say” — the second and third noble truths' own causal logic applied directly to the six sense fields. Distinctively for this vagga, its own narrated epilogue tracks Migajāla's actual subsequent practice and attainment — not a hypothetical or generic mendicant, but the very man who just asked the question, reported by name, closing on this project's own now-familiar full liberation formula. It is the first discourse since Book III's own Sāriputtasaṃyutta (SN 28) to grant a named individual a followed-through, first-person attainment within a single dialogue.

Reading guide

A brief teaching, requested

Migajāla asks for the Dhamma “in brief” so that he can go practice alone — the same word, eka vihāra, SN 35.63 has just finished redefining, now used by Migajāla himself with that redefinition already in view.

The same six senses, a new closing verb

Where SN 35.63 closed each sweep with “living with a partner” or “living alone,” this discourse closes instead on samudaya and its cessation — relishing declared the origin of suffering, its absence declared suffering's cessation.

A named epilogue

Unlike most discourses in this saṃyutta, which end on the teaching itself, this one continues: Migajāla approves, bows, departs, and — living alone, withdrawn, diligent, keen, and resolute — soon realizes the goal for himself, reported by name.

Achieving what gentlemen go forth for

The epilogue uses this project's own full standard liberation formula — “rebirth is ended, the spiritual journey has been completed” — closing with Migajāla explicitly “becoming one of the perfected,” an arahant.

Key terms

saṅkhittena — in brief — Migajāla's own request, opening this discourse
dukkhasamudaya — the origin of suffering — what relishing is here declared to be
nandi — relishing — carried over from SN 35.63, now the discourse's own single pivot
appamatta — diligent — part of the standard formula describing Migajāla's own solitary practice
arahant — one of the perfected — what Migajāla becomes by this discourse's own close

The text

The complete discourse: Migajāla's request, the Buddha's shortened teaching, and the narrated epilogue of Migajāla's own attainment. Translation: Bhikkhu Sujato (CC0, SuttaCentral).

§1Then Venerable Migajāla went up to the Buddha … and said to him: “Sir, may the Buddha please teach me Dhamma in brief. When I’ve heard it, I’ll live alone, withdrawn, diligent, keen, and resolute.”

§2“Migajāla, there are sights known by the eye, which are likable, desirable, agreeable, pleasant, sensual, and arousing. If a mendicant approves, welcomes, and keep clinging to them, this gives rise to relishing. Relishing is the origin of suffering, I say. There are sounds … smells … tastes … touches … ideas known by the mind, which are likable, desirable, agreeable, pleasant, sensual, and arousing. If a mendicant approves, welcomes, and keeps clinging to them, this gives rise to relishing. Relishing is the origin of suffering, I say.

§3There are sights known by the eye, which are likable, desirable, agreeable, pleasant, sensual, and arousing. If a mendicant doesn’t approve, welcome, and keep clinging to them, relishing ceases. When relishing ceases, suffering ceases, I say. There are sounds … smells … tastes … touches … ideas known by the mind, which are likable, desirable, agreeable, pleasant, sensual, and arousing. If a mendicant doesn’t approve, welcome, and keep clinging to them, relishing ceases. When relishing ceases, suffering ceases, I say.”

§4And then Venerable Migajāla approved and agreed with what the Buddha said. He got up from his seat, bowed, and respectfully circled the Buddha, keeping him on his right, before leaving. Then Migajāla, living alone, withdrawn, diligent, keen, and resolute, soon realized the supreme end of the spiritual path in this very life. He lived having achieved with his own insight the goal for which gentlemen rightly go forth from the lay life to homelessness. He understood: “Rebirth is ended; the spiritual journey has been completed; what had to be done has been done; there is nothing further for this place.” And Migajāla became one of the perfected.
