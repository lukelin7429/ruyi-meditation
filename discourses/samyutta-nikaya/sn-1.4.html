Saṃyutta Nikāya · Discourse 1.4

Time Flies

Accentisutta

Why this discourse

This discourse is best read directly alongside SN 1.3, the one immediately before it. Its opening image changes — time flying, nights passing, the stages of life leaving one by one — but its closing two lines, in both the deity's verse and the Buddha's, are identical, word for word, to SN 1.3's. The vagga is showing the same conclusion is reachable from more than one starting image of impermanence.

Reading guide

A new opening image

Where SN 1.3 pictured life as continuously ‘led onward,’ this discourse pictures time itself in motion: ‘time flies, nights pass by, the stages of life leave us one by one’ (vayoguṇā anupubbaṁ jahanti). The image has shifted from being carried to being left behind, piece by piece, as time moves past.

The identical closing couplet

From ‘seeing this peril in death’ onward, this verse and SN 1.3's match exactly: the deity again counsels ‘do good deeds that bring you joy,’ and the Buddha again answers that ‘a seeker of peace would drop the world's bait.’ Nothing here is a fresh answer — it is the same answer, reached from a different starting point.

Why the vagga repeats itself

Reciting several distinct images of impermenance — being led onward, time flying, the stages of life falling away — that all resolve to the same closing counsel is itself a rhetorical shape, not a redundancy to smooth over. It is closer to variations on a theme than to a single verse told twice.

Vayoguṇā, the stages of life

Vayoguṇā names the successive phases a life passes through — commentarial tradition reads this as childhood, youth, and old age — each one dropped in turn (anupubbaṁ jahanti, ‘left behind in order’) as time moves the person through them.

Key terms

accenti — “flies, passes swiftly” — the verb this discourse's title comes from, describing time's motion rather than the person's.
vayoguṇā — “the stages of life” — traditionally read as childhood, youth, and old age, each one left behind in turn as time passes.
anupubbaṁ jahanti — “leave us one by one, in order” — describing how the stages of life fall away successively, not all at once.
lokāmisa — “the world's bait” — the same image from SN 1.3, worldly pleasure as bait set to hook a creature, repeated here word for word.
santipekkho — “one who seeks peace” — the same description, again shared verbatim with SN 1.3's closing line.

The text

The discourse in full: a new opening image of impermanence, closed with the same answer already given at SN 1.3. Translation: Bhikkhu Sujato (CC0, SuttaCentral).

§1At Sāvatthī. Standing to one side, that deity spoke this verse in the Buddha’s presence:

§2“Time flies, nights pass by, the stages of life leave us one by one. Seeing this peril in death, do good deeds that bring you joy.”

§3“Time flies, nights pass by, the stages of life leave us one by one. Seeing this peril in death, a seeker of peace would drop the world’s bait.”
