Saṃyutta Nikāya · Discourse 35.69

Upasena and the Viper

Upasenaāsīvisasutta — Migajālavagga

Setting
Rājagaha, the Cool Grove, under the Snake's Hood Grotto
Speakers
Venerable Upasena addressing the mendicants; Venerable Sāriputta questioning him
Form
Pure narrative: a viper's bite, Upasena's calm request, Sāriputta's puzzled question, Upasena's diagnostic answer, Sāriputta's own diagnosis, and the predicted outcome fulfilled
Length
~2 minutes to read
Northern parallel
A rough counterpart may exist in the Chinese Saṃyukta-āgama (T99), though this reading guide does not assert a specific matching sutra number
Difficulty
★★★☆☆ — narrative pathos paired with a philosophically dense diagnosis

Why this discourse

This is Migajālavagga's own most dramatic single discourse: a viper falls on Venerable Upasena's body, and rather than showing alarm, he calmly instructs the mendicants around him to lift his body onto a cot and carry it outside “before it's scattered right here like a handful of hay” — a traditional image for the dissolving effect of certain venoms. Sāriputta, seeing no visible injury or deterioration, questions this urgency; Upasena answers that impairment would only afflict someone who thinks “I am the eye” or “the eye is mine” (and so across all six sense fields) — but he does not think this way. Sāriputta's own diagnosis names what Upasena has long ago eradicated: I-making (ahaṅkāra), mine-making (mamaṅkāra), and the underlying tendency to conceit (mānānusaya). The mendicants carry his body outside, and it disintegrates exactly as he predicted. This discourse stages, as literal embodied fact in the face of death, the very “this is not mine, I am not this, this is not my self” formula that structures the vagga's own closing triad still to come (SN 35.71–73).

Reading guide

A viper's bite, and no fear

Upasena, bitten, shows no alarm — only a calm, practical instruction to move his own body before it “scatters like a handful of hay,” a traditional image for certain venoms' dissolving effect.

Sāriputta's puzzlement, and Upasena's answer

Seeing no visible injury, Sāriputta asks why Upasena speaks this way; Upasena answers that impairment would only afflict one who identifies with the eye (and the remaining five fields) as “I” or “mine” — which he does not.

The diagnosis: I-making, mine-making, conceit, uprooted

Sāriputta names what Upasena has long since eradicated: ahaṅkāra (I-making), mamaṅkāra (mine-making), and mānānusaya (the underlying tendency to conceit) — the same underlying-tendency vocabulary this vagga's own closing discourses will approach differently.

The prediction fulfilled

The mendicants carry Upasena's body outside, and it disintegrates exactly as he foretold — this project's first narrative to close on a physical prediction stated, then confirmed, within the same discourse.

Key terms

āsīvisa — viper — the animal whose bite opens this discourse
ahaṅkāra — I-making — the first of the three things Sāriputta diagnoses as eradicated
mamaṅkāra — mine-making — the second
mānānusaya — the underlying tendency to conceit — the third, and subtlest
Sappasoṇḍikapabbhāra — the Snake's Hood Grotto — this discourse's own setting in the Cool Grove at Rājagaha

The text

The complete discourse: Upasena's request, Sāriputta's question and Upasena's answer, and the diagnosis and outcome. Translation: Bhikkhu Sujato (CC0, SuttaCentral).

§1At one time the venerables Sāriputta and Upasena were staying near Rājagaha in the Cool Grove, under the Snake’s Hood Grotto. Now at that time a viper fell on Upasena’s body. He addressed the mendicants, “Come, reverends, lift this body onto a cot and carry it outside before it’s scattered right here like a handful of hay.”

§2When he said this, Sāriputta said to him, “But we don’t see any impairment in your body or deterioration of your faculties. Yet you say: ‘Come, reverends, lift this body onto a cot and carry it outside before it’s scattered right here like a handful of hay.’” “Reverend Sāriputta, there may be an impairment in body or deterioration of faculties for someone who thinks: ‘I am the eye’ or ‘the eye is mine.’ Or ‘I am the ear … nose … tongue … body …’ Or ‘I am the mind’ or ‘the mind is mine.’ But I don’t think like that. So why would there be an impairment in my body or deterioration of my faculties?”

§3“That must be because Venerable Upasena has long ago totally eradicated I-making, mine-making, and the underlying tendency to conceit. That’s why it doesn’t occur to you: ‘I am the eye’ or ‘the eye is mine.’ Or ‘I am the ear … nose … tongue … body …’ Or ‘I am the mind’ or ‘the mind is mine.’” Then those mendicants lifted Upasena’s body onto a cot and carried it outside. And his body was scattered right there like a handful of hay.
