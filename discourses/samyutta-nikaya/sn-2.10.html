Saṃyutta Nikāya · Discourse 2.10

The Sun

Sūriyasutta

Setting
At Sāvatthī; the events described take place in the sky during a solar eclipse
Speakers
The Sun God, the Buddha, Rāhu, and Vepacitti
Form
A narrative built from verses closely paralleling the preceding discourse, with one added couplet
Length
~1.5 minutes to read
Northern parallel
A rough counterpart may exist in the Chinese Saṃyukta-āgama (T99), though this reading guide does not assert a specific matching sutra number
Difficulty
★★★☆☆ — closely parallel to SN 2.9, with one notable addition worth reading for

Why this discourse

This discourse closely parallels SN 2.9, substituting a solar eclipse for a lunar one and the Sun God for the Moon God, with Rāhu again seizing, releasing, and explaining himself to Vepacitti in nearly identical terms. The one clear addition is a couplet describing the sun as a beacon in the darkness, a blazing circle of awesome might, and the Buddha's closing appeal naming the Sun ‘my offspring’ — a term of endearment, not a literal genealogical claim. This discourse closes the Paṭhamavagga, the first sub-chapter of the Devaputtasaṃyutta.

Reading guide

A near-repeat, not an identical one

Most of this discourse's verses track SN 2.9's almost word for word — the Moon God's refuge formula becomes the Sun God's, Rāhu's fear of a head exploding in seven pieces recurs unchanged — making the differences worth noticing precisely because they are so few.

An added couplet, describing the sun itself

Unlike SN 2.9, this discourse's version of the Buddha's command to Rāhu adds two extra lines describing the sun directly: a beacon in the darkness, the blazing sun, a circle of awesome might, warning Rāhu not to swallow him as he traverses the sky.

‘My offspring’, an endearment

The Buddha's verse calls the Sun ‘my offspring’ (putta), a term of affection common in address across early Buddhist verse, not a claim that the Buddha and the Sun God share any literal kinship.

A vagga-closing discourse without a colophon

Unlike SN 1's vagga-closing discourses, several of which carried distinctive closing features, this discourse's final segments in the source are blank — the untranslated uddāna, the chapter's own summary verse, consistent with how this collection's compilers have handled such closings elsewhere and not quoted here.

Two eclipse myths, one purpose

Read together, SN 2.9 and SN 2.10 form a matched pair: both cosmic bodies, both seized and released, both preserved today as protection chants — the moon and sun treated as parallel cases of the same underlying rescue, closing the Paṭhamavagga on a note of shared cosmic order restored.

Key terms

Suriya — “the Sun God” — the deity seized by Rāhu in this discourse, paralleling Candimā in SN 2.9.
obhāsati — “a beacon in the darkness” (literally, illuminates) — part of the added couplet describing the sun directly, absent from SN 2.9's parallel verse.
teja — “awesome might” — describing the sun's blazing circle in the same added couplet.
putta — “offspring” — the term of endearment the Buddha uses in addressing the Sun in his closing appeal to Rāhu.
uddāna — “summary verse” — the untranslated, blank closing segments in the source marking the end of the Paṭhamavagga, not quoted in this reading guide's text.

The text

The discourse in full, closely paralleling SN 2.9 with one added couplet noted in the reading guide; the closing uddāna is blank in the source and not quoted here. Translation: Bhikkhu Sujato (CC0, SuttaCentral).

§1At Sāvatthī. Now at that time the Sun God had been seized by Rāhu, lord of titans. Then the Sun God, recollecting the Buddha, at that time spoke this verse:

§2“Homage to you, Buddha, hero! You’re free in every way. I’ve wandered into confinement: be my refuge!”

§3Then the Buddha addressed Rāhu in verse concerning the Sun God:

§4“The Sun God has gone for refuge to the Realized One, the perfected one. The Buddhas have compassion for the world—so Rāhu, release the Sun! He is a beacon in the darkness, the blazing sun, circle of awesome might. Rāhu, do not swallow him as he traverses the sky. Rāhu, release my offspring, the Sun!”

§5Then Rāhu, having released the Sun, rushed to see Vepacitti, lord of titans and stood to one side, shocked and awestruck. Vepacitti addressed him in verse:

§6“Why the rush? Rāhu, you released the Sun and came here looking like you’re in shock: why do you stand there so scared?”

§7“My head would have exploded in seven pieces, I would have found no joy in life, if, when enchanted by the Buddha’s verse, I had not released the Sun.”
