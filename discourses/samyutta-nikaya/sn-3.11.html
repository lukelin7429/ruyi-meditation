Saṃyutta Nikāya · Discourse 3.11

Seven Matted-Hair Ascetics

Sattajaṭilasutta

Setting
Near Sāvatthī, at the stilt longhouse of Migāra’s mother in the Eastern Monastery
Speakers
The Buddha and King Pasenadi of Kosala
Form
A witnessed scene, the Buddha's correction, and a narrative twist closed by a two-stanza verse
Length
~3.5 minutes to read
Northern parallel
A rough counterpart may exist in the Chinese Saṃyukta-āgama (T99), though this reading guide does not assert a specific matching sutra number
Difficulty
★★★★☆ — the twist ending recontextualizes everything that came before it

Why this discourse

Thirty-five striking ascetics of five different traditions pass near the Buddha, and King Pasenadi rises to bow reverently before them, announcing his royal name three times. Convinced he has witnessed genuine holiness, he tells the Buddha these must be perfected or nearly so — and is corrected: a sensual layman like Pasenadi cannot easily judge such things, which take long, attentive acquaintance to know. Then comes the twist: these ascetics were Pasenadi's own spies in disguise, returning from a mission, about to be bathed, dressed, and rewarded with sensual pleasures — the very king who bowed to them had engineered their disguise himself.

Reading guide

A king's own theatrical reverence

Pasenadi's bow is described with unusual physical detail — robe arranged over one shoulder, kneeling, cupped palms raised, his name announced three times — a public display of humility from a reigning monarch before figures he has never met, setting up the discourse's central irony before the Buddha says a word.

Four ways to truly know a person, all requiring time

The Buddha's correction is not a flat denial but a positive teaching: ethics, purity, resilience in trouble, and wisdom can each be known — but only through long acquaintance, attentive observation, and by the wise themselves, ruling out judgment from a single passing glance no matter how striking the appearance.

The reveal, delivered almost casually

Pasenadi's confession that the ascetics were his own undercover spies arrives without dramatic buildup, folded into a practical detail about what he'll do with their intelligence — the casualness itself is part of the discourse's point: the king had engineered the very disguise that fooled his own reverence.

The king unwittingly proves the Buddha's teaching

Pasenadi does not merely accept the Buddha's correction in the abstract; his own story becomes its demonstration — the person best positioned to know these figures were not ascetics at all was Pasenadi himself, and even he was taken in by appearance until he remembered his own scheme.

Fake earrings and gilded coins

The closing verse's two similes — clay disguised as jewelry, copper disguised as gold — extend the teaching beyond this one incident to a general claim about how the undisciplined can live disguised as the disciplined, appearing impressive while corrupt within, not limited to the specific case of spies in ascetic costume.

Key terms

jaṭila — “matted-hair ascetic” — one of five ascetic traditions represented among the thirty-five figures who pass by the Buddha.
sīla — “ethics” — the first of four qualities the Buddha says can only be known through long acquaintance.
saṁvāsena — “by living with them” — the specific condition the Buddha names for coming to know a person's ethics.
carā — “spies, undercover agents” — Pasenadi's own term for the ascetics he had just bowed to in reverence.
mattikākuṇḍala — “a fake earring made of clay” — the closing verse's first image for corruption disguised as impressiveness.

The text

The discourse in full, with the king's elided exclamation skipped as blank in the source. Translation: Bhikkhu Sujato (CC0, SuttaCentral).

§1At one time the Buddha was staying near Sāvatthī in the stilt longhouse of Migāra’s mother in the Eastern Monastery. Then in the late afternoon, the Buddha came out of retreat and sat outside the gate. Then King Pasenadi of Kosala went up to the Buddha, bowed, and sat down to one side.

§2Now at that time seven matted-hair ascetics, seven Jain ascetics, seven naked ascetics, seven one-cloth ascetics, and seven wanderers passed by not far from the Buddha. Their armpits and bodies were hairy, and their nails were long; and they carried their pack with shoulder-poles. Then King Pasenadi got up from his seat, arranged his robe over one shoulder, knelt with his right knee on the ground, raised his cupped palms toward those various ascetics, and pronounced his name three times: “Sirs, I am Pasenadi, king of Kosala! … I am Pasenadi, king of Kosala!”

§3Then, soon after those ascetics had left, King Pasenadi went up to the Buddha, bowed, sat down to one side, and said to him, “Sir, these are among those in the world who are perfected or who are on the path to perfection!”

§4“Great king, as a layman enjoying sensual pleasures, living at home with your children, using sandalwood imported from Kāsi, wearing garlands, fragrance, and makeup, and accepting gold and currency, it’s hard for you to know who is perfected or on the path to perfection.

§5You can get to know a person’s ethics by living with them. But only after a long time, not casually; only when attentive, not when inattentive; and only by the wise, not the witless. You can get to know a person’s purity by dealing with them. … You can get to know a person’s resilience in times of trouble. … You can get to know a person’s wisdom by discussion. But only after a long time, not casually; only when attentive, not when inattentive; and only by the wise, not the witless.”

§6“It’s incredible, sir, it’s amazing, how well said this was by the Buddha. …

§7Sir, these are my spies, my undercover agents returning after spying on the country. I shall later make use of what they first spied out. Now—when they have rinsed off the dust and dirt, and are nicely bathed and anointed, with hair and beard dressed, and dressed in white—they will amuse themselves, supplied and provided with the five kinds of sensual stimulation.”

§8Then, understanding this matter, on that occasion the Buddha recited these verses: “It’s not easy to know a man by his appearance. You shouldn’t trust them at first sight. For undisciplined men live in this world disguised as the disciplined. Like a fake earring made of clay, like a copper halfpenny covered with gold, they live hidden in the world, corrupt inside but impressive outside.”
