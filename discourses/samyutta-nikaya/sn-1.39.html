Saṃyutta Nikāya · Discourse 1.39

With Pajjunna's Daughter (1st)

Paṭhamapajjunnadhītusutta

Setting
Near Vesālī, at the Great Wood, in the hall with the peaked roof — a new setting for this vagga
Speakers
Kokanadā, Pajjunna's daughter, speaking alone in devotional verse
Form
Four four-line verses of praise and reflection, with no dialogue or reply from the Buddha
Length
~1.5 minutes to read
Difficulty
★☆☆☆☆ — direct in form
A named individual, with a named father
The first deity in this vagga identified both by her own personal name and by her parentage, rather than as an unnamed member of a group

Why this discourse

Unlike the group discourses earlier in this vagga, this one gives a single named individual the entire floor: Kokanadā, identified as the daughter of Pajjunna, arrives at Vesālī and speaks four verses of her own, naming herself within her own speech, describing a shift from merely having heard the teaching to now knowing it ‘as a witness’ while the Buddha himself teaches, and closing with the contrasting fates of those who denounce the teaching and those who accept it.

Reading guide

A named individual, not a group

Every deity discourse so far in this vagga has featured either an unnamed single deity, an unnamed collective host, or a specific but unnamed class (the Pure Abode deities, the fault-finders). This discourse is the first to name its speaker personally, and by parentage — ‘Pajjunna's daughter’ identifies her specifically as the offspring of Pajjunna, a deity elsewhere associated with rain.

Naming herself, mid-speech

Kokanadā's own verse names herself in the third person even as she speaks — ‘Kokanadā am I who worships him’ — a somewhat unusual self-referential convention, closer to formal self-introduction than to the anonymous questions most deities in this collection ask.

From hearsay to witnessed knowledge

Her second verse draws a specific epistemological distinction: ‘previously I had only heard the teaching realized by the Clear-eyed One’; now, listening to the Buddha teach directly, she knows it ‘as a witness’ (sakkhi) — a distinction between secondhand report and personally verified knowing, made explicit rather than assumed.

Denouncing the teaching, and its stated cost

The closing two verses contrast sharply: those who go about denouncing the teaching of the noble ones fall to the ‘terrible Hell of Screams’ (roruva) and suffer there long, while those who find acceptance and peace in the same teaching swell the hosts of gods after this life. The consequence named is specifically for denunciation of the teaching, not for unrelated wrongdoing.

Key terms

pajjunna — a deity elsewhere associated with rain; Kokanadā is identified throughout this discourse specifically as his daughter.
kokanadā — the name of the deity who speaks this discourse's verses, naming herself within her own speech.
sakkhi — “as a witness, firsthand” — how Kokanadā describes her present knowledge of the teaching, contrasted with merely having heard of it before.
cakkhumantena desite — “taught by the Clear-eyed One” — her description of the Buddha as the teaching's source, using an epithet for clear vision.
roruva — the “Hell of Screams,” the specific destination this discourse names for those who denounce the teaching of the noble ones.

The text

The discourse in full: a named deity's verses on witnessed knowledge and the cost of denouncing the teaching. Translation: Bhikkhu Sujato (CC0, SuttaCentral).

Kokanadā arrives at Vesālī

§1So I have heard. At one time the Buddha was staying near Vesālī, at the Great Wood, in the hall with the peaked roof. Then, late at night, the glorious Kokanadā, Pajjunna’s daughter, lighting up the entire Great Wood, went up to the Buddha, bowed, stood to one side, and recited these verses in the Buddha’s presence:

§2“Staying in the woods of Vesālī is the Buddha, best of creation. Kokanadā am I who worships him, Kokanadā, Pajjuna’s daughter.

From hearsay to witnessed knowledge

§3Previously I had only heard the teaching realized by the Clear-eyed One. But now I know it as a witness while the sage, the Holy One teaches.

Two fates, contrasted

§4There are simpletons who go about denouncing the teaching of the noble ones. They fall into the terrible Hell of Screams where they suffer long.

§5There are those who have found acceptance and peace in the teaching of the noble ones. After giving up this human body, they swell the hosts of gods.”
