Saṃyutta Nikāya · Discourse 22.62

The Scope of Definition

Niruttipathasutta — Upayavagga

Why this discourse

This discourse closes Upayavagga with an unusual subject: not meditation practice or liberation directly, but the correctness of ordinary tensed language. The Buddha names three “scopes of definition, labeling, and description” — was, is, and will be — and applies each strictly according to an aggregate's actual temporal status: an aggregate that has passed, ceased, and perished is correctly called “was,” never “is” or “will be”; one not yet born is correctly called “will be”; one currently arisen is correctly called “is.” The discourse's closing observation is what gives it its real force: even the most radical skeptics and nihilists, who rejected cause, action, and meaning itself, never dared criticize these three basic linguistic categories — out of fear of being blamed and attacked for doing so.

Reading guide

Three tenses, strictly and exclusively assigned

The discourse's central claim is precise: each of the three temporal categories — was, is, will be — applies to an aggregate's status exclusively, never interchangeably. Something that has passed, ceased, and perished is correctly designated “was” and specifically “not ‘is’ or ‘will be’” — the discourse explicitly rules out the other two tenses for each case, not merely naming the correct one.

A structure recalling SN 22.38's three-times rehearsal

This discourse's threefold temporal structure recalls SN 22.38's rehearsal with Ānanda earlier in the book, which likewise divided the aggregates' arising, vanishing, and persisting-change across past, future, and present. Where that discourse addressed the aggregates' impermanent nature directly, this discourse addresses something one level more abstract: the correctness of the language used to describe that nature.

These categories declared uncorrupted since the beginning

The discourse makes a striking claim about these three linguistic categories themselves: they are “uncorrupted, as they have been since the beginning” and will not be corrupted going forward. This is not a claim about the aggregates' impermanence but about the stability of the linguistic convention used to correctly describe that impermanence — a subtle distinction between what changes (the aggregates) and what does not (the rules for correctly talking about them).

Even radical skeptics respected this linguistic bedrock

The discourse's closing observation names, without identifying them individually, “loud and bold advocates of the doctrines of no-cause, inaction, and nihilism” — recalling SN 22.60's named engagement with Pūraṇa Kassapa's fatalistic school — and states that even they never dared to criticize or reject these three basic categories, out of fear of being blamed, attacked, provoked, and faulted. Even the most radical philosophical rejection of cause and meaning stopped short of denying that something which has ended is correctly called “was.”

Key terms

adhivacanapatha, niruttipatha, paññattipatha — “scope of definition, labeling, and description” — the discourse's title concept, the three linguistic categories this discourse examines.
atītaṁ niruddhaṁ vigataṁ — “has passed, ceased, and perished” — the condition correctly designated “was,” and explicitly not “is” or “will be.”
asaṅkiṇṇā, na saṅkīyanti, na saṅkīyissanti — “uncorrupted…not being corrupted now, nor will they be” — the discourse's claim about the stability of these categories themselves, distinct from the aggregates' own impermanence.
ahetuvādā akiriyavādā natthikavādā — “advocates of the doctrines of no-cause, inaction, and nihilism” — recalling SN 22.60's named engagement with Pūraṇa Kassapa's rival school.
nindābhayā gārayhabhayā upārambhabhayā — “for fear of being blamed, attacked, provoked, and faulted” — the discourse's explanation for why even radical skeptics respected this linguistic bedrock.

The text

The discourse in full. Elided repetitions (feeling, perception, and choices, each following the same pattern spelled out in full for form and consciousness) are given exactly as bilara-data preserves them. Translation: Bhikkhu Sujato (CC0, SuttaCentral).

§1“Mendicants, there are these three scopes of definition, labeling, and description. They’re uncorrupted, as they have been since the beginning. They’re not being corrupted now, nor will they be. Sensible ascetics and brahmins don’t look down on them. What three? When form has passed, ceased, and perished, its designation, label, and description is ‘was’. It’s not ‘is’ or ‘will be’.

§2consciousness has passed, ceased, and perished, its designation, label, and description is ‘was’. It’s not ‘is’ or ‘will be’.

§3When form is not yet born, and has not yet appeared, its designation, label, and description is ‘will be’. It’s not ‘is’ or ‘was’.

§4consciousness is not yet born, and has not yet appeared, its designation, label, and description is ‘will be’. It’s not ‘is’ or ‘was’.

§5When form has been born, and has appeared, its designation, label, and description is ‘is’. It’s not ‘was’ or ‘will be’.

§6consciousness has been born, and has appeared, its designation, label, and description is ‘is’. It’s not ‘was’ or ‘will be’.

§7These are the three scopes of definition, labeling, and description. They’re uncorrupted, as they have been since the beginning. They’re not being corrupted now, nor will they be. Sensible ascetics and brahmins don’t look down on them. Even those those who were loud and bold advocates of the doctrines of no-cause, inaction, and nihilism, didn’t imagine that these three scopes of definition should be criticized or rejected. Why is that? For fear of being blamed, attacked, provoked, and faulted.”
