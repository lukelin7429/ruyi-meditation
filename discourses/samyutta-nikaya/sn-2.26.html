Saṃyutta Nikāya · Discourse 2.26

With Rohitassa

Rohitassasutta

Setting
At Sāvatthī; no further narrative detail given
Speakers
The Buddha and the godling Rohitassa, recounting his own former life as a seer
Form
A direct question, a first-person autobiographical account, and a doctrinally central answering verse
Length
~2.5 minutes to read
Northern parallel
A rough counterpart may exist in the Chinese Saṃyukta-āgama (T99), though this reading guide does not assert a specific matching sutra number
Difficulty
★★★★☆ — the narrative is simple, but the closing teaching is among this book's doctrinally weightiest

Why this discourse

Rohitassa asks whether the end of the world can be reached by traveling, and the Buddha says plainly it cannot. Rohitassa confirms this from hard experience: in a past life as a sky-walking seer, faster than an arrow, with a stride spanning ocean to ocean, he traveled for a full hundred years and died along the way, never arriving. The Buddha's reply is one of this collection's most quoted teachings: there is no end to suffering without reaching the end of the world, and the world, its origin, its cessation, and the path to its cessation are all found within this fathom-long body, with its perception and mind.

Reading guide

A question answered before it is explained

The Buddha's initial answer to Rohitassa's question is flat and unadorned — simply that it is not possible — with no immediate elaboration, leaving Rohitassa's own account to supply the experiential weight behind the claim before the fuller teaching arrives.

A seer's spectacular but futile speed

Rohitassa's autobiography describes abilities far beyond ordinary travel — as fast as a light arrow shot across a palm tree's shadow, a stride spanning ocean to ocean — and even this, sustained for a full hundred years without rest beyond eating, drinking, and sleeping, was not enough.

Death along the way, not arrival

The account's ending is stated without embellishment: Rohitassa passed away along the way, having never reached the end of the world — a failure total enough that even his exceptional, near-superhuman means could not overcome it.

The world in a fathom-long body

The Buddha's central teaching redefines the entire question: the world worth describing — its origin, cessation, and the path to that cessation — is not a distant region to be physically reached but is located within ‘this fathom-long carcass with its perception and mind’, meaning within present experience itself.

Two claims held together, not one replacing the other

The Buddha does not simply reverse Rohitassa's assumption; he holds two claims together explicitly — the end of the world can never be reached by traveling, and yet without reaching the end of the world there is no release from suffering — resolved only by relocating where that end is actually to be found.

Key terms

lokassa anta — “the end of the world” — what Rohitassa asks whether it is possible to reach by traveling, and what the Buddha's teaching relocates entirely.
ākāsacārī — “sky-walker” — Rohitassa's description of himself as a seer with psychic powers in his past life.
vyāmamatte kaḷevare — “this fathom-long carcass” — the Buddha's central image, locating the world within the physical body itself.
saññī samanaka — “with its perception and mind” — qualifying the fathom-long body as the seat of the world's origin and cessation, not merely physical matter alone.
lokantagū — “gone to the end of the world” — what an intelligent person who understands the world is said to have achieved, in the closing verse.

The text

The discourse in full: Rohitassa's question, his autobiography as a failed sky-walking seer, and the Buddha's central teaching on the world within the body. Translation: Bhikkhu Sujato (CC0, SuttaCentral).

§1At Sāvatthī. Standing to one side, the godling Rohitassa said to the Buddha: “Sir, is it possible to know or see or reach the end of the world by traveling to a place where there’s no being born, growing old, dying, passing away, or being reborn?” “Reverend, I say it’s not possible to know or see or reach the end of the world by traveling to a place where there’s no being born, growing old, dying, passing away, or being reborn.”

§2“It’s incredible, sir, it’s amazing, how well said this was by the Buddha. Once upon a time, I was a seer called Rohitassa, son of the benefactors. I was a sky-walker with psychic powers. I was as fast as a light arrow easily shot across the shadow of a palm tree by a well-trained expert archer with a strong bow. My stride was such that it could span from the eastern ocean to the western ocean. This wish came to me: ‘I will reach the end of the world by traveling.’ Having such speed and stride, I traveled for my whole lifespan of a hundred years—pausing only to eat and drink, go to the toilet, and sleep to dispel weariness—and I passed away along the way, never reaching the end of the world.

§3It’s incredible, sir, it’s amazing, how well said this was by the Buddha. ‘Reverend, I say it’s not possible to know or see or reach the end of the world by traveling to a place where there’s no being born, growing old, dying, passing away, or being reborn.’”

§4“But Reverend, I also say there’s no making an end of suffering without reaching the end of the world. For it is in this fathom-long carcass with its perception and mind that I describe the world, its origin, its cessation, and the practice that leads to its cessation.

§5The end of the world can never be reached by traveling. But without reaching the end of the world, there’s no release from suffering. So an intelligent person, understanding the world, has completed the spiritual journey and gone to the end of the world. Assuaged, knowing the end of the world, they do not hope for this world or the next.”
