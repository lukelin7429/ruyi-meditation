Why this discourse

The question describes a real hazard: a grove called ‘Deluding,’ resounding with alluring nymphs but haunted by a gang of goblins — how, the speaker asks, will I keep going through it? The answer doesn't address the grove directly. It describes a different path entirely and the vehicle for traveling it: a chariot called ‘unswerving,’ fitted with the wheels of the teaching, its bench-back conscience, its upholstery mindfulness, the teaching itself as driver, and right view running ahead as guide. Anyone, woman or man, who has such a vehicle has already drawn near to extinguishment.

Reading guide

A dangerous grove, answered indirectly

The question's imagery is concrete and unsettling: a specific named place, ‘Deluding’ (mohana), combining alluring beauty with genuine danger. Rather than offering advice for navigating that particular grove, the reply redirects entirely toward a different journey and a different vehicle altogether.

A chariot built entirely from qualities

Every part of the described chariot is an abstraction made concrete: the path itself is called ‘the direct way,’ heading to a place called ‘fearless’; the chariot is ‘unswerving,’ fitted with wheels made of the teaching itself; its bench-back is conscience (hiri), its upholstery mindfulness (sati) — nothing about this vehicle is a literal, physical object.

The teaching as driver, right view as scout

The verse's most striking claim assigns roles within the allegory precisely: the teaching itself (dhamma) drives, while right view (sammādiṭṭhi) runs out ahead, functioning as a scout or outrider rather than as the driver. Understanding correctly leads the way; the teaching as a whole steers.

An explicit, stated inclusivity

The verse's closing line states plainly that ‘any woman or man’ (itthī vā puriso vā) who has this vehicle draws near to extinguishment — an explicit inclusion this collection doesn't always state so directly, closing what could otherwise read as a purely abstract allegory on a note of universal availability.

Key terms

mohanaṁ — “Deluding,” the name the question gives the dangerous grove it describes, combining beguiling nymphs with haunting goblins.
ujuko nāma so maggo — “that path is called ‘the direct way’” — the reply's redirection toward an entirely different journey.
hirī tassa apālambo — “conscience is its bench-back” — the first of several abstract qualities built directly into the chariot's physical structure.
dhammaṁ sārathiṁ brūmi — “I say the teaching is the driver” — assigning the controlling role in the allegory to the teaching as a whole.
itthī vā puriso vā — “any woman or man” — the verse's explicit, stated inclusivity in naming who can possess this vehicle.

The text

The discourse in full: a fearful question about a dangerous grove, answered by an extended chariot allegory for the path. Translation: Bhikkhu Sujato (CC0, SuttaCentral).

A dangerous, deluding grove

§1“It’s resounding with a group of nymphs, but haunted by a gang of goblins! This grove is called ‘Deluding’. How will I keep going?”

A chariot built from named qualities

§2“That path is called ‘the direct way’, and it’s headed for the place called ‘fearless’. The chariot is called ‘unswerving’, fitted with wheels of the teaching. Conscience is its bench-back, mindfulness its upholstery. I say the teaching is the driver, with right view running out in front. Any woman or man who has such a vehicle, by means of this vehicle has drawn near to extinguishment.”
