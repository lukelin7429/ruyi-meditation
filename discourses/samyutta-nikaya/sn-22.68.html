Saṃyutta Nikāya · Discourse 22.68

Not-Self

Anattasutta — Arahantavagga

Setting
Sāvatthī
Speakers
An unnamed mendicant requests a teaching in brief, then explains it back to the Buddha
Form
The identical narrative template as SN 22.66-67, with "not-self" substituted as the central term
Length
~2 minutes to read
Northern parallel
A rough counterpart may exist in the Chinese Saṃyukta-āgama (T99), though this reading guide does not assert a specific matching sutra number
Difficulty
★☆☆☆☆ — the third term of this vagga's four-part quartet

Why this discourse

This discourse completes the traditional three marks within the quartet SN 22.66 opened: give up desire for whatever is not-self. With impermanence, suffering, and now not-self all given the identical treatment — a direct claim, followed by the mendicant's first-person resolution to give up desire — this discourse closes the loop on the standard tilakkhaṇa before SN 22.69 adds one further, related term not part of the traditional three.

Reading guide

The third mark, given the same treatment as the first two

As with impermanence and suffering before it, not-self is stated here as a direct, unqualified claim: form is not-self. No argument connects it back to the previous two marks within this discourse itself — each discourse in the quartet presents its term independently, exactly as SN 22.12-14's original direct-statement triplet did in Aniccavagga.

Completing the traditional three marks within this vagga's narrative frame

With this discourse, the quartet has now delivered impermanence, suffering, and not-self — the full traditional tilakkhaṇa — each through the identical request-and-confirm narrative structure this vagga uses throughout. The three marks, previously explored at length as direct teachings in Aniccavagga, now also exist in this narrative-embedded form.

A resolution stated identically for the third time

The mendicant's confirmed explanation again takes the same first-person shape: form is not-self, and I should give up desire for it. The consistency of this phrasing across all three discourses in the quartet so far emphasizes that the underlying practice — giving up desire — remains constant regardless of which mark motivates it.

One term remaining before the quartet closes

SN 22.69 will complete the quartet with a fourth, related but distinct term — “not belonging to self” (anattaniya) — before the vagga moves to its eighth and final matched-template discourse in SN 22.70.

Key terms

anattā — “not-self” — the term substituted here, completing the traditional three marks within this vagga's quartet.
chandaṁ pajahatha — “give up desire” — the same formula from SN 22.66-67, unchanged in wording.
pajahitabbo me chando — “I should give up desire for it” — the mendicant's first-person resolution, now applied to not-self.
tilakkhaṇa — the “three marks” — impermanence, suffering, and not-self, now completed within this quartet's narrative form.
arahataṁ ahosi — “became one of the perfected” — the identical outcome closing this discourse.

The text

The discourse in full. Elided repetitions (feeling, perception, and choices, each following the same formula spelled out in full for form and consciousness) are given exactly as bilara-data preserves them. Translation: Bhikkhu Sujato (CC0, SuttaCentral).

§1Then a mendicant went up to the Buddha … and asked him, “Sir, may the Buddha please teach me Dhamma in brief. When I’ve heard it, I’ll live alone, withdrawn, diligent, keen, and resolute.” “Mendicant, give up desire for what is not-self.” “Understood, Blessed One! Understood, Holy One!”

§2“But how do you see the detailed meaning of my brief statement?” “Sir, form is not-self; I should give up desire for it.

§3Consciousness is not-self; I should give up desire for it. That’s how I understand the detailed meaning of the Buddha’s brief statement.”

§4“Good, good, mendicant! It’s good that you understand the detailed meaning of what I’ve said in brief like this. Form is not-self; you should give up desire for it.

§5Consciousness is not-self; you should give up desire for it. This is how to understand the detailed meaning of what I said in brief.” … And that mendicant became one of the perfected.
