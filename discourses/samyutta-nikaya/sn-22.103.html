Saṃyutta Nikāya · Discourse 22.103

Ends

Antasutta — Antavagga

Setting
Sāvatthī
Speakers
The Buddha, addressing the mendicants
Form
A fourfold technical definition, following the four-noble-truths template exactly
Length
Short — a compact, formulaic teaching
Northern parallel
The four-truths template recurs widely; this specific application to sakkāya is distinctive to this discourse and its companion, SN 22.105
Difficulty
Moderate — depends on already knowing the four noble truths' structure to appreciate what's being remapped

Why this discourse

Opens Antavagga ("Ends") by mapping the four noble truths' exact template — the thing itself, its origin, its cessation, and the path to its cessation — onto sakkāya, "substantial reality," showing this technical term for self-identification to be structured exactly like suffering itself.

Reading guide

Four "ends," one template

"There are these four ends" — anta, here meaning something like a defining boundary or terminus, not merely an ending: "the end that is substantial reality, the end that is the origin of substantial reality, the end that is the cessation of substantial reality, and the end that is the practice that leads to the cessation of substantial reality."

This is the four noble truths' own template — suffering, its origin, its cessation, the path — applied word for word to sakkāya instead of dukkha, making explicit what the two terms already imply about each other.

Substantial reality defined as the five grasping aggregates

"It should be said: the five grasping aggregates" — the same identification used throughout Khandhavagga to define dukkha itself, confirming that sakkāya and dukkha are, at the level of what they refer to, the same five aggregates viewed under different names.

Origin: craving in its three modes

The origin is craving "mixed up with relishing and greed, taking pleasure there wherever it alights" — specified as craving for sensual pleasures, for existence, and for nonexistence, the same threefold craving formula used throughout the four-truths material across the Canon.

Cessation and path, given in full

Cessation is craving's "fading away... with nothing left over; giving it away, letting it go, releasing it, and not clinging to it" — and the path is, exactly as elsewhere, "simply this noble eightfold path": right view, purpose, speech, action, livelihood, effort, mindfulness, and immersion.

Nothing here is abbreviated or elided — this discourse gives the complete four-part formula in full, setting the pattern its two companion discourses, SN 22.104 and SN 22.105, will each restate with a single term changed.

Key terms

anta — end, boundary, terminus — the term giving Antavagga its name, used here for each of the four defining points
sakkāya — substantial reality — the technical term this discourse defines using the four-truths template
ponobhavikā taṇhā — craving that leads to future lives — the specific formula naming the origin
vibhavataṇhā — craving for nonexistence — the third of the threefold craving formula, alongside craving for sensual pleasure and for continued existence
ariyo aṭṭhaṅgiko maggo — the noble eightfold path — named in full as the practice leading to cessation

The text

The full discourse: the four ends named, then each defined in turn. Translation: Bhikkhu Sujato (CC0, SuttaCentral).

§1At Sāvatthī. “Mendicants, there are these four ends. What four? The end that is substantial reality, the end that is the origin of substantial reality, the end that is the cessation of substantial reality, and the end that is the practice that leads to the cessation of substantial reality.

§2And what is the end that is substantial reality? It should be said: the five grasping aggregates. What five? That is, the grasping aggregates of form, feeling, perception, choices, and consciousness. This is called the end that is substantial reality.

§3And what is the end that is the origin of substantial reality? It’s the craving that leads to future lives, mixed up with relishing and greed, taking pleasure there wherever it alights. That is, craving for sensual pleasures, craving for existence, and craving for nonexistence. This is called the end that is the origin of substantial reality.

§4And what is the end that is the cessation of substantial reality? It’s the fading away and cessation of that very same craving with nothing left over; giving it away, letting it go, releasing it, and not clinging to it. This is called the end that is the cessation of substantial reality.

§5And what is the end that is the practice that leads to the cessation of substantial reality? It is simply this noble eightfold path, that is: right view, right purpose, right speech, right action, right livelihood, right effort, right mindfulness, and right immersion. This is called the end that is the practice that leads to the cessation of substantial reality. These are the four ends.”
