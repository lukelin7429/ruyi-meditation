Saṃyutta Nikāya · Discourse 22.105

Substantial Reality

Sakkāyasutta — Antavagga

Setting
Sāvatthī
Speakers
The Buddha, addressing the mendicants
Form
The same four-part formula as SN 22.103 and SN 22.104, in SN 22.104's "I will teach you" phrasing but under sakkāya's own name
Length
Short — closing the third of three near-identical discourses
Northern parallel
Shares its structure with the widely attested four-noble-truths formula found throughout the Canon
Difficulty
Approachable — its value lies in comparison with its two companions rather than in isolation

Why this discourse

Closes the three-discourse set begun by SN 22.103 and SN 22.104: sakkāya's own name returns, but now delivered in SN 22.104's "I will teach you..." phrasing rather than SN 22.103's "there are these four ends" — the two structural variations recombined with the two available names, completing the set's full permutation.

Reading guide

A third naming, a mixed phrasing

This discourse takes SN 22.104's opening formula — "I will teach you substantial reality, the origin of substantial reality, the cessation of substantial reality, and the practice that leads to the cessation of substantial reality" — and applies it to sakkāya, the term SN 22.103 had introduced with a different opening formula entirely.

The result completes a small but precise structural grid: two terms (sakkāya, dukkha) and two opening formulas ("there are these four ends," "I will teach you"), with SN 22.103 pairing term-one with formula-one, SN 22.104 pairing term-two with formula-two, and this discourse pairing term-one with formula-two.

A small variation worth noticing: continuing vs. ending existence

Where SN 22.103 and SN 22.104 both name the origin's third craving as "craving for nonexistence" (vibhavataṇhā), this discourse phrases the same threefold craving slightly differently: "craving to continue existence, and craving to end existence" — a small wording shift in translation that nonetheless names the same underlying pair.

Why this closes rather than opens the set

Having established the equivalence of sakkāya and dukkha through SN 22.103 and SN 22.104's near-identical structures, this third discourse's slight recombination (same term as 22.103, same phrasing as 22.104) functions as a kind of confirmation: however the pieces are combined, the content underneath remains exactly the same four points about the same five grasping aggregates.

Moving into the vagga's next phase

The three-discourse set on this fourfold structure now complete, Antavagga turns next to a differently shaped trio: SN 22.106's "should be completely understood" formula, followed by the pair of "ascetics and brahmins" discourses that open the vagga's second half.

Key terms

sakkāya — substantial reality — returning here in its own name, closing the three-discourse set
bhavataṇhā — craving to continue existence — this discourse's own phrasing of the second craving in the threefold formula
vibhavataṇhā — craving to end existence — the third craving, phrased here slightly differently than in SN 22.103-104
desessāmi — I will teach — the opening verb shared with SN 22.104, distinguishing this discourse's phrasing from SN 22.103's own
catusacca — the four-truths pattern (not itself a term in this discourse, but the structural template all three discourses in this set share)

The text

The full discourse, closing the three-discourse set with SN 22.104's opening formula applied to sakkāya's own name. Translation: Bhikkhu Sujato (CC0, SuttaCentral).

§1At Sāvatthī. “Mendicants, I will teach you substantial reality, the origin of substantial reality, the cessation of substantial reality, and the practice that leads to the cessation of substantial reality. Listen …

§2And what is substantial reality? It should be said: the five grasping aggregates. What five? That is, the grasping aggregates of form, feeling, perception, choices, and consciousness. This is called substantial reality.

§3And what is the origin of substantial reality? It’s the craving that leads to future lives, mixed up with relishing and greed, taking pleasure there wherever it alights. That is, craving for sensual pleasures, craving to continue existence, and craving to end existence. This is called the origin of substantial reality.

§4And what is the cessation of substantial reality? It’s the fading away and cessation of that very same craving with nothing left over; giving it away, letting it go, releasing it, and not clinging to it. This is called the cessation of substantial reality.

§5And what is the practice that leads to the cessation of substantial reality? It is simply this noble eightfold path, that is: right view, right purpose, right speech, right action, right livelihood, right effort, right mindfulness, and right immersion. This is called the practice that leads to the cessation of substantial reality.”
