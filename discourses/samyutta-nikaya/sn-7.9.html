Saṃyutta Nikāya · Discourse 7.9

With Bhāradvāja of Sundarikā

Sundarikasutta

Setting
On the bank of the Sundarikā river, in the Kosalan lands
Speakers
The brahmin Sundarika Bhāradvāja ("Bhāradvāja of Sundarikā") and the Buddha
Form
An almsround-like encounter, a famous verse on birth and conduct, a dramatic demonstration, and an extended teaching in verse
Length
~6 minutes to read
Northern parallel
A close parallel exists as the Sundarikabhāradvāja Sutta in the Sutta Nipāta, and a rough counterpart may exist in the Chinese Saṃyukta-āgama (T99), though this reading guide does not assert a specific matching sutra number
Difficulty
★★★★★ — the richest and most celebrated discourse in this vagga

Why this discourse

This discourse gives Arahantavagga its most substantial and widely known text, closely paralleled elsewhere in the canon as the Sutta Nipāta's own Sundarikabhāradvāja Sutta. A brahmin performing a fire sacrifice looks for someone worthy to eat the leftover oblation, spots the meditating Buddha, and nearly turns back on seeing his shaven head — before asking the question that draws out one of the canon's most quoted lines: ‘don't ask about birth, ask about conduct.’ What follows escalates through a refused offering, a startling physical demonstration when the rejected oblation is discarded, and finally an extended teaching that relocates the entire apparatus of fire sacrifice — flame, smoke, ashes, purification — onto the inner discipline of a well-tamed mind.

Reading guide

A search for worth that nearly rejects its own answer

The brahmin's initial reaction to the Buddha's shaven head — wanting to turn back, assuming a shaven ascetic couldn't be the worthy recipient he's looking for — almost derails the encounter before it begins, caught only by his own second thought that even some brahmins shave their heads.

Birth dismissed, conduct substituted, in the canon's own words

‘Don't ask about birth, ask about conduct’ opens the Buddha's reply with a directness matching its later fame; the verse doesn't merely soften caste distinctions, it redirects the entire question toward a different, achievable criterion.

A refusal repeated from SN 7.8, now witnessed dramatically

The Buddha's rejection of food ‘enchanted by a verse’ repeats SN 7.8's refusal almost verbatim, but here the consequence of discarding the offering is shown rather than merely stated — the leftover oblation, dropped into water, sizzles and hisses like an iron cauldron heated all day, a vivid, almost alarming physical confirmation of the food's charged nature.

External fire named and set aside before the real teaching begins

The Buddha's extended verses don't simply criticize fire sacrifice in passing; they name it directly — ‘when you're kindling the wood, brahmin, don't imagine this is purity, for it's just external’ — clearing the ground explicitly before offering what replaces it.

A full alternative vocabulary for the entire sacrificial apparatus

Rather than simply rejecting fire sacrifice, the teaching builds a complete internal parallel to every element of it: conceit as the burden carried, anger as smoke, lies as ashes, the tongue as ladle, the heart as fireplace, and a well-tamed self as the light itself — closing with the Dhamma imagined as a lake where knowledge-masters bathe and cross to the far shore with dry limbs.

Key terms

mā jātiṁ puccha caraṇañca puccha — “don't ask about birth, ask about conduct” — the discourse's most celebrated line, opening the Buddha's reply.
gāthābhigītaṁ me abhojaneyyaṁ — “food enchanted by a verse isn't fit for me to eat” — the refusal repeated almost verbatim from SN 7.8.
cicciṭāyati ciṭiciṭāyati sandhūpāyati sampadhūpāyati — “sizzled and hissed, steaming and fuming” — the dramatic sound of the discarded oblation meeting water, compared to a cauldron heated all day.
suddhiṁ amaññi bahiddhā — “don't imagine this is purity, for it's just external” — naming and dismissing fire sacrifice's claim to purify.
ajjhattamevujjalayāmi jotiṁ — “now I light only the inner flame” — the Buddha's own reinterpretation of sacred fire, replacing external kindling entirely.

The text

The discourse in full — the longest and richest text in this vagga, closely paralleled in the Sutta Nipāta. Translation: Bhikkhu Sujato (CC0, SuttaCentral).

§1At one time the Buddha was staying in the Kosalan lands on the bank of the Sundarikā river. Now at that time the brahmin Bhāradvāja of Sundarikā was serving the sacred flame and performing the fire sacrifice on the bank of the Sundarikā. Then he surveyed the four quarters, wondering, “Now who might eat the leftovers of this oblation?” He saw the Buddha meditating at the root of a certain tree with his robe pulled over his head. Taking the leftovers of the oblation in his left hand and a pitcher in the right he approached the Buddha. When he heard Bhāradvāja of Sundarikā’s footsteps the Buddha uncovered his head. Bhāradvāja of Sundarikā thought, “This man is shaven, he is shaven!” And he wanted to turn back. But he thought, “Even some brahmins are shaven. Why don’t I go to him and ask about his birth?”

§2Then the brahmin Bhāradvāja of Sundarikā went up to the Buddha, and said to him, “Sir, what are you by birth?”

§3“Don’t ask about birth, ask about conduct; for the fire of Jātaveda is born from sticks. A steadfast sage, even though from a low class family, is a thoroughbred checked by conscience. Tamed by truth, fulfilled by taming, a complete knowledge master who has completed the spiritual journey—that’s who a sacrificer should woo, and offer a timely oblation to one worthy of a religious donation.”

§4“My sacrificial offering must have been well performed, since I have met such a knowledge master! It’s because I’d never met anyone like you that others ate the leftover oblation. May the worthy Gotama eat! The worthy one is a brahmin.”

§5“Food enchanted by a verse isn’t fit for me to eat. That’s not the principle of those who see, brahmin. The Buddhas cast aside things enchanted with verses. Since there is such a principle, brahmin, that’s how they live. Serve with other food and drink the consummate one, the great seer, with defilements ended and remorse stilled. For he is the field for the seeker of merit.”

§6“Then, Mister Gotama, to whom should I give the leftovers of this oblation?” “Brahmin, I don’t see anyone in this world—with its gods, Māras, and Divinities, this population with its ascetics and brahmins, its gods and humans—who can properly digest these leftovers, except for the Realized One or one of his disciples. Well then, brahmin, throw out those leftovers where there is little that grows, or drop them into water that has no living creatures.”

§7So Bhāradvāja of Sundarikā dropped the leftover oblation in water that had no living creatures. And when those leftovers were placed in the water, they sizzled and hissed, steaming and fuming. Suppose there was an iron cauldron that had been heated all day. If you placed it in the water, it would sizzle and hiss, steaming and fuming. In the same way, when those leftovers were placed in the water, they sizzled and hissed, steaming and fuming.

§8Then the brahmin Bhāradvāja of Sundarikā, shocked and awestruck, went up to the Buddha, and stood to one side. The Buddha addressed him in verse:

§9“When you’re kindling the wood, brahmin, don’t imagine this is purity, for it’s just external. For those who are skilled say this is no way to purity, when one seeks purity in externals. I’ve given up kindling firewood, brahmin, now I light only the inner flame. Always blazing, always serene, I am a perfected one leading the spiritual life.

§10Conceit, brahmin, is the burden of your pack, anger your smoke, and lies your ashes. The tongue is the ladle, the heart the fireplace; a well-tamed self is a person’s light. The teaching is a lake, brahmin, into which ethics are the stairs, unclouded, praised by the fine to the good. There the knowledge masters go to bathe, and cross to the far shore with dry limbs. Truth, principle, restraint, the spiritual life, attaining divinity based on the middle, brahmin— pay homage to the upright ones. I declare that man one who follows the teaching.”

§11When he had spoken, the brahmin Bhāradvāja of Sundarikā said to the Buddha, “Excellent, worthy Gotama …” … And Venerable Bhāradvāja became one of the perfected.
