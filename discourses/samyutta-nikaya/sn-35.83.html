Saṃyutta Nikāya · Discourse 35.83

Phagguna's Question

Phaggunapañhāsutta — Gilānavagga

Setting
Not restated in this discourse
Speakers
The Venerable Phagguna questioning; the Buddha answering
Form
A question run across all six sense faculties — does any exist by which past, fully quenched Buddhas could be described? — answered no for each, closing this vagga with its own colophon and uddāna
Length
~2 minutes to read
Northern parallel
A rough counterpart may exist in the Chinese Saṃyukta-āgama (T99), though this reading guide does not assert a specific matching sutra number
Difficulty
★★★★☆ — a genuinely difficult question about describing what has gone beyond description

Why this discourse

This discourse closes Gilānavagga with its own hardest question. Venerable Phagguna asks whether there exists any eye, ear, nose, tongue, body, or mind by which the fully quenched Buddhas of the past — those who have “cut off proliferation, cut off the course, finished off the cycle, and passed beyond all suffering” — could be described (paññāpeyya). The Buddha answers no, for each faculty in turn: no such eye, no such mind exists by which they could be described. This is not a claim that the fully quenched Buddhas of the past have ceased to exist or continue to exist in some other form — the discourse does not put the question that way — but a refusal of the terms in which the question assumes a describable faculty must persist. This resonates with this project's own earlier encounter, in Diṭṭhisaṃyutta (SN 24.9-18), with the traditional ten undetermined questions, several of which ask whether the Tathāgata exists, does not exist, both, or neither after death: both discourses treat ordinary descriptive categories as unable to reach what has gone beyond proliferation (papañca), though this discourse frames the problem through the six sense faculties specifically rather than as one of the ten questions directly. Beyond the discourse itself, this file's own closing lines carry a colophon reading “Gilānavaggo tatiyo” — “Gilānavagga is the third” — confirming, as this project's own established pattern (SN 22.52, SN 35.52, SN 35.62, SN 35.73) predicts, that this vagga is the third vagga of Saḷāyatanasaṃyutta's own second paṇṇāsaka, following Avijjāvagga (first, SN 35.53-62) and Migajālavagga (second, SN 35.63-73). The closing uddāna, left untranslated in Sujato's own English exactly as at SN 35.62 and SN 35.73, groups this vagga's own ten discourses explicitly by count — two on the sick, three more with Rādha, two more told on ignorance, then a mendicant, the world, and Phagguna — matching this reading guide's own discourse-by-discourse count precisely.

Reading guide

A question about describing what has gone beyond description

Phagguna asks whether any of the six sense faculties exists by which the fully quenched Buddhas of the past could be described. The Buddha answers no for each in turn — not asserting that they have ceased or continue to exist in some other form, but refusing the terms in which the question assumes a describable faculty must persist.

Cut off proliferation, cut off the course, finished off the cycle

The three-part description of a fully quenched Buddha — papañca (proliferation) cut off, vaṭuma (the course) cut off, vaṭṭa (the cycle) finished — names what has ended without asserting what, if anything, remains to be described.

An echo of the ten undetermined questions

This project's own earlier encounter with the ten undetermined questions (SN 24.9-18, in Diṭṭhisaṃyutta) included whether the Tathāgata exists, does not exist, both, or neither after death. This discourse does not pose the question in that exact form, but shares its underlying refusal: ordinary descriptive categories cannot reach what has gone beyond proliferation.

A colophon confirming the second fifty's own third vagga

This file's own closing colophon, “Gilānavaggo tatiyo” (“Gilānavagga is the third”), confirms — following the same pattern already established at SN 22.52, SN 35.52, SN 35.62, and SN 35.73 — that this vagga is the third vagga of Saḷāyatanasaṃyutta's own second paṇṇāsaka, after Avijjāvagga and Migajālavagga opened it in turn.

Ten discourses, grouped explicitly by count

This file's own closing uddāna groups this vagga's own ten discourses by number — two on the sick, three more with Rādha, two more told on ignorance, then a mendicant, the world, and Phagguna — a more explicit grouping than most vagga-uddānas this project has met, matching this reading guide's own count exactly. Left untranslated in Sujato's own English, this reading guide describes rather than quotes its content.

Key terms

paññāpeyya — could be described, made known — what Phagguna asks whether any faculty could achieve, of the fully quenched Buddhas of the past
papañca — proliferation — what a fully quenched Buddha of the past has cut off, part of the three-fold closing description
vaṭṭa — the cycle — what a fully quenched Buddha of the past has finished off, completing the three-fold description
paṇṇāsaka — a fifty — this vagga's own colophon confirms it is the third vagga of Saḷāyatanasaṃyutta's own second one
uddāna — a mnemonic summary verse — this file's own closing verse, grouping all ten discourses of this vagga explicitly by count

The text

The complete discourse: Phagguna's question run across all six sense faculties, and the Buddha's answer denying each in turn. Translation: Bhikkhu Sujato (CC0, SuttaCentral).

§1And then Venerable Phagguna went up to the Buddha … and said to him:

§2“Sir, suppose someone were to describe the Buddhas of the past who have become fully quenched, cut off proliferation, cut off the course, finished off the cycle, and passed beyond all suffering. Does the eye exist by which they could be described? Does the ear … nose … tongue … body exist …? Does the mind exist by which they could be described?”

§3“Phagguna, suppose someone were to describe the Buddhas of the past who have become fully quenched, cut off proliferation, cut off the course, finished off the cycle, and passed beyond all suffering. The eye does not exist by which they could be described. The ear … nose … tongue … body does not exist … The mind does not exist by which they could be described.”
