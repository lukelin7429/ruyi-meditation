Saṃyutta Nikāya · Discourse 1.2

Liberation

Nimokkhasutta

Setting
Near Sāvatthī, in Jeta’s Grove, late at night; the same circumstances as SN 1.1
Speakers
The Buddha and an unnamed, radiant deity
Form
A three-part riddle-and-answer exchange in prose, closed with a compact verse naming three causes
Length
~1.5 minutes to read
Northern parallel
A rough counterpart may exist in the Chinese Saṃyukta-āgama (T99), though this reading guide does not assert a specific matching sutra number
Difficulty
★★★☆☆ — short, but the closing verse compresses three separate technical claims into three lines

Why this discourse

A deity asks the Buddha, twice over, whether he knows the liberation, emancipation, and seclusion of sentient beings — first whether he knows it at all, then how he knows it. The answer, given in verse, names three specific causes: the ending of relish for rebirth, the finishing of perception and consciousness, and the cessation and stilling of feelings. Three of the five aggregates — perception, consciousness, and feeling — already appear here, in the second discourse of the entire collection, well before any discourse devoted to the aggregates as a topic in their own right.

Reading guide

A question asked twice, then answered once

The deity's exchange follows a three-step pattern already used at SN 1.1: a first question asking simply whether the Buddha knows a thing, a second confirming that he does, and a third asking how — which the Buddha answers directly, in verse, without further prompting.

Three technical terms, closely related

Nimokkha, pamokkha, and viveka — liberation, emancipation, and seclusion — are named together as a set, without being distinguished from one another in this discourse. The verse answer addresses all three at once, as a single achievement rather than three separate ones.

Three causes, three aggregates

The verse names three specific causes: the ending of relish for continued existence (nandībhavaparikkhaya), the finishing of perception and consciousness (saññāviññāṇasaṅkhaya), and the cessation and stilling of feelings (vedanānaṁ nirodhā upasamā). Perception, consciousness, and feeling are three of the five aggregates (khandha) that later become this collection's own dedicated subject in its third book, the Khandhavagga.

A verse, not further prose explanation

Unlike a discourse that might unpack each of these three causes in prose, this one simply states them, compressed into four lines of verse, and ends. Nothing in the text explains what ‘finishing perception and consciousness’ or ‘stilling feelings’ concretely involves; the deity asks no further question, and none is offered unprompted.

A shape this book's opening chapter repeats

This same short question-and-answer shape, closed by a compact verse, recurs across the Naḷavagga, the first sub-chapter of the Devatāsaṃyutta this discourse belongs to — the same ‘deity approaches, asks, is answered’ frame already met at SN 1.1, now applied to a different subject.

Key terms

nimokkha, pamokkha, viveka — liberation, emancipation, and seclusion — three closely related terms named together as a single achievement in this discourse's question.
nandībhavaparikkhaya — “the ending of relish for rebirth” — the first of the verse's three named causes.
saññāviññāṇasaṅkhaya — “the finishing of perception and consciousness” — the second cause, naming two of the five aggregates directly.
vedanānaṁ nirodhā upasamā — “the cessation and stilling of feelings” — the third cause, naming a third aggregate.
khandha — “aggregate” — perception, consciousness, and feeling, three of the five aggregates, appear here well before this collection's own dedicated book on the subject.

The text

The discourse in full: a deity's question, asked twice, answered once in verse. Translation: Bhikkhu Sujato (CC0, SuttaCentral).

§1At Sāvatthī. Then, late at night, a glorious deity, lighting up the entire Jeta’s Grove, went up to the Buddha, bowed, stood to one side, and said to him,

§2“Good fellow, do you know the liberation, emancipation, and seclusion of sentient beings?”

§3“I do, good sir.”

§4“But how is it that you know the liberation, emancipation, and seclusion of sentient beings?”

§5“Due to the ending of relish for rebirth; due to the finishing of perception and consciousness; due to the cessation and stilling of feelings—that, respectable sir, is how I know the liberation, emancipation, and seclusion of sentient beings.”
