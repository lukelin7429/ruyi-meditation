Saṃyutta Nikāya · Discourse 3.18

Good Friends

Kalyāṇamittasutta

Setting
At Sāvatthī; the Buddha's own recalled exchange took place earlier among the Sakyans, at a town called Townsville
Speakers
King Pasenadi, the Buddha, and (within the Buddha's own recollection) Venerable Ānanda
Form
A private royal reflection, answered by the Buddha recounting an earlier conversation, closed with practical advice and verse
Length
~4 minutes to read
Northern parallel
A rough counterpart may exist in the Chinese Saṃyukta-āgama (T99), though this reading guide does not assert a specific matching sutra number
Difficulty
★★★★☆ — a nested narrative structure worth tracking carefully

Why this discourse

Pasenadi reflects privately that the Buddha's teaching, however well explained, only benefits someone with good friends — not someone surrounded by bad company. The Buddha confirms this, then does something unusual: rather than simply elaborating, he recalls a specific earlier conversation with Venerable Ānanda, who had once suggested good friendship is ‘half the spiritual life’. The Buddha had corrected him then, insisting it is the whole of the spiritual life, since good friends make the noble eightfold path possible — and, more radically, since relying on the Buddha himself as a good friend frees beings from rebirth, old age, and death entirely.

Reading guide

A memory embedded inside the current conversation

This discourse's structure is unusual for this saṃyutta: rather than answering Pasenadi directly, the Buddha recounts a specific past exchange with Ānanda, complete with location (a Sakyan town called Townsville) and Ānanda's exact words, embedding one dialogue inside another.

Half, corrected to whole

Ānanda's original claim — good friendship is half the spiritual life — is not a modest understatement by ordinary standards, yet the Buddha corrects it as too cautious: not half, but the entire spiritual life, doubling the claim's scope rather than softening it.

Two distinct arguments for the same conclusion

The Buddha gives two separate justifications for calling good friendship the whole spiritual life: first, that good friends make developing the noble eightfold path possible; second, and more directly, that relying on the Buddha himself as a good friend frees beings from rebirth, old age, death, and the full range of suffering — the second argument identifying the Buddha's own role with the very category being discussed.

From a general teaching back to the king's own life

Having delivered this recalled teaching, the Buddha returns to addressing Pasenadi directly, advising him to train in having good friends and then to rely on a single further thing — diligence in skillful qualities — connecting this discourse back to SN 3.17's answer immediately before it.

Diligence spreading outward from the king himself

The advice's final movement extends beyond Pasenadi's own conduct: if the king lives diligently, his harem, vassals, troops, and people will observe this and choose to live diligently too — ethical example functioning as a form of governance, protecting not only the king but his entire household and treasury.

Key terms

kalyāṇamitta — “good friend” — the quality this discourse is named for and whose scope the Buddha expands.
upaḍḍhaṁ brahmacariyaṁ — “half the spiritual life” — Ānanda's original claim, corrected within the Buddha's recollection.
sakalameva brahmacariyaṁ — “the whole of the spiritual life” — the Buddha's correction, doubling rather than softening the claim.
ariya aṭṭhaṅgika magga — “the noble eightfold path” — what a mendicant with good friends can be expected to develop and cultivate.
appamādena sampādehi — “live supported by diligence” — the Buddha's practical advice to Pasenadi, connecting this discourse to SN 3.17's teaching.

The text

The discourse in full, with several structurally repeated passages elided as blank in the source. Translation: Bhikkhu Sujato (CC0, SuttaCentral).

§1At Sāvatthī. Seated to one side, King Pasenadi said to the Buddha, “Just now, sir, as I was in private retreat this thought came to mind. ‘The teaching is well explained by the Buddha. But it’s for someone with good friends, companions, and associates, not for someone with bad friends, companions, and associates.’”

§2“That’s so true, great king! That’s so true!” said the Buddha. And he repeated the king’s statement, adding:

§3“Great king, this one time I was staying in the land of the Sakyans where they have a town named Townsville. Then the mendicant Ānanda came to me, bowed, sat down to one side, and said: ‘Sir, good friends, companions, and associates are half the spiritual life.’

§4When he had spoken, I said to him: ‘Not so, Ānanda! Not so, Ānanda! Good friends, companions, and associates are the whole of the spiritual life. A mendicant with good friends, companions, and associates can expect to develop and cultivate the noble eightfold path.

§5And how does a mendicant with good friends develop and cultivate the noble eightfold path? It’s when a mendicant develops right view, right purpose, right speech, right action, right livelihood, right effort, right mindfulness, and right immersion, which rely on seclusion, fading away, and cessation, and ripen as letting go. That’s how a mendicant with good friends develops and cultivates the noble eightfold path. And here’s another way to understand how good friends are the whole of the spiritual life.

§6For, by relying on me as a good friend, sentient beings who are liable to rebirth, old age, and death, to sorrow, lamentation, pain, sadness, and distress are freed from all these things. This is another way to understand how good friends are the whole of the spiritual life.’

§7So, great king, you should train like this: ‘I will have good friends, companions, and associates.’ That’s how you should train. When you have good friends, companions, and associates, you should live supported by one thing: diligence in skillful qualities.

§8When you’re diligent, supported by diligence, your ladies of the harem, aristocrat vassals, troops, and people of town and country will think: ‘The king lives diligently, supported by diligence. We’d better live diligently, supported by diligence!’

§9When you’re diligent, supported by diligence, then not only you yourself, but your ladies of the harem, and your treasury and storehouses will be guarded and protected.” That is what the Buddha said. …

§10“For one who desires high wealth again and again, the astute praise diligence in making merit. Being diligent, an astute person secures both benefits: the benefit in this life, and in lives to come. Attentive, comprehending the meaning, they are said to be astute.”
