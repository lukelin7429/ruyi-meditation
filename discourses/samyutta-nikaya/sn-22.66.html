Saṃyutta Nikāya · Discourse 22.66

Impermanence

Aniccasutta — Arahantavagga

Setting
Sāvatthī
Speakers
An unnamed mendicant requests a teaching in brief, then explains it back to the Buddha
Form
The same narrative template as SN 22.63-65, now built around a simpler "give up desire for X" formula rather than the Māra framing
Length
~2 minutes to read
Northern parallel
A rough counterpart may exist in the Chinese Saṃyukta-āgama (T99), though this reading guide does not assert a specific matching sutra number
Difficulty
★☆☆☆☆ — opens a new cluster of four discourses within the same overall narrative template

Why this discourse

This discourse keeps the vagga's fixed narrative shell but drops the Māra imagery used in SN 22.63-65 for a simpler, more direct instruction: “give up desire for whatever's impermanent.” This opens a new cluster of four consecutive discourses (SN 22.66-69) that will each substitute a different characterization of the aggregates — impermanence here, suffering, not-self, and “not belonging to self” in turn — into this same simplified formula, echoing the three marks already explored at length in Aniccavagga but now delivered through this vagga's request-and-confirm narrative structure.

Reading guide

A simpler formula opening a second cluster

Where SN 22.63-65 built their formula around Māra's dominion, this discourse strips the instruction down to its plainest form: give up desire for whatever's impermanent. No adversarial figure is named; the instruction addresses the aggregates' nature directly.

An echo of Aniccavagga's own opening move

This formula's content directly recalls SN 22.12's opening claim in Aniccavagga — the aggregates are impermanent — but delivered here through the narrative frame of a mendicant's request and confirmed explanation, rather than as a direct teaching addressed to the assembly. The same doctrinal content is given a second, differently structured presentation.

The mendicant's own reasoning, stated as a personal resolution

In his confirmed explanation, the mendicant states each aggregate's impermanence and his own resulting resolution in the first person: “form is impermanent; I should give up desire for it.” This first-person framing, rather than a third-person description of what a learned noble disciple does, gives this cluster's discourses a more personally applied character than SN 22.12's original third-person statement.

Opening a quartet that will run through impermanence, suffering, not-self, and one further term

This discourse begins a set of four (SN 22.66-69) that will substitute impermanence, then suffering, then not-self, then “not belonging to self” — a related but distinct fourth term not identical to not-self itself — each fitted into the identical “give up desire for X” formula.

Key terms

anicca — “impermanent” — this discourse's title term, opening a new cluster of four within the vagga's matched narrative set.
chandaṁ pajahatha — “give up desire” — the simplified formula replacing SN 22.63-65's Māra framing.
pajahitabbo me chando — “I should give up desire for it” — the mendicant's first-person resolution, stated for each aggregate in turn.
saṅkhittena — “in brief” — the mendicant's request, unchanged from SN 22.63-65.
arahataṁ ahosi — “became one of the perfected” — the identical outcome closing this discourse, as in SN 22.63-65.

The text

The discourse in full. Elided repetitions (feeling, perception, and choices, each following the same formula spelled out in full for form and consciousness) are given exactly as bilara-data preserves them. Translation: Bhikkhu Sujato (CC0, SuttaCentral).

§1Then a mendicant went up to the Buddha … and asked him, “Sir, may the Buddha please teach me Dhamma in brief. When I’ve heard it, I’ll live alone, withdrawn, diligent, keen, and resolute.” “Mendicant, give up desire for whatever’s impermanent.” “Understood, Blessed One! Understood, Holy One!”

§2“But how do you see the detailed meaning of my brief statement?” “Sir, form is impermanent; I should give up desire for it.

§3Consciousness is impermanent; I should give up desire for it. That’s how I understand the detailed meaning of the Buddha’s brief statement.”

§4“Good, good, mendicant! It’s good that you understand the detailed meaning of what I’ve said in brief like this. Form is impermanent; you should give up desire for it.

§5Consciousness is impermanent; you should give up desire for it. This is how to understand the detailed meaning of what I said in brief.” … And that mendicant became one of the perfected.
