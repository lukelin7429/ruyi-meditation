Saṃyutta Nikāya · Discourse 22.82

A Full Moon Night

Puṇṇamasutta — Khajjanīyavagga

Setting
Sāvatthī, the stilt longhouse of Migāra's mother, Eastern Monastery, on the full moon sabbath
Speakers
An unnamed mendicant asks the Buddha ten questions in sequence, approving each answer before asking the next
Form
A systematic ten-question dialogue, closing the vagga with an encyclopedic synthesis, followed by a second mendicant's philosophical objection
Length
~7 minutes to read
Northern parallel
A rough counterpart may exist in the Chinese Saṃyukta-āgama (T99), though this reading guide does not assert a specific matching sutra number
Difficulty
★★★★★ — closes the vagga with the most systematically comprehensive dialogue in the entire book

Why this discourse

This discourse closes Khajjanīyavagga with a genuinely unique structure: on the full moon sabbath night, with the Buddha seated in the open surrounded by the assembled Saṅgha, a single mendicant asks ten questions in careful sequence, approving each answer with “good, sir” before asking the next. The questions build systematically through nearly every major technical teaching this book has developed — the aggregates' definition, their root, grasping's relationship to them, their scope, their causes, identity view's arising and non-arising, gratification/drawback/escape, and I-making/mine-making — before a second mendicant's silent doubt raises one of the canon's most famous philosophical challenges to the teaching of not-self.

Reading guide

Ten questions, each building on the confirmed answer before it

Unlike any other discourse in this vagga, this one is structured as a genuine extended dialogue: are these the five grasping aggregates? What is their root (desire)? Is grasping identical to the aggregates or something separate (neither — the desire and greed for them is the grasping)? Can there be different kinds of desire and greed for them? What is the scope of the term “aggregates”? What is the cause of each aggregate being found? How does identity view arise, and how does it not arise? What is each aggregate's gratification, drawback, and escape? And how does one know and see so there is no I-making or mine-making? Each answer draws on material already established separately elsewhere in this book — SN 22.56's causes, SN 22.44's sakkāya, SN 22.26's triad, SN 22.71's combined formula — gathered here into a single systematic sequence.

A precise, non-identical answer to "what is grasping"

The dialogue's third question deserves particular attention: is grasping (upādāna) the exact same thing as the five grasping aggregates, or something else entirely? The Buddha's answer refuses both options: “neither — rather, the desire and greed for them is the grasping there.” Grasping is neither identical with the aggregates nor separate from them; it is a specific relation (desire and greed) that a mind can have toward them.

A summary verse naming its own ten questions

The discourse itself supplies a rare translated summary verse at its close, naming the ten topics in sequence: “two on the aggregates…on the term, and on the cause; two questions on substantial reality; gratification, and that with consciousness — these are the ten questions the mendicant came to ask.” This kind of self-naming summary, translated rather than left in untranslated Pali, is rare in this book.

A second monk's doubt, and a stern redirection rather than an argument

After the ten questions close, a different mendicant silently wonders: if the aggregates are not-self, how can deeds done by not-self affect a self? This is one of the canon's classic philosophical challenges to the not-self teaching — a puzzle about moral responsibility without a fixed self to bear it. The Buddha's response is notably not a philosophical counter-argument: he calls the questioner “some futile person…their mind dominated by craving,” reminds the assembly they have already been trained in the empirical method of questioning itself, and simply runs the standard three-times argument once more — redirecting to the method rather than engaging the objection on its own abstract terms.

Key terms

puṇṇama — “full moon” — the sabbath occasion (uposatha) on which this discourse's extended dialogue takes place.
chandarāgo tattha upādānaṁ — “the desire and greed for them is the grasping there” — the Buddha's precise answer to whether grasping is identical with or separate from the aggregates.
uddāna — the discourse's closing summary verse, unusually translated here rather than left in untranslated Pali, naming all ten questions in sequence.
moghapurisa — “futile person” — the Buddha's sharp description of the second mendicant who raises the not-self/moral-responsibility objection.
kevalaṁ paripuṇṇaṁ pañhavyākaraṇaṁ sikkhāpitattha — “you have been educated by me in questioning with regards to all these things” — the Buddha's redirection to method rather than direct philosophical engagement with the objection.

The text

The discourse in full, including its closing summary verse. Elided repetitions across the dialogue's several questions (feeling, perception, and choices, spelled out in full for form and consciousness where the source does so) are given exactly as bilara-data preserves them. Translation: Bhikkhu Sujato (CC0, SuttaCentral).

§1“Sir, are these the five grasping aggregates, that is: form, feeling, perception, choices, and consciousness?”

§2“But sir, what is the root of these five grasping aggregates?” “These five grasping aggregates are rooted in desire.” …

§3“But sir, is that grasping the exact same thing as the five grasping aggregates? Or is grasping one thing and the five grasping aggregates another?” “Neither. Rather, the desire and greed for them is the grasping there.”

§4“But sir, can there be different kinds of desire and greed for the five grasping aggregates?” “There can,” said the Buddha. “It’s when someone thinks: ‘In the future, may I be of such form, such feeling, such perception, such choices, or such consciousness!’ That’s how there can be different kinds of desire and greed for the five grasping aggregates.”

§5“Sir, what is the scope of the term ‘aggregates’ as applied to the aggregates?”

§6“What is the cause, sir, what is the reason why the aggregate of form is found? What is the cause, what is the reason why the aggregate of feeling … perception … choices … consciousness is found?” “The four principal states are the reason why the aggregate of form is found.

§7“Sir, how does substantialist view come about?” “It’s when an unlearned ordinary person has not seen the noble ones, and is neither skilled nor trained in the teaching of the noble ones. They’ve not seen true persons, and are neither skilled nor trained in the teaching of the true persons. They regard form as self, self as having form, form in self, or self in form. They regard feeling … perception … choices … consciousness as self, self as having consciousness, consciousness in self, or self in consciousness. That’s how substantialist view comes about.”

§8“But sir, how does substantialist view not come about?” “It’s when a learned noble disciple has seen the noble ones, and is skilled and trained in the teaching of the noble ones. They’ve seen true persons, and are skilled and trained in the teaching of the true persons. They don’t regard form as self, self as having form, form in self, or self in form. They don’t regard feeling … perception … choices … consciousness as self, self as having consciousness, consciousness in self, or self in consciousness. That’s how substantialist view does not come about.”

§9“Sir, what’s the gratification, the drawback, and the escape when it comes to form, feeling, perception, choices, and consciousness?” “The pleasure and happiness that arise from form: this is its gratification. That form is impermanent, suffering, and perishable: this is its drawback. Removing and giving up desire and greed for form: this is its escape.

§10“Sir, how does one know and see so that there’s no I-making, mine-making, or underlying tendency to conceit for this conscious body and externally for all signs?” “One truly sees any kind of form at all—past, future, or present; internal or external; solid or subtle; inferior or superior; far or near: <em>all</em> form—with right understanding: ‘This is not mine, I am not this, this is not my self.’

§11“Two on the aggregates; exactly the same; and can there be; on the term; and on the cause; two questions on substantial reality; gratification; and that with consciousness: these are the ten questions the mendicant came to ask.”

§12“So it seems, worthy sir, that form, feeling, perception, choices, and consciousness are not-self. Then how will the deeds done by not-self affect the self?”

§13“It’s possible that some futile person here—unknowing and ignorant, their mind dominated by craving—thinks they can overstep the teacher’s instructions. They think: ‘So it seems, worthy sir, that form, feeling, perception, choices, and consciousness are not-self. Then how will the deeds done by not-self affect the self?’ Now, mendicants, you have been educated by me in questioning with regards to all these things in all such cases.
