Saṃyutta Nikāya · Discourse 9.5

With Ānanda

Ānandasutta

Setting
A forest grove in the Kosalan lands
Speakers
An unnamed forest deity and Venerable Ānanda
Form
An observed imbalance, corrected with a single compact verse
Length
~1.5 minutes to read
Northern parallel
A rough counterpart may exist in the Chinese Saṃyukta-āgama (T99), though this reading guide does not assert a specific matching sutra number
Difficulty
★★☆☆☆ — brief, with a pointed closing question

Why this discourse

Ānanda appears here not battling an obvious temptation like SN 9.1's monk, but caught in something subtler: spending too much time persuading lay people, an imbalance toward social engagement rather than the seclusion he'd originally sought out. The forest deity's single verse doesn't scold him for wrongdoing exactly; it simply reminds him of his own original intention — the jungle, the root of a tree, extinguishment laid in his heart — and asks a pointed, almost dismissive question about the busywork currently occupying him: what use is this hullabaloo to you?

Reading guide

A subtler imbalance than obvious temptation

Unlike SN 9.1's lust or SN 9.2's sleep, Ānanda's issue here isn't a conventional lapse at all; spending time persuading lay people sounds like productive, even admirable activity, making the deity's intervention a correction of excess rather than of obvious wrongdoing.

A reminder of original intention, not a new instruction

The verse doesn't introduce anything Ānanda hasn't already committed to; it simply restates what he ‘used to’ do — seek the jungle, lay extinguishment in his heart — holding up his own earlier commitment as the standard he's currently drifting from.

The vocative "Gotama" recurring once more

This is at least the third time in this collection the term ‘Gotama’ is used as direct address, here for Ānanda; since Ānanda was genuinely of the Buddha's own Sākiya-Gotama clan, this instance continues to support reading it as a family-name vocative rather than confusion with the Buddha himself.

A dismissive closing image for well-intentioned busyness

‘Hullabaloo’ (biḷibiḷikā) is a strikingly unflattering word for what might otherwise be described as valuable lay outreach; the deity's word choice itself carries the judgment, without needing further explanation of what's wrong with it.

Urgency as the only recorded response

As in SN 9.1 and SN 9.3, the discourse ends simply with Ānanda struck by a sense of urgency — no defense, no argument, no further verse in reply, closing the exchange as quickly as it began.

Key terms

ativelaṁ gihisaññattibahulo viharati — “spending too much time persuading lay people” — Ānanda's specific imbalance, an excess rather than an obvious fault.
rukkhamūlagahanaṁ pasakkiya — “you've left for the jungle, the root of a tree” — recalling Ānanda's own original commitment.
nibbānaṁ hadayasmiṁ opiya — “having laid extinguishment in your heart” — naming what that original commitment was actually aimed at.
jhāya gotama mā pamādo — “meditate, don't be negligent!” — the direct imperative at the verse's center.
kiṁ te biḷibiḷikā karissati — “what use is this hullabaloo to you?” — the pointed, dismissive closing question.

The text

The discourse in full. Translation: Bhikkhu Sujato (CC0, SuttaCentral).

§1At one time Venerable Ānanda was staying in the land of the Kosalans in a certain forest grove. Now at that time Ānanda was spending too much time persuading lay people. Then the deity haunting that forest had sympathy for Ānanda, wanting what’s best for him. So they approached him wanting to stir him up, and recited these verses:

§2“You’ve left for the jungle, the root of a tree, having laid extinguishment in your heart. Meditate, Gotama, don’t be negligent! What use is this hullabaloo to you?”

§3Impelled by that deity, Venerable Ānanda was struck with a sense of urgency.
