Saṃyutta Nikāya · Discourse 7.3

With Bhāradvāja the Fiend

Asurindakasutta

Setting
Near Rājagaha, in the Bamboo Grove
Speakers
The brahmin Asurindaka Bhāradvāja ("Bhāradvāja the Fiend") and the Buddha
Form
Abuse met with silence, a taunt, then a single verse redefining victory
Length
~2.5 minutes to read
Northern parallel
A rough counterpart may exist in the Chinese Saṃyukta-āgama (T99), though this reading guide does not assert a specific matching sutra number
Difficulty
★★★☆☆ — brief, with a sharp turn on the meaning of winning

Why this discourse

This is the third member of the Bhāradvāja clan in as many discourses to arrive at the Buddha already furious over a kinsman's ordination, and the pattern shifts here in a telling way: rather than answering the abuse with an immediate teaching, as in SN 7.1 and SN 7.2, the Buddha simply says nothing. It's the brahmin's own taunt at this silence — declaring himself the winner — that draws out the verse, and that verse turns the taunt inside out: the one who thinks he's won by speaking harshly is the fool, while the real victory belongs entirely to the one who endures without responding in kind.

Reading guide

Silence itself as the first response

Where SN 7.1 and SN 7.2 both show the Buddha meeting hostility with an immediate teaching, here he offers nothing at all at first — the silence is itself the response, and it's precisely this non-reaction that provokes the brahmin's next move.

A taunt claiming victory over an opponent who never engaged

‘You're beaten, ascetic, you're beaten!’ only makes sense as a claim if silence is read as defeat — the brahmin's triumph depends entirely on mistaking non-engagement for concession, a misunderstanding the verse then corrects directly.

Victory relocated from speech to endurance

The verse doesn't deny that a kind of ‘winning’ happened; it relocates where the real victory lies — not in the harsh words spoken, but in the endurance of the one who received them without retaliating.

A verse shared, with small variation, across this vagga

Several lines here — on not returning anger, on winning ‘a battle hard to win’, on being mistaken for a fool by those inexpert in the teaching — repeat almost verbatim from SN 7.2's closing verses, suggesting a shared stock of teaching-verses reused across different brahmins' confrontations rather than freshly composed for each one.

A third conversion, told with maximal compression

The discourse's closing lines are almost entirely elided in the source, repeating the same conversion and arahantship formula already given in full in SN 7.1 and 7.2 — this reading guide follows that same compression rather than reconstructing the elided text.

Key terms

bhagavā tuṇhī ahosi — “the Buddha kept silent” — the initial response to abuse, itself meaningful rather than absent.
jitosi, samaṇa, jitosi — “you're beaten, ascetic, you're beaten!” — the brahmin's taunt, claiming victory over the Buddha's silence.
jayaṁ ve maññati bālo — “the fool thinks he's won” — the verse's opening line, naming the taunt's error directly.
yā titikkhā vijānato — “that very endurance, for one who understands” — the verse's relocation of true victory.
saṅgāmaṁ jeti dujjayaṁ — “you win a battle hard to win” — a line shared with SN 7.2's verse, reused here almost verbatim.

The text

The discourse in full. The closing conversion formula, identical to SN 7.1 and 7.2, is elided in the source and is not reconstructed here. Translation: Bhikkhu Sujato (CC0, SuttaCentral).

§1At one time the Buddha was staying near Rājagaha, in the Bamboo Grove, the squirrels’ feeding ground. The brahmin Bhāradvāja the Fiend heard a rumor to the effect that a brahmin of the Bhāradvāja clan had gone forth from the lay life to homelessness in the presence of the ascetic Gotama. Angry and displeased he went to the Buddha and abused and insulted him with rude, harsh words. But when he said this, the Buddha kept silent. Then Bhāradvāja the Fiend said to the Buddha, “You’re beaten, ascetic, you’re beaten!”

§2“‘Ha! I won!’ thinks the fool, when speaking with harsh words. The victory for one who understands is that very endurance.

§3When you get angry at an angry person you just make things worse for yourself. When you don’t get angry at an angry person you win a battle hard to win.

§4When you know that the other is angry, you act for the good of both yourself and the other if you’re mindful and stay calm.

§5People inexpert in Dhamma consider one who heals both oneself and the other to be a fool.”

§6When he had spoken, Bhāradvāja the Fiend said to the Buddha, “Excellent, worthy Gotama! …” … And Venerable Bhāradvāja became one of the perfected.
