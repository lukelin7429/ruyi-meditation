Saṃyutta Nikāya · Discourse 22.60

With Mahāli

Mahālisutta — Upayavagga

Setting

Why this discourse

This discourse steps outside the vagga's usual Sāvatthī setting to record a direct philosophical confrontation. Mahāli, a Licchavi nobleman (distinct from any of this book's mendicant speakers), reports to the Buddha a claim made by Pūraṇa Kassapa — one of the six rival teachers whose fatalistic and amoral doctrines are catalogued elsewhere in the canon, notably in DN 2 — that there is no cause or reason for either the corruption or the purification of sentient beings; both simply happen without cause. The Buddha's response is a flat rejection followed by a precise, positive account: there is a cause, and it lies in the double-sided nature of the aggregates themselves, which are neither purely pleasurable nor purely painful.

Reading guide

A rival teacher's doctrine, reported and rejected

Mahāli reports Pūraṇa Kassapa's claim exactly: sentient beings are corrupted without cause or reason, and purified without cause or reason. This is a form of fatalism that denies any causal explanation for moral or spiritual development in either direction. The Buddha's answer is direct and unequivocal: there is a cause and reason for both corruption and purification.

Corruption explained by the aggregates' pleasurable side

The Buddha's account of corruption turns on a conditional: if form were exclusively painful, soaked and steeped in pain with no admixture of pleasure, sentient beings would not be aroused by it. But because form is genuinely pleasurable — steeped in pleasure and not only pain — sentient beings are aroused by it, become caught up in it, and so become corrupted. The cause of corruption is not an arbitrary flaw but the aggregates' real, undeniable pleasurable quality.

Purification explained by the aggregates' painful side

The mirror-image account of purification uses the identical conditional structure with the opposite quality: if form were perfectly pleasurable, steeped in pleasure with no admixture of pain, sentient beings would never grow disillusioned with it. But because form is genuinely painful — steeped in pain and not only pleasure — sentient beings do grow disillusioned, desire fades, and they are purified. Corruption and purification are thus explained by two real, opposite facets of the very same aggregates, not by chance.

A rare confrontation with a named rival system

Unlike most discourses in this book, which address mendicants or individual householders without reference to competing philosophical schools, this discourse names a specific rival teacher and doctrine directly. This gives the discourse's teaching a pointed, argumentative edge — not simply presenting the Buddhist position in isolation, but showing exactly what alternative position it is meant to refute, and why the aggregates' own mixed character supplies the refutation.

Key terms

ahetu appaccayā — “without cause or reason” — Pūraṇa Kassapa's claim about both corruption and purification, directly rejected by the Buddha.
saṅkiliṭṭhā — “corrupted” — the condition the Buddha traces to sentient beings being aroused by the aggregates' genuine pleasurable quality.
vodāyanti — “purified” — the condition traced to disillusionment arising from the aggregates' genuine painful quality.
sukhanissitañceva na dukkhanissitañ ca — “steeped in pleasure and not steeped in pain” — part of the conditional structure explaining why sentient beings are aroused rather than repelled.
Pūraṇa Kassapa — one of the six rival teachers whose fatalistic and amoral doctrines are catalogued elsewhere in the canon, notably in DN 2, here directly named and refuted.

The text

The discourse in full. Elided repetitions in each section (feeling, perception, and choices, each following the same pattern spelled out in full for form and consciousness) are given exactly as bilara-data preserves them. Translation: Bhikkhu Sujato (CC0, SuttaCentral).

§1“Sir, Pūraṇa Kassapa says this: ‘There is no cause or reason for the corruption of sentient beings. Sentient beings are corrupted without cause or reason. There’s no cause or reason for the purification of sentient beings. Sentient beings are purified without cause or reason.’ What does the Buddha say about this?”

§2“Mahāli, there is a cause and reason for the corruption of sentient beings. Sentient beings are corrupted with cause and reason. There is a cause and reason for the purification of sentient beings. Sentient beings are purified with cause and reason.”

§3“Mahāli, if form were exclusively painful—soaked and steeped in pain and not steeped in pleasure—sentient beings wouldn’t be aroused by it. But because form is pleasurable—soaked and steeped in pleasure and not steeped in pain—sentient beings are aroused by it. Since they are aroused by it, they’re caught up in it, and so they become corrupted. This is a cause and reason for the corruption of sentient beings. This is how sentient beings are corrupted with cause and reason.

§4consciousness were exclusively painful—soaked and steeped in pain and not steeped in pleasure—sentient beings wouldn’t be aroused by it. But because consciousness is pleasurable—soaked and steeped in pleasure and not steeped in pain—sentient beings are aroused by it. Since they are aroused by it, they’re caught up in it, and so they become corrupted. This is a cause and reason for the corruption of sentient beings. This is how sentient beings are corrupted with cause and reason.”

§5“Mahāli, if form was perfectly pleasurable—soaked and steeped in pleasure and not steeped in pain—sentient beings wouldn’t grow disillusioned with it. But because form is painful—soaked and steeped in pain and not steeped in pleasure—sentient beings do grow disillusioned with it. Being disillusioned, desire fades away. When desire fades away they are purified. This is a cause and reason for the purification of sentient beings. This is how sentient beings are purified with cause and reason.

§6consciousness was perfectly pleasurable—soaked and steeped in pleasure and not steeped in pain—sentient beings wouldn’t grow disillusioned with it. But because consciousness is painful—soaked and steeped in pain and not steeped in pleasure—sentient beings do grow disillusioned with it. Being disillusioned, desire fades away. When desire fades away they are purified. This is a cause and reason for the purification of sentient beings. This is how sentient beings are purified with cause and reason.”
