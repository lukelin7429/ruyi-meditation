Aṅguttara Nikāya · Discourse 4.190

Sabbath

Uposathasutta — Brāhmaṇavagga — the nineteenth chapter of the Fours

Setting
Sāvatthī, in the stilt longhouse of Migāra’s mother, in the Eastern Monastery, on the sabbath day
Speakers
The Buddha, addressing the Saṅgha of mendicants
Form
An address to a silent assembly, then four attainments named and each defined in full
Length
~4 minutes to read
Northern parallel
The fourfold survey of jhāna, the immeasurables, the formless attainments, and the four truths as a complete map of practice is widespread across the early collections; this reading guide does not assert a specific matching sutra number
Difficulty
★★★☆☆ — the chapter’s longest discourse, and its most complete synthesis

Why this discourse

On the sabbath, the Buddha looks around a silent assembly and calls it free of chaff, pure, consolidated in the core — worth traveling many leagues to see. Then he names four things some of the mendicants present have attained: to the gods, to divinity, to the imperturbable, to nobility. Each is defined in full, and each is a system this nipāta has already met elsewhere — the four absorptions, the four immeasurables, the four formless attainments, and the four noble truths, gathered here into one closing survey.

Reading guide

The teaching in one sentence

A single silent assembly holds mendicants who have reached four entirely different kinds of attainment, and this discourse names all four in full.

A silent assembly, praised without qualification

The scene is exact: the sabbath day, the Buddha seated, surrounded by the Saṅgha, and looking around at mendicants who are so very silent. What follows is a run of praise with no hedging in it — free of chaff, pure, and consolidated in the core… rarely seen in the world… worth traveling many leagues to see, even if you have to carry your own provisions in a knapsack.

The formula naming this assembly worthy of offerings, hospitality, religious donation, and veneration, and calling it the supreme field of merit for the world, is the identical formula AN 4.181 used to close its description of a single mendicant with four qualities. What was said there of one warrior-like mendicant is said here of an entire silent room.

Four attainments, named before they are defined

Devappatta, attained to the gods. Brahmappatta, attained to divinity. Āneñjappatta, attained to the imperturbable. Ariyappatta, attained to nobility. The discourse states plainly that mendicants of each of the four kinds are present in this very assembly, then takes each in turn and defines it completely.

Attained to the gods: the four absorptions

The first attainment is the four jhāna, given in the collection’s standard formula from the first, born of seclusion, through the fourth, beyond pleasure and pain. This is the identical formula met at AN 4.123 and again as the pleasant path of AN 4.163, and the name ‘attained to the gods’ recalls directly the mechanism AN 4.123 described: this practice, enjoyed and not lost, leads to rebirth in the company of gods.

Attained to divinity: the four immeasurables

The second attainment is love, compassion, rejoicing, and equanimity, each spread in every direction, abundant, expansive, limitless, free of enmity and ill will — the same formula met at AN 4.125 and AN 4.126. Brahmappatta, attained to divinity, uses the same root as AN 4.63’s praise of a compassionate household life as ‘living with Divinity’, now applied to mendicants rather than householders.

Attained to the imperturbable: the four formless states

The third attainment runs through all four formless dimensions in sequence: infinite space, infinite consciousness, nothingness, and neither perception nor non-perception, each reached by going totally beyond the one before it.

This is where the chapter’s threads converge most visibly. The dimension of nothingness, third in this sequence, is the very attainment AN 4.185 isolated as the practice behind the fourth truth of the brahmins — I don’t belong to anyone, nothing belongs to me. The dimension of neither perception nor non-perception, fourth and last here, is the attainment AN 4.171 examined at length in the previous chapter, where the same state, identically reached, produced either a returner or a non-returner depending on whether the lower fetters had been given up.

Āneñja, imperturbable, unshakeable, names what distinguishes these four from the absorptions before them: a stability the formless attainments have that the form-based jhāna do not, in the tradition’s standard account.

Attained to nobility: the four noble truths

The fourth and last attainment is truly understanding suffering, its origin, its cessation, and the practice leading to its cessation — the identical four truths that defined marksmanship at AN 4.181 and penetrating wisdom at AN 4.186, both earlier in this same chapter.

Ariyappatta, attained to nobility, closes the discourse’s survey by returning to the one system this chapter has invoked more often than any other, now named directly rather than through a simile or a question.

A chapter closing on its own convergence

It is worth stating directly what this discourse accomplishes structurally, because it is unusual even within a collection built from repeated formulas. Four complete systems of practice, each already given full treatment somewhere in the Fours — the absorptions, the immeasurables, the formless attainments, the four truths — are gathered here and presented as four different things a single assembly, observed on a single afternoon, actually contains.

The chapter that opened with a warrior simile mapping four military qualities onto a single mendicant closes with the same worthiness formula mapping four complete practice traditions onto a single roomful of them. Nothing forces these two discourses to be read as bookends, and this reading guide does not claim the compilers intended the echo. What can be said is that AN 4.181 and AN 4.190 are the only two discourses in this chapter to use the full five-part worthiness formula, and they are, respectively, its first and its last.

Key terms

apalāpa — “chaff” — what the assembly is said to be free of; the husk separated from grain, applied to a community without spiritual substance.
devappatta — “attained to the gods” — via the four absorptions, recalling the rebirth mechanism described at AN 4.123.
brahmappatta — “attained to divinity” — via the four immeasurables, sharing its root with AN 4.63’s praise of a compassionate household.
āneñjappatta — “attained to the imperturbable” — via the four formless dimensions, including the very states examined at AN 4.171 and AN 4.185.
ariyappatta — “attained to nobility” — via the four noble truths, the system this chapter has returned to more often than any other.

The text

The discourse in full: the silent assembly praised, and four attainments named and defined completely. Translation: Bhikkhu Sujato (CC0, SuttaCentral).

A chaff-free assembly

§1At one time the Buddha was staying near Sāvatthī in the stilt longhouse of Migāra’s mother in the Eastern Monastery. Now, at that time it was the sabbath, and the Buddha was sitting surrounded by the Saṅgha of monks. Then the Buddha looked around the Saṅgha of mendicants, who were so very silent. He addressed them: “This assembly has no chaff, mendicants, it is free of chaff, pure, and consolidated in the core. Such is this Saṅgha of mendicants, such is this assembly! An assembly such as this is rarely seen in the world. An assembly such as this is worthy of offerings dedicated to the gods, worthy of hospitality, worthy of a religious donation, worthy of greeting with cupped palms, and is the supreme field of merit for the world. For an assembly such as this, giving little becomes much, while giving much becomes even more. An assembly such as this is worth traveling many leagues to see, even if you have to carry your own provisions in a knapsack.

Four attainments

§2There are mendicants staying in this Saṅgha who have attained to the gods. There are mendicants staying in this Saṅgha who have attained to divinity. There are mendicants staying in this Saṅgha who have attained to the imperturbable. There are mendicants staying in this Saṅgha who have attained to nobility.

Attained to the gods

§3And how has a mendicant attained to the gods? It’s when a mendicant, quite secluded from sensual pleasures, secluded from unskillful qualities, enters and remains in the first absorption … As the placing of the mind and keeping it connected are stilled, they enter and remain in the second absorption … third absorption … fourth absorption … That’s how a mendicant has attained to the gods.

Attained to divinity

§4And how has a mendicant attained to divinity? Firstly, a mendicant meditates spreading a heart full of love to one direction, and to the second, and to the third, and to the fourth. In the same way above, below, across, everywhere, all around, they spread a heart full of love to the whole world—abundant, expansive, limitless, free of enmity and ill will. Furthermore, a mendicant meditates spreading a heart full of compassion … rejoicing … equanimity to one direction, and to the second, and to the third, and to the fourth. In the same way above, below, across, everywhere, all around, they spread a heart full of equanimity to the whole world—abundant, expansive, limitless, free of enmity and ill will. That’s how a mendicant has attained to divinity.

Attained to the imperturbable

§5And how has a mendicant attained to the imperturbable? It’s when a mendicant—going totally beyond perceptions of form, with the disappearance of perceptions of impingement, not focusing on perceptions of diversity—aware that ‘space is infinite’, enters and remains in the dimension of infinite space. Going totally beyond the dimension of infinite space, aware that ‘consciousness is infinite’, he enters and remains in the dimension of infinite consciousness. Going totally beyond the dimension of infinite consciousness, aware that ‘there is nothing at all’, he enters and remains in the dimension of nothingness. Going totally beyond the dimension of nothingness, he enters and remains in the dimension of neither perception nor non-perception. That’s how a mendicant has attained to the imperturbable.

Attained to nobility

§6And how has a mendicant attained to nobility? It’s when they truly understand: ‘This is suffering’ … ‘This is the origin of suffering’ … ‘This is the cessation of suffering’ … ‘This is the practice that leads to the cessation of suffering’. That’s how a mendicant has attained to nobility.”
