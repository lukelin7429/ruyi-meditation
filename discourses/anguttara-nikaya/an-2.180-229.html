Aṅguttara Nikāya · Discourses 2.180–229

Anger, Abbreviated

Kodhapeyyāla — the sixteenth chapter of the Twos

Setting
None stated; the chapter continues from AN 2.1, whose setting at Sāvatthī is understood to hold
Speakers
The Buddha alone, addressing the mendicants
Form
Fifty discourses transmitted as ten abbreviated entries — five pairs of faults, and their opposites, run through five predicates each
Length
~3 minutes to read
Northern parallel
The list of paired faults is standard in the Chinese Āgamas and in the Abhidharma treatment of the secondary defilements; this reading guide does not assert a specific matching sutra number
Difficulty
★★☆☆☆ — mechanical by design, and worth reading once for the list it holds

Why this discourse

The Twos end in four chapters of pure combination, and this is the first. Five pairs of faults — anger and acrimony, disdain and contempt, jealousy and stinginess, deceit and deviousness, and the absence of conscience and prudence — are run through five predicates, then the same five pairs in their positive form through five more. Fifty discourses in ten written entries. Nothing here is argued. What the chapter is good for is the list itself, which is one of the canon’s most precise inventories of the ways people are unpleasant to one another.

Reading guide

The teaching in one sentence

Five pairs of faults do five bad things, and their opposites do five good ones.

How to read a peyyāla chapter

Peyyāla is the canonical convention for abbreviating repeated material: the varying terms are listed once with ellipses, and the reciter expands. This chapter is fifty discourses written as ten, and Sujato’s translation preserves the compression rather than unrolling it silently, which is why the paragraph numbers below cover five discourses each.

Expanding it takes about five times as long and is exactly as tedious as it sounds. The point is not to read fifty near-identical sentences but to see the shape: a fixed list of five items, multiplied by a fixed list of predicates. The Aṅguttara does this whenever it has a list it considers settled, and the compression is a sign that the material was regarded as reference rather than as teaching.

The five pairs

Kodha and upanāha: anger and acrimony. The flare, and the grudge that keeps it after the flare has gone. Naming them separately is the chapter’s best single move, because they behave differently — anger is loud and brief, acrimony is quiet and durable, and a person who has dealt with the first often has not touched the second.

Makkha and paḷāsa: disdain and contempt. Sujato’s rendering; the first is more usually “denigration” and involves refusing to acknowledge another’s worth or another’s help, and the second is a kind of rivalry that insists on parity with those above one. Both are about placing oneself relative to others.

Issā and macchariya: jealousy and stinginess. Again a pair that runs together and is worth separating — jealousy resents what someone else has, stinginess refuses to release what one has oneself. They are the same reflex pointed outward and inward.

Māyā and sāṭheyya: deceit and deviousness. Concealing what one is, and operating crookedly. And finally the absence of hiri and ottappa, the two bright things AN 2.7–9 said protect the world, which appear here as the fifth fault rather than as a separate category.

The five predicates

Each pair is run through: simply being named as two things (AN 2.180–189); living in suffering or happily (190–199); leading or not leading to the decline of a trainee (200–209); being placed in hell or heaven as if delivered there (210–219); and being reborn in a bad or good destination after death (220–229).

The last two look like duplicates and are not quite. “Placed as if delivered there” is the image of AN 1.43–44 in the Ones: something carried and set down, the destination following from the state of mind with no interval and no tribunal. The final pair is the ordinary rebirth formula. The chapter states the immediate consequence and the eventual one separately.

What the list is good for

As a teaching this chapter says nothing new. As an inventory it is unusually useful, because the pairs are not synonyms and the distinctions inside them are the kind a person cannot easily make for themselves.

Anyone can notice that they are angry. Noticing that the anger has gone and the acrimony has not is harder, and requires having been given the second word. The same for jealousy against stinginess, and for deceit against deviousness. The value of a list like this is that it hands over vocabulary, and vocabulary is what makes a state findable.

These five pairs also became a fixed set in the later tradition. The Abhidharma classifies most of them among the secondary defilements — 隨煩惱 — and the Abhidharmakośa, on this site, discusses several of them by name in exactly this grouping.

Key terms

peyyāla — the canonical convention for abbreviating repeated material, and the reason this chapter’s fifty discourses occupy ten written entries.
kodha / upanāha — “anger” and “acrimony” — the flare, and the grudge that outlasts it. Named separately because they behave differently.
issā / macchariya — “jealousy” and “stinginess” — resenting what another has, and refusing to release what one has. The same reflex pointed outward and inward.
māyā / sāṭheyya — “deceit” and “deviousness” — concealing what one is, and operating crookedly.
upakkilesa — “secondary defilement” — the later category, 隨煩惱 in Chinese, under which the Abhidharma classifies most of these five pairs.

The text

All fifty discourses of the chapter, in the form the canon transmits them: each paragraph below carries a range of five because the text abbreviates the repeated material. Translation: Bhikkhu Sujato (CC0, SuttaCentral).

Named as two things (AN 2.180–189)

§180–184“There are these two things. What two? Anger and acrimony … disdain and contempt … jealousy and stinginess … deceit and deviousness … lack of conscience and prudence. These are the two things.”

§185–189“There are these two things. What two? Freedom from anger and acrimony … freedom from disdain and contempt … freedom from jealousy and stinginess … freedom from deceit and deviousness … conscience and prudence. These are the two things.”

Living in suffering, living happily (AN 2.190–199)

§190–194“Anyone who has two things lives in suffering. What two? Anger and acrimony … disdain and contempt … jealousy and stinginess … deceit and deviousness … lack of conscience and prudence. Anyone who has these two things lives in suffering.”

§195–199“Anyone who has these two things lives happily. What two? Freedom from anger and acrimony … freedom from disdain and contempt … freedom from jealousy and stinginess … freedom from deceit and deviousness … conscience and prudence. Anyone who has these two things lives happily.”

The decline of a trainee (AN 2.200–209)

§200–204“These two things lead to the decline of a mendicant trainee. What two? Anger and acrimony … disdain and contempt … jealousy and stinginess … deceit and deviousness … lack of conscience and prudence. These two things lead to the decline of a mendicant trainee.”

§205–209“These two things don’t lead to the decline of a mendicant trainee. What two? Freedom from anger and acrimony … freedom from disdain and contempt … freedom from jealousy and stinginess … freedom from deceit and deviousness … conscience and prudence. These two things don’t lead to the decline of a mendicant trainee.”

Placed as if delivered there (AN 2.210–219)

§210–214“Anyone who has two things is placed in hell as if delivered there. What two? Anger and acrimony … disdain and contempt … jealousy and stinginess … deceit and deviousness … lack of conscience and prudence. Anyone who has these two things is placed in hell as if delivered there.”

§215–219“Anyone who has two things is placed in heaven as if delivered there. What two? Freedom from anger and acrimony … freedom from disdain and contempt … freedom from jealousy and stinginess … freedom from deceit and deviousness … conscience and prudence. Anyone who has these two things is placed in heaven as if delivered there.”

And after death (AN 2.220–229)

§220–224“When they have two things, some people, when their body breaks up, after death, are reborn in a place of loss, a bad place, the underworld, hell. What two? Anger and acrimony … disdain and contempt … jealousy and stinginess … deceit and deviousness … lack of conscience and prudence. When they have two things, some people, when their body breaks up, after death, are reborn in a place of loss, a bad place, the underworld, hell.”

§225–229“When they have two things, some people—when their body breaks up, after death—are reborn in a good place, a heavenly realm. What two? Freedom from anger and acrimony … freedom from disdain and contempt … freedom from jealousy and stinginess … freedom from deceit and deviousness … conscience and prudence. When they have these two things, some people—when their body breaks up, after death—are reborn in a good place, a heavenly realm.”
