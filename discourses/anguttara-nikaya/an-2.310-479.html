Aṅguttara Nikāya · Discourses 2.310–479

Greed, Abbreviated

Rāgapeyyāla — the nineteenth and last chapter of the Twos

Setting
None stated; the chapter continues from AN 2.1, whose setting at Sāvatthī is understood to hold
Speakers
The Buddha alone, addressing the mendicants; the closing lines are the compilers’ colophon
Form
A hundred and seventy discourses transmitted as two entries — seventeen defilements times ten operations, with one answer throughout
Length
~2 minutes to read
Northern parallel
The pairing of serenity and discernment as the remedy for the defilements is standard across the Chinese Āgamas; this reading guide does not assert a specific matching sutra number
Difficulty
★★☆☆☆ — the arithmetic is the content, and the answer never changes

Why this discourse

The Book of the Twos ends with a multiplication table. Seventeen defilements — greed, hate, delusion, and fourteen more — are each run through ten operations: insight into it, complete understanding of it, finishing it, giving it up, ending it, its vanishing, its fading away, its cessation, giving it away, letting it go. A hundred and seventy discourses. And for every one of them the answer is the same two things, named back at AN 2.31: serenity and discernment. It is the least varied chapter in the nipāta and the most emphatic, because the emphasis is carried entirely by the invariance.

Reading guide

The teaching in one sentence

Whatever the defilement and whatever you want done to it, the answer is serenity and discernment.

The multiplication

Seventeen items: greed, hate, delusion, anger, acrimony, disdain, contempt, jealousy, stinginess, deceit, deviousness, obstinacy, aggression, conceit, arrogance, vanity, and negligence. The first three are the root defilements; the next ten are the five pairs from the Kodhapeyyāla; the last four are a cluster around self-regard, closing on negligence, which the Ones treated at length.

Ten operations: abhiññā insight into, pariññā complete understanding of, parikkhaya finishing, pahāna giving up, khaya ending, vaya vanishing, virāga fading away, nirodha cessation, cāga giving away, paṭinissagga letting go.

Those ten are not stages, and reading them as a sequence is a mistake worth avoiding. They are ten ways of naming what happens to a defilement when it goes, ordered roughly from the cognitive to the volitional — knowing it, understanding it, its exhaustion, your relinquishing of it. The Aṅguttara covers the ground by enumeration rather than by definition, as it does everywhere.

Why the answer never changes

A hundred and seventy discourses with one answer is a strong claim, and it is worth stating what it amounts to. It is not that serenity and discernment are useful for these things. It is that there is no defilement on the list, and no operation on the list, that requires anything else.

That closes off a possibility a practitioner might reasonably entertain: that different faults need different remedies. The Ones spent a chapter doing exactly that for the five hindrances — the feature of ugliness for sensual desire, love for ill will, energy for dullness. This chapter says that at the level of what actually removes a defilement rather than what counters it in the moment, the toolkit has two items.

AN 2.31 gave the mechanism, and it explains the invariance. Serenity develops the mind, and a developed mind gives up greed; discernment develops wisdom, and developed wisdom gives up ignorance. Every item on the seventeen is a form of one or the other, or of both, so the two remedies exhaust the field. A one-line chapter would have said so. This one says so a hundred and seventy times.

The colophon

The chapter, and the Book of the Twos, closes with the standard formula: that is what the Buddha said. Satisfied, the mendicants approved what the Buddha said. The Book of the Twos is finished.

Unlike the Ones, the Twos do not append a disputed count of their own discourses. Modern editions number them to four hundred and seventy-nine, which is itself a figure produced by choosing how to expand the four abbreviated chapters that close the book — the same counting question the Ones’ colophon raised. Nineteen chapters, and the last four of them hold more than half the total.

That proportion is worth carrying forward into the Threes. The Aṅguttara grows by multiplication as much as by composition, and a reader who expects each nipāta to consist of discourses like AN 2.15 or AN 2.33 will be surprised by how much of it looks like this page instead.

What to do with it

Practically, nothing — and that is a legitimate answer for one chapter in nineteen. The seventeen items are worth reading once as a list, because several of them (thambha, obstinacy; sārambha, aggression; mada, vanity) do not appear elsewhere in the first two nipātas and are useful words to have.

And the invariance is worth noticing once. A reader who has come this far through the Twos has met a great many pairs. The book ends by naming one pair and applying it to everything, which is as close to a summary as a collection organized by number can give.

Key terms

rāga — “greed” — the first of the seventeen and the chapter’s name; with hate and delusion, one of the three root defilements.
thambha / sārambha — “obstinacy” and “aggression” — two of the four items clustered around self-regard near the end of the list, and not found elsewhere in the first two nipātas.
pariññā — “complete understanding” — the second of the ten operations, and a technical term for the full comprehension that ends attachment to a thing.
paṭinissagga — “letting go, relinquishment” — the last of the ten operations, and the most volitional of them.
samatha / vipassanā — serenity and discernment — the single answer given a hundred and seventy times, whose mechanism AN 2.31 supplied.

The text

All hundred and seventy discourses of the chapter, in the form the canon transmits them: greed written out once with its ten operations, and then the remaining sixteen defilements in a single abbreviated entry. The closing lines are the compilers’ colophon to the Book of the Twos. Translation: Bhikkhu Sujato (CC0, SuttaCentral).

Greed, ten ways (AN 2.310–319)

§310–319“For insight into greed, two things should be developed. What two? Serenity and discernment. For insight into greed, these two things should be developed.” “For the complete understanding … finishing … giving up … ending … vanishing … fading away … cessation … giving away … letting go of greed, two things should be developed.”

And sixteen more defilements (AN 2.320–479)

§320–479“Of hate … delusion … anger … acrimony … disdain … contempt … jealousy … stinginess … deceit … deviousness … obstinacy … aggression … conceit … arrogance … vanity … negligence … for insight … complete understanding … finishing … giving up … ending … vanishing … fading away … cessation … giving away … letting go … two things should be developed. What two? Serenity and discernment. For the letting go of negligence, these two things should be developed.” That is what the Buddha said. Satisfied, the mendicants approved what the Buddha said. The Book of the Twos is finished.
