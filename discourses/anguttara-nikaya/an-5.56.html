Aṅguttara Nikāya · Discourse 5.56

Mentor

Upajjhāyasutta — Nīvaraṇavagga — the sixth chapter of the Fives

Setting
None stated; the discourse continues from AN 5.1, whose setting at Sāvatthī in Jeta’s Grove is understood to hold
Speakers
An unnamed mendicant, his mentor, and the Buddha
Form
A symptom reported twice, a diagnosis and prescription, a report of complete success, and the identical instruction repeated
Length
~4 minutes to read
Northern parallel
Mentor-mediated consultation with the Buddha over a specific meditative difficulty recurs across the Chinese Āgamas; this reading guide does not assert a specific matching sutra number
Difficulty
★★★☆☆ — after this chapter's hardest discourse, one of its most encouraging

Why this discourse

After AN 5.55's account of practice failing badly, this discourse shows practice working completely, start to finish. A mendicant describes a cluster of symptoms with unusual precision — a drugged feeling in the body, disorientation, teachings that won't come to mind, drowsiness, dissatisfaction, doubt — and rather than treating any of this as a personal failing beyond remedy, the Buddha names a specific, correctable cause and gives a specific fix, which the mendicant then applies alone and which works completely.

Reading guide

The teaching in one sentence

A mendicant reporting a drugged, disoriented, drowsy, dissatisfied, and doubtful state is told this comes from unguarded senses, overeating, insufficient dedication to wakefulness, and neglecting the awakening factors, and instructed to reverse all five — which he does, alone, becoming an arahant.

A precise cluster of symptoms, taken seriously

Madhurakajāto kāyo, a body that feels drugged, is an unusually vivid and specific complaint, and it is not treated as vague or dismissible. The mendicant names five distinct experiences — bodily heaviness, spatial disorientation, teachings that fail to surface in memory, dullness and drowsiness filling the mind, dissatisfaction with the spiritual life, and doubt about the teachings — and the Buddha's response engages with all five as a genuinely diagnosable condition, not a character flaw.

Diagnosis, in four correctable causes

The Buddha names exactly what produces this state: unguarded sense doors, immoderate eating, insufficient dedication to wakefulness, and neglecting to discern skillful qualities and develop the awakening factors in the evening and toward dawn. Every one of these four is a matter of practice, not disposition — the discourse implies the mendicant's difficulty is not a fixed trait but a set of specific, correctable habits.

The turnaround, reported in the mendicant's own words

The discourse gives the mendicant's transformed report the same precision as his original complaint, symptom by symptom, negated one at a time: no longer drugged, no longer disoriented, teachings now come to mind, no more drowsiness, satisfaction in the spiritual life, no doubt. This is not summarized as ‘he improved’; the discourse restates the entire original list in reverse, giving the recovery the same specificity as the original complaint.

From individual instruction to general teaching

The Buddha's second delivery of this same instruction, after the mendicant's success, shifts address from singular bhikkhu to plural bhikkhave — the identical fix, now offered to the whole assembly rather than the one mendicant who needed it. A remedy that worked for one specific, precisely described case becomes, without modification, general instruction.

Key terms

madhurakajāto kāyo — “a body that feels drugged” — the mendicant's opening complaint, an unusually vivid physical description.
indriyesu aguttadvāra — “unguarded sense doors” — the first of four diagnosed causes of the mendicant's condition.
bhojane amattaññu — “not knowing moderation in eating” — the second cause, overeating as a specific, correctable habit.
bodhipakkhiyā dhammā — “qualities on the side of awakening” — the framework the fourth cause says was being neglected in the evening and toward dawn.
saddhivihārika — “co-resident, protégé” — the term for the mendicant in relation to the mentor who brings him to the Buddha both times in this discourse.

The text

The discourse in full: the mendicant's complaint, the Buddha's diagnosis and instruction, the mendicant's success, and the same instruction restated for the whole assembly. Translation: Bhikkhu Sujato (CC0, SuttaCentral).

The complaint

§1Then a mendicant went up to his own mentor, and said, “Now, sir, my body feels like it’s drugged. I’m disorientated, the teachings don’t spring to mind, and dullness and drowsiness fill my mind. I lead the spiritual life dissatisfied, and have doubts about the teachings.”

§2Then that mendicant took his protégé to the Buddha, bowed, sat down to one side, and said to him, “Sir, this mendicant says this: ‘Now, sir, my body feels like it’s drugged. I’m disorientated, the teachings don’t spring to mind, and dullness and drowsiness fill my mind. I lead the spiritual life dissatisfied, and have doubts about the teachings.’”

The diagnosis and instruction

§3“That’s how it is, mendicant, when your sense doors are unguarded, you eat too much, you’re not dedicated to wakefulness, you’re unable to discern skillful qualities, and you don’t pursue the development of the qualities on the side of awakening in the evening and toward dawn. Your body feels like it’s drugged. You’re disorientated, the teachings don’t spring to mind, and dullness and drowsiness fill your mind. You lead the spiritual life dissatisfied, and have doubts about the teachings.

§4So you should train like this: ‘I will guard my sense doors, eat in moderation, be dedicated to wakefulness, discern skillful qualities, and pursue the development of the qualities on the side of awakening in the evening and toward dawn.’ That’s how you should train.”

Practicing alone

§5When that mendicant had been given this advice by the Buddha, he got up from his seat, bowed, and respectfully circled the Buddha, keeping him on his right, before leaving.

§6Then that mendicant, living alone, withdrawn, diligent, keen, and resolute, soon realized the supreme culmination of the spiritual path in this very life. He lived having achieved with his own insight the goal for which gentlemen rightly go forth from the lay life to homelessness.

§7He understood: “Rebirth is ended; the spiritual journey has been completed; what had to be done has been done; there is nothing further for this place.” And that mendicant became one of the perfected.

The turnaround, reported

§8When that mendicant had attained perfection, he went up to his own mentor, and said, “Now, sir, my body doesn’t feel like it’s drugged. I’m not disorientated, the teachings spring to mind, and dullness and drowsiness don’t fill my mind. I lead the spiritual life satisfied, and have no doubts about the teachings.”

§9Then that mendicant took his protégé to the Buddha, bowed, sat down to one side, and said to him, “Sir, this mendicant says this: ‘Now, sir, my body doesn’t feel like it’s drugged. I’m not disorientated, the teachings spring to mind, and dullness and drowsiness don’t fill my mind. I lead the spiritual life satisfied, and have no doubts about the teachings.’”

The same instruction, for everyone

§10“That’s how it is, mendicant, when your sense doors are guarded, you’re moderate in eating, you’re dedicated to wakefulness, you’re able to discern skillful qualities, and you pursue the development of the qualities on the side of awakening in the evening and toward dawn. Your body doesn’t feel like it’s drugged. You’re not disorientated, the teachings spring to mind, and dullness and drowsiness don’t fill your mind. You lead the spiritual life satisfied, and have no doubts about the teachings.

§11So you should train like this: ‘We will guard our sense doors, eat in moderation, be dedicated to wakefulness, discern skillful qualities, and pursue the development of the qualities on the side of awakening in the evening and toward dawn.’ That’s how you should train.”
