Aṅguttara Nikāya · Discourses 2.280–309

Why the Rules Exist

Vinayapeyyāla — the eighteenth chapter of the Twos

Why this discourse

This chapter is the canon’s own answer to the question every reader of the Vinaya eventually asks: what are all these rules for? Ten paired reasons are given, and then applied, one procedure at a time, to everything the monastic law contains — the code, its recitation, ordination, banishment, probation, the seven methods of settling a dispute. The list is stable across every application, which is the point: the same ten reasons justify the smallest rule and the gravest procedure, and none of the ten is “because it is the rule.”

Reading guide

The teaching in one sentence

Every rule and every procedure in the monastic law exists for the same ten paired reasons, all of which are about people rather than about order.

The ten reasons

For the well-being and comfort of the Saṅgha. For keeping difficult individuals in check and for the comfort of good-hearted mendicants. For restraining defilements that affect this life and protecting against defilements that affect lives to come — and then the same structure for threats, for faults, for hazards, and for unskillful qualities, each in its this-life and future-life form. Out of sympathy for laypeople and for breaking up factions of mendicants with corrupt wishes. For inspiring confidence in those without it and increasing confidence in those who have it. And for the continuation of the true teaching and the support of the training.

Read the list as a whole and its character is striking. Four of the ten pairs are about protecting individuals from consequences, in this life and the next. Two are about the comfort and well-being of the community. One is explicitly about laypeople — not monastic order but sympathy for the people outside the order. One is about what outsiders will think, in both directions. And only the last is about the institution’s own survival.

What is absent is as telling. There is no appeal to the rule’s antiquity, to the authority of whoever laid it down, to purity as such, or to obedience as a value. The justification offered is entirely consequential, and it is offered for every rule without exception.

“Out of sympathy for laypeople”

The eighth reason deserves separate attention because it is easy to read past and is unusual. Gihīnaṁ anukampāya: out of sympathy or compassion for householders.

Monastic rules are usually explained, when they are explained at all, in terms of the monastic’s own progress. This reason says that some of them exist because of the effect they have on people who are not monastics at all — the donors who feed the community, the villages a wandering Saṅgha passes through, the families whose sons and daughters have gone forth. A community supported entirely by other people’s labor has obligations to them, and this reason names those obligations as part of what the rules are for.

Paired with it in the same clause is breaking up factions of mendicants with corrupt wishes, which is the internal counterpart: protecting the community from being captured from inside. External obligation and internal capture, filed as one reason.

What the reasons are applied to

AN 2.280 gives the ten reasons for the training rules. AN 2.281–309 then applies the same ten to a long list of procedures: the monastic code and its recitation, the suspension of that recitation, the invitation to admonish and its setting aside, condemnation, placing under dependence, banishment, reconciliation, debarment, probation, being sent back to the beginning, penance, reinstatement, restoration, removal, ordination, the three grades of formal act, laying down new rules and amending existing ones, and the seven methods for settling a disciplinary matter.

That last group is worth naming even in a guide that cannot explain each: settling face-to-face, through recollection, because of past insanity, according to what has been admitted, by majority decision, by further penalty, and by covering over as if with grass — the last being a procedure for closing a dispute that has become too tangled to adjudicate, by mutual agreement to let it lie.

The point of the enumeration is that the reasons do not change. Ordination and banishment, the gravest procedures at either end, are justified by the same ten as the smallest amendment to a minor rule. There is no separate class of rules held to a different standard.

Why this chapter matters to a lay reader

Most people reading this page will never be subject to a single one of these procedures, and the temptation is to treat the chapter as internal monastic housekeeping. It is worth resisting for two reasons.

The first is that the list is a model of how to justify a rule, and it transfers. Any institution can ask of its own regulations whether they serve the ten kinds of reason named here, and most institutions will find that some of their rules serve none of them and persist for the reasons this list conspicuously omits: because they are old, because someone senior wants them, or because changing them would be admitting something.

The second is that this chapter is the answer to a charge often made against monastic Buddhism, that its elaborate discipline is legalism for its own sake. The canon’s own account is that it is not, and says so at the head of the Vinaya in several traditions. Whether a given community lives up to that account is a separate question, and the account is the standard by which it can be asked.

Key terms

sikkhāpada — “training rule” — an individual precept of the monastic code, and what AN 2.280 gives the ten reasons for.
gihīnaṁ anukampāya — “out of sympathy for laypeople” — the eighth reason, and the one that grounds monastic discipline in obligations to people outside the community.
pātimokkha — “the monastic code” — the list of training rules recited fortnightly, and the first of the procedures the ten reasons are applied to.
adhikaraṇasamatha — “settling of a disciplinary matter” — the seven methods, ending with covering over as if with grass: closing a dispute too tangled to adjudicate by mutual agreement to let it lie.
saddhammaṭṭhitiyā — “for the continuation of the true teaching” — the tenth and last reason, and the only one about the institution’s own survival.

The text

All thirty discourses of the chapter, in the form the canon transmits them: the ten reasons written out once and then applied, in a single abbreviated entry, to every procedure of the monastic law. Translation: Bhikkhu Sujato (CC0, SuttaCentral).

The ten reasons (AN 2.280)

§280“For two reasons the Realized One laid down training rules for his disciples. What two? For the well-being and comfort of the Saṅgha … For keeping difficult individuals in check and for the comfort of good-hearted mendicants … For restraining defilements that affect this life and protecting against defilements that affect lives to come … For restraining threats to this life and protecting against threats to lives to come … For restraining faults that affect this life and protecting against faults that affect lives to come … For restraining hazards that affect this life and protecting against hazards that affect lives to come … For restraining unskillful qualities that affect this life and protecting against unskillful qualities that affect lives to come … Out of sympathy for laypeople and for breaking up factions of mendicants with corrupt wishes … For inspiring confidence in those without it, and increasing confidence in those who have it … For the continuation of the true teaching and the support of the training. These are the two reasons why the Realized One laid down training rules for his disciples.”

Applied to every procedure (AN 2.281–309)

§281–309“For two reasons the Realized One laid down for his disciples the monastic code … the recitation of the monastic code … the suspension of the recitation of the monastic code … the invitation to admonish … the setting aside of the invitation to admonish … the disciplinary act of condemnation … placing under dependence … banishment … reconciliation … debarment … probation … being sent back to the beginning … penance … reinstatement … restoration … removal … ordination … an act with a motion … an act with a motion and one announcement … an act with a motion and three announcements … laying down what was not previously laid down … amending what was laid down … the settling of a disciplinary matter by the following methods: face-to-face … through recollection … because of past insanity … acting according to what has been admitted … majority decision … further penalty… covering over as if with grass. What two? For the well-being and comfort of the Saṅgha … For keeping difficult individuals in check and for the comfort of good-hearted mendicants … For restraining defilements that affect this life and protecting against defilements that affect lives to come … For restraining threats to this life and protecting against threats to lives to come … For restraining faults that affect this life and protecting against faults that affect lives to come … For restraining hazards that affect this life and protecting against hazards that affect lives to come … For restraining unskillful qualities that affect this life and protecting against unskillful qualities that affect lives to come … Out of sympathy for laypeople and for breaking up factions of mendicants with corrupt wishes … For inspiring confidence in those without it, and increasing confidence in those who have it … For the continuation of the true teaching and the support of the training. These are the two reasons why the Realized One laid down the settlement of a disciplinary matter by covering over with grass for his disciples.”
