Aṅguttara Nikāya · Discourse 4.177

With Rāhula

Rāhulasutta — Cetanāvagga — the eighteenth chapter of the Fours

Setting
None stated; the Cetanāvagga gives no location for this discourse, and it is addressed to the mendicants directly
Speakers
The Buddha, addressing Venerable Rāhula
Form
Four elements, each given the identical three-part treatment, and a closing summary
Length
~2 minutes to read
Northern parallel
The element meditation given to Rāhula is best known in its longer, six-element form at MN 62; this reading guide does not assert a specific matching sutra number for this shorter version
Difficulty
★★★☆☆ — simple in form, and worth reading beside its more famous longer relative

Why this discourse

The Buddha teaches his own son a way of seeing the body that dissolves the line between inside and outside. The earth inside you and the earth outside you are just the earth element — the same stuff, met twice. Water, fire, and air get the identical treatment. See any of them as not mine, not I, not myself, and disillusionment follows, and the mind detaches. This is the short version of a teaching the tradition also preserves at much greater length.

Reading guide

The teaching in one sentence

What is called the body’s earth, water, fire, and air is not different in kind from earth, water, fire, and air anywhere else, and seeing that dissolves ownership of it.

Interior and exterior, the same element

Each of the four gets the identical opening move: the interior earth element and the exterior earth element are just the earth element. Not similar to it, not made of the same kind of stuff — simply it, without remainder.

That collapsing of the distinction is the whole engine of the teaching. A person ordinarily treats what is inside the skin as a different category of thing from what is outside it, invested with a possessiveness the outside version never gets. The discourse removes the category difference before asking anything else of the listener.

The formula applied four times

Having collapsed the distinction, each element gets the same instruction: it should be truly seen with right understanding as this is not mine, I am not this, this is not my self — three denials, of possession, of identity, and of self, in that order.

And each ends with the same result: seeing truly with right understanding, one grows disillusioned with that element and detaches the mind from it. The verb for disillusionment, nibbindati, is not aversion; it is closer to a loss of interest that follows from having seen something correctly rather than from having argued oneself out of caring about it.

Why four elements here, and six elsewhere

The much longer discourse the tradition gives Rāhula on the same subject, remembered as the greater discourse of advice to Rāhula, adds space and consciousness as a fifth and sixth element, and gives each of the six far more extensive treatment, including the famous simile of enduring insult like the earth enduring what is thrown on it.

This version has only four, and the reason is not doctrinal. It is the chapter this discourse belongs to. The Fours can hold exactly four elements and no more, and a reader who has followed the collection this far should recognize the pattern: a teaching too large for its number gets trimmed to fit, as the five faculties were cut to four at AN 4.151 and restored to five two chapters later.

Reading the two versions together is instructive precisely because of what the shorter one lacks. Space and consciousness do not divide as cleanly into ‘interior’ and ‘exterior’ as the four physical elements do, and their absence here may be as much a matter of what fits the simpler formula as of what fits the number four.

The closing line

The discourse ends by naming what a mendicant who sees all four elements this way is called: one who has cut off craving, cast off the fetters, and by rightly comprehending conceit has made an end of suffering.

Three achievements in one sentence, and the third is worth pausing on. Sammā mānābhisamayā, by rightly comprehending conceit, names the specific operation this meditation performs on māna, the measuring of oneself against others that AN 4.159 used as fuel for a mendicant’s own liberation and that the tradition elsewhere calls one of the last things to go.

Comparison of self to other requires there to be a self doing the comparing. A meditation that dissolves the line between what is inside and what is outside the body removes exactly the boundary conceit depends on, which is presumably why this particular closing formula, rather than a more general one, was attached to it.

Rāhula, and why this teaching

The discourse gives no narrative beyond the bare address: the Buddha speaks to Rāhula directly, without the visit-and-greeting frame several other discourses in this chapter use. Rāhula was the Buddha’s son before either went forth, and the tradition records a whole cluster of discourses given to him as he grew from a young novice into a mendicant in his own right.

This reading guide does not speculate about why the elements meditation in particular was chosen for him on this occasion; the text gives no reason beyond the address itself. What can be said is that the collection preserves several distinct teachings to the same person, at different lengths and evidently at different points, and this is one of the shortest of them.

Key terms

pathavīdhātu — “earth element” — solidity, whether met as the body or as the ground; first of the four treated identically here.
ajjhattikā / bāhirā — “interior” and “exterior” — the distinction the discourse collapses for each element in turn.
netaṁ mama, nesohamasmi, na meso attā — “this is not mine, I am not this, this is not my self” — the threefold denial of possession, identity, and self, applied to each element.
nibbindati — “grows disillusioned” — a loss of interest following correct seeing, not aversion argued into being.
sammā mānābhisamayā — “by rightly comprehending conceit” — the closing achievement, dependent on dissolving the interior/exterior boundary māna requires.

The text

The discourse in full: the four elements, each seen as neither self nor belonging to self. Translation: Bhikkhu Sujato (CC0, SuttaCentral).

The earth element

§1Then Venerable Rāhula went up to the Buddha, bowed, and sat down to one side. The Buddha said to him: “Rāhula, the interior earth element and the exterior earth element are just the earth element. This should be truly seen with right understanding like this: ‘This is not mine, I am not this, this is not my self.’ When you truly see with right understanding, you grow disillusioned with the earth element, detaching the mind from the earth element.

The water element

§2The interior water element and the exterior water element are just the water element. This should be truly seen with right understanding like this: ‘This is not mine, I am not this, this is not my self.’ When you truly see with right understanding, you grow disillusioned with the water element, detaching the mind from the water element.

The fire element

§3The interior fire element and the exterior fire element are just the fire element. This should be truly seen with right understanding like this: ‘This is not mine, I am not this, this is not my self.’ When you truly see with right understanding, you grow disillusioned with the fire element, detaching the mind from the fire element.

The air element

§4The interior air element and the exterior air element are just the air element. This should be truly seen with right understanding like this: ‘This is not mine, I am not this, this is not my self.’ When you truly see with right understanding, you grow disillusioned with the air element, detaching the mind from the air element.

Cutting off craving

§5When a mendicant sees these four elements as neither self nor belonging to self, they’re called a mendicant who has cut off craving, cast off the fetters, and by rightly comprehending conceit has made an end of suffering.”
