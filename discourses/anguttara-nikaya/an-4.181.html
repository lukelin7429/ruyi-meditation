Aṅguttara Nikāya · Discourse 4.181

A Warrior

Yodhājīvasutta — Brāhmaṇavagga — the nineteenth chapter of the Fours

Setting
None stated; the Brāhmaṇavagga gives no location for this discourse, and it is addressed to the mendicants directly
Speakers
The Buddha alone, addressing the mendicants
Form
A military simile stated, then each of its four terms mapped and defined
Length
~3 minutes to read
Northern parallel
Warrior similes for spiritual accomplishment are attested across the early collections; this reading guide does not assert a specific matching sutra number
Difficulty
★★☆☆☆ — a clear simile, mapped without remainder onto four things already familiar from this nipāta

Why this discourse

A warrior with four factors is fit to serve a king: skilled in the basics, a long-distance shooter, a marksman, one who shatters large objects. A mendicant with the matching four is the supreme field of merit for the world. The simile is not decorative — each of the four terms gets mapped onto something exact: the training rules, the five aggregates seen as not-self, the four noble truths, and the destruction of ignorance itself.

Reading guide

The teaching in one sentence

A warrior’s four qualifications are matched, term for term, to four qualities that make a mendicant worthy of the highest offerings.

The four military terms

Ṭhānakusala, skilled in the basics — the fundamentals of stance and handling before anything else. Dūrepātī, a long-distance shooter, one whose arrows reach far. Akkhaṇavedhī, a marksman, one who strikes the vital point precisely, without missing. And one who mahato kāyassa padāletā, shatters a large mass — breaks open something substantial that a lesser blow would not dent.

The four are not arbitrary military virtues chosen for effect. They form a natural sequence: grounding, reach, precision, and force sufficient to break something genuinely large. Each of the four mendicant-qualities that follows preserves exactly that logic.

Skilled in the basics: ethics

The mendicant version is given in the collection’s standard formula for ethical conduct: sīlavā, ethical, restrained by the monastic code, well-conducted, resorting for alms in suitable places, seeing danger in the slightest fault and keeping the rules undertaken.

This is the fundamental stance, and the simile is precise about its place: it is what a warrior does before anything else is possible, not a separate achievement standing beside the other three. Everything that follows is built on it.

A long-distance shooter: the five aggregates seen as not-self

This is the discourse’s largest section by far, because ‘long-distance’ is mapped onto range in the most literal sense available: seeing every possible instance of form, feeling, perception, choices, and consciousness — past, future, or present, internal or external, solid or subtle, inferior or superior, far or near, all of it — with the same three-part understanding, ‘this is not mine, I am not this, this is not my self’.

The reach of the arrow is the reach of the seeing. A shooter who can only hit what is close is not a long-distance shooter, and a practitioner who can see impermanence in the present moment but not across every temporal and qualitative range of the aggregates has not yet done what this term names. The formula’s exhaustive listing — past/future/present, internal/external, and the rest — is not padding; it is the discourse insisting the range actually be total.

A marksman: the four noble truths

Truly understanding this is suffering… this is the origin of suffering… this is the cessation of suffering… this is the practice that leads to the cessation of suffering is called marksmanship — hitting the vital point precisely.

Where the previous quality was about range, this one is about accuracy: four things, understood exactly, without missing any of the four or blurring one into another. The same four truths reappear as the definition of ‘penetrating wisdom’ in AN 4.186, later in this chapter, and this discourse’s marksman image gives that later definition a concrete picture to stand on.

Shattering large objects: the mass of ignorance

The fourth quality closes the sequence with force rather than precision: shattering the great mass of ignorance, mahantaṁ avijjākkhandhaṁ padāletā. Where the marksman hits a point, this quality breaks open something large.

The word khandha, mass or aggregate, is the same word used for the five aggregates the second quality saw through. Ignorance here is described with the same vocabulary of bulk and substance as the very thing being seen through — a reminder that what is being broken is not a small obstacle but something with real mass, requiring more than a well-aimed shot.

The discourse ends by restoring the full honorific formula it opened with: such a mendicant is worthy of offerings dedicated to the gods, worthy of hospitality, worthy of a religious donation, worthy of veneration with cupped palms, and the supreme field of merit for the world. The same formula closes this chapter’s final discourse, AN 4.190, applied there to an entire assembly rather than to one mendicant.

Key terms

ṭhānakusala — “skilled in the basics” — the fundamentals of stance; mapped onto ordinary ethical conduct, the ground everything else stands on.
dūrepātī — “long-distance shooter” — mapped onto seeing every instance of the five aggregates, across every temporal and qualitative range, as not-self.
akkhaṇavedhī — “marksman” — hitting the vital point without missing; mapped onto the four noble truths, understood exactly.
mahato kāyassa padāletā — “one who shatters a large mass” — mapped onto breaking open avijjākkhandha, the great mass of ignorance.
khandha — “mass”, aggregate — the same word for the five aggregates seen through by the second quality, applied here to the bulk of ignorance itself.

The text

The discourse in full: the warrior simile, and each of its four terms defined in turn. Translation: Bhikkhu Sujato (CC0, SuttaCentral).

A warrior’s four factors

§1“Mendicants, a warrior with four factors is worthy of a king, fit to serve a king, and is reckoned a factor of kingship. What four? He’s skilled in the basics, a long-distance shooter, a marksman, one who shatters large objects. A warrior with these four factors is worthy of a king, fit to serve a king, and is reckoned a factor of kingship. In the same way, a mendicant with four qualities is worthy of offerings dedicated to the gods, worthy of hospitality, worthy of a religious donation, worthy of veneration with cupped palms, and is the supreme field of merit for the world. What four? He’s skilled in the basics, a long-distance shooter, a marksman, one who shatters large objects.

Skilled in the basics

§2And how is a mendicant skilled in the basics? It’s when a mendicant is ethical, restrained in the monastic code, conducting themselves well and resorting for alms in suitable places. Seeing danger in the slightest fault, they keep the rules they’ve undertaken. That’s how a mendicant is skilled in the basics.

A long-distance shooter

§3And how is a mendicant a long-distance shooter? It’s when a mendicant truly sees any kind of form at all—past, future, or present; internal or external; solid or subtle; inferior or superior; far or near: <em>all</em> form—with right understanding: ‘This is not mine, I am not this, this is not my self.’ They truly see any kind of feeling … perception … choices … consciousness at all—past, future, or present; internal or external; solid or subtle; inferior or superior; far or near, <em>all</em> consciousness—with right understanding: ‘This is not mine, I am not this, this is not my self.’ That’s how a mendicant is a long-distance shooter.

A marksman

§4And how is a mendicant a marksman? It’s when they truly understand: ‘This is suffering’ … ‘This is the origin of suffering’ … ‘This is the cessation of suffering’ … ‘This is the practice that leads to the cessation of suffering’. That’s how a mendicant is a marksman.

One who shatters large objects

§5And how does a mendicant shatter large objects? It’s when a mendicant shatters the great mass of ignorance. That’s how a mendicant shatters large objects. A mendicant with these four qualities … is the supreme field of merit for the world.”
