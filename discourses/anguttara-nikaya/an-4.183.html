Aṅguttara Nikāya · Discourse 4.183

Vassakāra on What is Heard

Sutasutta — Brāhmaṇavagga — the nineteenth chapter of the Fours

Setting
Rājagaha, in the Bamboo Grove, the squirrels’ feeding ground
Speakers
Vassakāra, a chief minister of Magadha, and the Buddha
Form
A stated view, and a reply that rejects both it and its opposite
Length
~1 minute to read
Northern parallel
The criterion of skillful versus unskillful consequence for speech recurs across the early collections' treatments of right speech; this reading guide does not assert a specific matching sutra number
Difficulty
★★★☆☆ — a short exchange whose refusal of two tempting extremes is easy to read past

Why this discourse

A chief minister of Magadha states a simple doctrine: there is nothing wrong with saying ‘so I have seen’, ‘so I have heard’, ‘so I have thought’, ‘so I have known’ — reporting whatever passed through any of the four channels, without restriction. The Buddha does not agree, and does not simply disagree either. He declines both the position offered and its obvious opposite, and locates the actual criterion somewhere neither extreme had considered.

Reading guide

The teaching in one sentence

Whether to speak of something seen, heard, thought, or known is decided by its effect on skillful and unskillful qualities, not by which of the four channels it came through or by a blanket rule of transparency.

Vassakāra’s doctrine

Vassakāra names the chief minister of Magadha, and his stated view is a clean principle: reporting what one has seen, heard, thought, or known is never wrong, regardless of content, because the report is accurate to the experience. ‘So I have seen’, said of something actually seen, cannot itself be a fault.

This is a recognizable and appealing position — a kind of blanket honesty ethic, where truthful report of one’s own experience is always permissible because truthfulness is the only relevant standard being tested.

The two extremes the Buddha refuses

The Buddha’s answer is built as a pair of denials before it gives any positive content: I don’t say you should talk about everything you see, hear, think, and know. But I also don’t say you should talk about nothing you see, hear, think, and know.

That double refusal is doing real work. The first sentence rejects Vassakāra’s position directly. The second rejects a response a listener might reach for next — that if unrestricted reporting is wrong, the corrective must be discretion, reticence, or silence as a general policy. Neither extreme is endorsed, and the discourse does not pause to explain why before moving to what actually decides the matter.

The actual criterion

When talking about certain things you’ve seen, heard, thought, or known, unskillful qualities grow while skillful qualities decline. I say that you shouldn’t talk about those things. When talking about other things… unskillful qualities decline while skillful qualities grow. I say that you should talk about those things.

The test is entirely consequentialist, and it is applied identically across all four channels — seeing, hearing, thinking, knowing get no separate treatment. What matters is not the source of the information and not whether the statement is accurate to that source. What matters is what speaking it does, in a specific listener and occasion, to the growth or decline of skillful and unskillful qualities.

That is a genuinely different axis from the one both Vassakāra’s position and its obvious opposite were arguing along. Neither ‘always report accurately’ nor ‘restrict what you report’ asks the question this criterion asks, which is not about the statement’s truth-value or its epistemic origin at all.

What this leaves open

The discourse gives the criterion and stops; it does not supply examples of what kinds of report tend to grow unskillful qualities and what kinds tend to grow skillful ones. That specificity is left to the listener’s own judgment of the actual situation, which is consistent with a test defined by effect rather than by content — the same words, said to different people or in different circumstances, could satisfy the criterion in one case and fail it in another.

It is worth being honest about what this does not settle. A criterion this general requires real judgment to apply and offers no shortcut; a listener cannot look up in advance which category of statement is safe. The discourse trusts the listener with exactly the kind of discernment its own criterion presupposes.

Vassakāra, twice in this chapter

Vassakāra returns four discourses later, in AN 4.187, with a different question entirely and a story of his own to illustrate the Buddha’s answer. In both appearances he arrives with a stated view, has it examined rather than simply confirmed or denied, and leaves having approved of what he heard.

The pattern is worth noticing across the two discourses: a senior political figure brings a tidy doctrine, and in both cases the Buddha’s response does not simply accept or reject it but relocates the question to a different, more precise axis before answering.

Key terms

diṭṭha / suta / mata / viññāta — “seen”, “heard”, “thought”, “known” — the four channels Vassakāra’s doctrine treats as equally reportable, and which the Buddha’s criterion applies to identically.
Vassakāra — the chief minister of Magadha, who appears twice in this chapter, at AN 4.183 and AN 4.187.
akusalā dhammā abhivaḍḍhanti — “unskillful qualities grow” — the negative half of the criterion that decides whether something should be said.
kusalā dhammā parihāyanti — “skillful qualities decline” — paired with the growth of the unskillful, marking exactly the situation to be avoided.
brāhmaṇa — “brahmin” — the Buddha’s address to Vassakāra throughout, even though Vassakāra’s office is that of a minister rather than a temple priest.

The text

The discourse in full: Vassakāra's doctrine, the Buddha's refusal of both extremes, and the actual criterion. Translation: Bhikkhu Sujato (CC0, SuttaCentral).

Vassakāra’s doctrine

§1At one time the Buddha was staying near Rājagaha, in the Bamboo Grove, the squirrels’ feeding ground. Then Vassakāra the brahmin, a chief minister of Magadha, went up to the Buddha, and exchanged greetings with him. When the greetings and polite conversation were over, he sat down to one side and said to the Buddha: “Worthy Gotama, this is my doctrine and view: There’s nothing wrong with talking about what you’ve seen, saying: ‘So I have seen.’ There’s nothing wrong with talking about what you’ve heard, saying: ‘So I have heard.’ There’s nothing wrong with talking about what you’ve thought, saying: ‘So I have thought.’ There’s nothing wrong with talking about what you’ve known, saying: ‘So I have known.’”

The Buddha’s answer

§2“Brahmin, I don’t say you should talk about everything you see, hear, think, and know. But I also don’t say you should talk about nothing you see, hear, think, and know. When talking about certain things you’ve seen, heard, thought, or known, unskillful qualities grow while skillful qualities decline. I say that you shouldn’t talk about those things. When talking about other things you’ve seen, heard, thought, or known, unskillful qualities decline while skillful qualities grow. I say that you should talk about those things.”

Vassakāra departs

§3Then Vassakāra the brahmin, having approved and agreed with what the Buddha said, got up from his seat and left.
