Aṅguttara Nikāya · Discourse 4.125

Love (1st)

Paṭhamamettāsutta — Bhayavagga — the thirteenth chapter of the Fours

Setting
None stated; the Bhayavagga gives no location for this discourse, and it is addressed to the mendicants directly
Speakers
The Buddha alone, addressing the mendicants
Form
AN 4.123 rerun with the four immeasurables in place of the four absorptions
Length
~4 minutes to read
Northern parallel
The immeasurables and their divine fruits are standard across the early collections; this reading guide does not assert a specific matching sutra number
Difficulty
★★★★☆ — the same difficulty as AN 4.123, applied to material most readers are fonder of

Why this discourse

Take the discourse on the four absorptions, keep every sentence of it, and change only what the person is doing: instead of the first absorption, a heart full of love spread to every direction; instead of the second, compassion; then rejoicing; then equanimity. The realms are the same, the lifespans are the same, and so is the ending — the ordinary person who has spread limitless love to the whole world for an eon goes afterwards to hell, the animal realm, or the ghost realm.

Reading guide

The teaching in one sentence

The immeasurables produce the same magnificent, temporary results as the absorptions, and are subject to the same limit.

The mapping

Love goes with the Divinity’s host and one eon; compassion with the gods of streaming radiance and two; rejoicing with the gods of universal beauty and four; equanimity with the gods of abundant fruit and five hundred. These are precisely the four realms AN 4.123 assigned to the four absorptions, in the same order.

The parallel is exact and the discourse does not argue for it. Setting the two texts side by side, the immeasurables and the absorptions are being treated as interchangeable routes to identical destinations — a strong structural claim, made silently, by reusing a template.

This reading guide does not assert which of the two schemes is prior, or how the correspondence was arrived at. What can be said from the text alone is that the compiler who put these four discourses next to each other wanted them read as one system, and built them so they could not be read otherwise.

The formula for each immeasurable is the standard one and repays reading in full: to one direction, and a second, and a third, and a fourth; above, below, across, everywhere, all around; abundant, expansive, limitless, free of enmity and ill will. Nothing about a particular person, and nothing about feeling warmly.

The same three verbs

They enjoy this and like it and find it rewarding. The identical clause from AN 4.123, in the identical position, doing the identical damage.

It is worth dwelling on how counterintuitive this is. The state being relished is limitless goodwill toward every being in every direction. There is nothing selfish in its content. And the discourse treats relishing it exactly as it treated relishing the first absorption, with the same consequence.

The claim is therefore not about the moral quality of the object. It is about the structure of relishing, which does not improve when what is being relished improves. Something can be entirely good and still be held in a way that leads nowhere.

The fall, again

An ordinary person stays there until the lifespan of those gods is spent, then they go to hell or the animal realm or the ghost realm.

This lands harder here than in AN 4.123, and it should be allowed to. A reader can more easily accept that a meditative technique buys only time; it is harder to accept that an eon of boundless love does too.

The discourse does not soften it and neither should a teacher. What it says is that the immeasurables are brahmavihāra — divine dwellings, and dwellings are places one stays in and leaves. AN 4.63 in this same collection called living with a compassionate household life ‘living with Divinity’ and meant it as praise. The praise stands. The limit stands too.

There is also no suggestion that the practice was wasted or wrong. The noble disciple in this discourse is doing exactly the same practice and is extinguished at the end of the same lifespan. What the collection is separating is not good practice from bad but practice from view.

Why equanimity is last

The order — love, compassion, rejoicing, equanimity — is the standard one, and its final term is the one most often misread. Upekkhā here is not indifference and it is not the end of caring. It is spread with the same formula as the other three: to every direction, abundant, expansive, limitless, free of enmity and ill will.

Placing it fourth and giving it five hundred eons says something about its weight. In AN 4.123 the fourth absorption, equanimity’s natural home, received the same figure. The two schemes agree on where the summit is.

For a reader working with the four in practice, the sequence is usually taught as a progression in which each term corrects a failure mode of the one before: love that cannot meet suffering becomes compassion, compassion that curdles into grief is corrected by rejoicing, and rejoicing that becomes attachment to others’ good fortune is corrected by equanimity. The discourse itself says none of this. It only gives the order and the lifespans.

Reading it with its neighbors

AN 4.125 is to AN 4.123 what AN 4.126 will be to AN 4.124. The chapter has laid out four texts in a two-by-two: absorptions or immeasurables, relished or contemplated.

Taught as a set, the grid makes a point no single discourse in it makes. Change the practice and the destination stays where it is. Change what the mind does with the practice and the destination moves. Whatever the collection thinks the practices are for, it does not think they are the operative variable.

That is a useful corrective in both directions. It refuses the idea that any particular technique is the thing that saves, and it equally refuses the idea that the immeasurables are a lesser or preliminary practice — they are given the same realms, the same eons, and the same treatment as the absorptions, without comment.

Key terms

brahmavihāra — “divine dwelling” — the traditional name for the four; a dwelling is somewhere one stays, and this discourse is about leaving.
mettā — “love” — the first of the four; goodwill without an object in particular, spread in every direction.
muditā — “rejoicing” — gladness at what is good for others; third, and matched here with the gods of universal beauty.
upekkhā — “equanimity” — fourth, and spread with the same limitless formula as the other three, which rules out reading it as indifference.
appamāṇa — “limitless” — the word in the spreading formula that gives the four their other name, the immeasurables.

The text

The discourse in full: love written out, compassion and rejoicing compressed, equanimity written out again, with the same comparison closing it. Translation: Bhikkhu Sujato (CC0, SuttaCentral).

Love

§1“Mendicants, these four individuals are found in the world. What four? Firstly, an individual meditates spreading a heart full of love to one direction, and to the second, and to the third, and to the fourth. In the same way above, below, across, everywhere, all around, they spread a heart full of love to the whole world—abundant, expansive, limitless, free of enmity and ill will. They enjoy this and like it and find it rewarding. If they abide in that, are committed to it, and meditate on it often without losing it, when they die they’re reborn in the company of the gods of the Divinity’s host. The lifespan of the gods of the Divinity’s host is one eon. An ordinary person stays there until the lifespan of those gods is spent, then they go to hell or the animal realm or the ghost realm. But a disciple of the Buddha stays there until the lifespan of those gods is spent, then they’re extinguished in that very life. This is the difference between a learned noble disciple and an unlearned ordinary person, that is, when there is a place of rebirth.

Compassion and rejoicing

§2Furthermore, an individual meditates spreading a heart full of compassion … They’re reborn in the company of the gods of streaming radiance. The lifespan of the gods of streaming radiance is two eons. Furthermore, an individual meditates spreading a heart full of rejoicing … They’re reborn in the company of the gods of universal beauty. The lifespan of the gods of universal beauty is four eons.

Equanimity

§3Furthermore, an individual meditates spreading a heart full of equanimity to one direction, and to the second, and to the third, and to the fourth. In the same way above, below, across, everywhere, all around, they spread a heart full of equanimity to the whole world—abundant, expansive, limitless, free of enmity and ill will. They enjoy this and like it and find it rewarding. If they abide in that, are committed to it, and meditate on it often without losing it, when they die they’re reborn in the company of the gods of abundant fruit. The lifespan of the gods of abundant fruit is five hundred eons. An ordinary person stays there until the lifespan of those gods is spent, then they go to hell or the animal realm or the ghost realm. But a disciple of the Buddha stays there until the lifespan of those gods is spent, then they’re extinguished in that very life. This is the difference between a learned noble disciple and an unlearned ordinary person, that is, when there is a place of rebirth. These are the four individuals found in the world.”
