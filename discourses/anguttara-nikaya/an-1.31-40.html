Aṅguttara Nikāya · Discourses 1.31–40

Wild

Adantavagga — the fourth chapter of the Ones

Setting

Length
~2 minutes to read

Difficulty
★☆☆☆☆ — simple to read, though the four verbs are easy to blur together and worth separating

Why this discourse

The previous chapter said the mind is the thing that matters most. This one begins to say what is actually done to it, and it does so with four different verbs rather than one. Tamed, guarded, protected, restrained: these are not synonyms padding out a list, they are four distinct relationships to a mind, and pulling them apart is the whole value of reading the chapter closely. It is also the chapter where the canon’s central image for training — the wild animal brought into working partnership rather than broken — enters the Ones, an image the Chan tradition would eventually turn into ten pictures of a boy and an ox.

Reading guide

The teaching in one sentence

An untamed, unguarded, unprotected, unrestrained mind does more damage than anything else a person has; the same mind under those four kinds of care does more good than anything else.

Four verbs, then all four at once

The structure is worth mapping before reading. AN 1.31–32 give untamed and tamed; 33–34 unguarded and guarded; 35–36 unprotected and protected; 37–38 unrestrained and restrained. Then 39–40 close the chapter by stacking all four terms into a single negative statement and a single positive one.

That closing pair is the reason the chapter is not simply repetitive. The four qualities are presented separately so they can be recognized separately, and then combined so that no one of them is mistaken for the whole job.

The animal behind the first verb

Danta and its negative adanta are, in ordinary Pāli, what you say about an elephant, a horse, or an ox. A danta animal is one that has been trained — which in this vocabulary means it will now work with a rider or in harness, not that its spirit has been broken. The distinction matters. The Buddha is elsewhere called purisadammasārathi, the trainer of persons fit to be trained, using exactly this stable-and-paddock language about himself.

The image is one of the most durable in Buddhist literature. It runs through the Dhammapada’s chapter on the mind, and in China it eventually produces the ten oxherding pictures, in which the ox is progressively found, roped, tamed, ridden home, and finally forgotten. Anyone who has met those pictures has already met this chapter without knowing it.

Guarded, protected, restrained — three different jobs

English flattens the next three verbs almost to synonyms, and the Pāli does not. Gutta is what a sentry does: watching, on duty, aware of what is approaching. Rakkhita is what you do with something valuable you intend to keep: protecting it from loss. Saṁvuta is literally closed or shut together — it is the same word that appears in indriya-saṁvara, the restraint of the senses that the first chapter of the Ones pointed toward.

So the chapter gives, in order, a trainer, a sentry, a custodian, and a gate. Four postures toward one mind. A student who can say which of the four is missing on a given day has got more out of this chapter than one who can recite all ten entries.

Is this repression?

It is worth meeting the obvious objection head on, because a room of modern readers will raise it. Tamed, guarded, protected, restrained: in English these can all be heard as a program of clamping down on oneself, and a century of popular psychology has taught people that clamping down on oneself does not work and is usually harmful.

Two things separate what is described here from repression. The first is the taming image itself: an animal that has been trained is more capable than an untrained one, not less. Nothing is amputated; a capacity is brought into usable form — which is exactly the point the previous chapter made with the word workable. The second is what saṁvara means in practice elsewhere in the canon. Sense restraint is consistently described not as refusing to see or hear, but as declining to seize on features and elaborate them into something worth pursuing. It is a matter of what you do after contact, not of preventing contact.

That said, the objection should not be dismissed too fast either. The canon does record practitioners who applied restraint destructively, and the tradition responded by adjusting the instruction rather than by telling them to try harder. The corrective for an over-restrained practitioner is not less training but different training — which is itself a piece of information about how training was understood.

Where the chapter is pointing

Read forward, this chapter sets up the next one. Having said that a trained mind is the beneficial one, the collection immediately asks what happens when a mind is aimed in the wrong direction, and answers it with the rice-spike simile of AN 1.41. Training without direction is the gap those two chapters close between them.

Key terms

adanta / danta — “untamed” and “tamed” — ordinary stable vocabulary for a working animal brought into partnership, not broken. The Buddha uses the same root of himself as purisadammasārathi, trainer of persons fit to be trained.
agutta / gutta — “unguarded” and “guarded” — the sentry’s word. Watching, on duty, aware of what is approaching.
arakkhita / rakkhita — “unprotected” and “protected” — what is done with something valuable one intends to keep from loss.
asaṁvuta / saṁvuta — “unrestrained” and “restrained,” literally unclosed and closed. The same root as indriya-saṁvara, sense restraint — a matter of what one does after contact, not of preventing contact.
mahato anatthāya / atthāya — “for great harm” / “for great benefit” — the fixed refrain that closes every entry in the chapter, carried over from AN 1.21–30.

The text

All ten discourses of the chapter, in order. Each §N below is one complete discourse — §31 is AN 1.31, §40 is AN 1.40 — and the last pair stacks all four qualities together. Translation: Bhikkhu Sujato (CC0, SuttaCentral).

Four qualities, one at a time (AN 1.31–38)

§31“Mendicants, I do not see a single thing that, when it’s not tamed, is so very harmful as the mind. A wild mind is very harmful.”

§32“Mendicants, I do not see a single thing that, when it is tamed, is so very beneficial as the mind. A tamed mind is very beneficial.”

§33“Mendicants, I do not see a single thing that, when it’s not guarded, is so very harmful as the mind. An unguarded mind is very harmful.”

§34“Mendicants, I do not see a single thing that, when it is guarded, is so very beneficial as the mind. A guarded mind is very beneficial.”

§35“Mendicants, I do not see a single thing that, when it’s not protected, is so very harmful as the mind. An unprotected mind is very harmful.”

§36“Mendicants, I do not see a single thing that, when it is protected, is so very beneficial as the mind. A protected mind is very beneficial.”

§37“Mendicants, I do not see a single thing that, when it’s not restrained, is so very harmful as the mind. An unrestrained mind is very harmful.”

§38“Mendicants, I do not see a single thing that, when it is restrained, is so very beneficial as the mind. A restrained mind is very beneficial.”

All four together (AN 1.39–40)

§39“Mendicants, I do not see a single thing that, when it is not tamed, guarded, protected, and restrained, is so very harmful as the mind. An untamed, unguarded, unprotected, and unrestrained mind is very harmful.”

§40“Mendicants, I do not see a single thing that, when it is tamed, guarded, protected, and restrained, is so very beneficial as the mind. A tamed, guarded, protected, and restrained mind is very beneficial.”
