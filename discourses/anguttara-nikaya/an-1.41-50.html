Aṅguttara Nikāya · Discourses 1.41–50

A Spike

Paṇihitaacchavagga — the fifth chapter of the Ones

Setting
None stated; the chapter continues from AN 1.1, whose setting at Sāvatthī is understood to hold
Speakers
The Buddha alone, addressing the mendicants
Form
Similes in impossible-and-possible pairs, two statements about reading another person’s mind, and three short declarations, the last two of which are the most quoted sentences in the Ones
Length
~4 minutes to read
Northern parallel
The murky-water and clear-water similes and the “this is impossible” formula are both well attested in the Chinese Saṁyukta-āgama (T99) and Madhyama-āgama (T26); this reading guide does not assert a specific matching sutra number
Difficulty
★★★☆☆ — the similes are easy, but the closing pair on the radiant mind carries centuries of contested interpretation

Why this discourse

This is the chapter where the Ones stop asserting and start showing. Four of its ten entries are similes, and they are good ones — a husk of grain trodden underfoot, a lake you can or cannot see the bottom of, the most pliable timber the speaker knew. Then, in its last two lines, the chapter says something it does not explain and never repeats in this form: that this mind is radiant, and that what obscures it arrives from outside. Those two sentences are among the most consequential in the entire Pāli canon — not because of what they settle, but because of what later Buddhists, especially in China, built on top of them.

Reading guide

The teaching in one sentence

Direction and clarity decide what a mind can accomplish — and the mind that is being directed and cleared is, in itself, already luminous.

The spike (AN 1.41–42)

A spike of rice or barley — the bristle on an ear of grain — is trodden on. If it is lying the wrong way, it cannot draw blood no matter how hard you step. If it is lying the right way, it can. Nothing about the spike has changed except its orientation, and the whole outcome turns on that.

The Pāli terms are micchāpaṇihita and sammāpaṇihita: wrongly and rightly aimed, from a root meaning to set down or place. The point is unusually specific for a teaching about effort. It does not say a lazy mind fails and an energetic one succeeds; it says a misdirected mind cannot break ignorance however hard it is pressed. Force applied along the wrong vector produces nothing. That is a more useful and more uncomfortable claim than the one about effort, and it is the reason this chapter follows the one on taming: a trained mind pointed the wrong way is still a spike lying flat.

Knowing where a mind is going (AN 1.43–44)

The two entries in the middle of the chapter are the ones a modern reader is most likely to stumble over. The Buddha says that having comprehended the mind of a person whose mind is corrupted, he knows that if they died at that moment they would be “placed in hell as if delivered there” — and the reverse for a pure mind and a heavenly rebirth.

Two things are worth noticing before the passage is either accepted or rejected. First, no one is judging. The Pāli image is of something carried and set down: the destination follows from the state of mind the way a placed object follows from the placing, with no tribunal in between. Second, the operative factor named is the mind’s condition at that moment, not a tally of past deeds. Whatever one makes of the cosmology, the mechanism being described is continuous with everything else in this chapter: the state of the mind is what determines what follows from it.

The lake (AN 1.45–46)

The best-known simile here. A lake that is cloudy, churned up, and muddy hides the clams, the pebbles, and the schools of fish from someone with perfectly good eyesight standing right on the bank. Clear the water and the same eyes see everything. The failure was never in the eye.

What the clouded mind is said to be unable to do is worth reading carefully, because it is not simply “meditate well.” It cannot know what is good for itself, what is good for another, or what is good for both; and it cannot realize any distinction in knowledge and vision worthy of the noble ones. The ordinary and the exalted are placed on the same list. Whatever else clarity is for, it is first of all for being able to tell what actually helps.

This simile travels a long way. Zhiyi returns to still and turbid water repeatedly in the Mohe Zhiguan, where śamatha is the settling and vipaśyanā the seeing that settling makes possible —止 and 觀 as two halves of the one image this sutta states in four lines.

Boxwood and speed (AN 1.47–48)

Two brief entries carry the chapter forward. The first names the most pliable and workable timber the speaker’s audience knew and says a developed mind is more so — picking up kammaniya from AN 1.21 and giving it something to be measured against. The second is a rarity: the Buddha says the mind changes so quickly that it is not easy to give a simile for it at all. A collection built almost entirely out of similes reporting that it has run out of one is worth pausing on.

The radiant mind (AN 1.49–50)

The chapter closes with two sentences. Pabhassaramidaṁ, bhikkhave, cittaṁ — “this mind is radiant” — and then, in one case, that it is corrupted by āgantuka corruptions, and in the other, that it is freed from them. Āgantuka is the word for a visitor or an incomer: something that arrives, that was not originally of the house. Sujato renders it “passing.”

What these lines mean in their own context is genuinely disputed, and the honest position is to say so. The Theravāda commentaries read pabhassara as referring to the bhavaṅga, the mind’s resting continuum, and take the passage as a claim about the mind’s capacity to be purified rather than about a pure essence underneath. The surrounding canon offers no support for reading it as an eternal, unconditioned self — which the same canon rejects repeatedly and explicitly. The next chapter, AN 1.51–52, does supply a gloss, and it is a practical one: the difference it draws is between someone who understands this about the mind and someone who does not.

What is not disputed is the influence. Read together with a handful of similar passages, these two sentences stand behind the East Asian doctrine of the intrinsically pure mind (自性清淨心), behind the buddha-nature literature, and ultimately behind Huineng’s “the self-nature of bodhi is fundamentally pure.” That is a real line of descent, traceable through texts this site carries in translation, and it is worth showing students both ends of it — while being clear that the Chinese development is a development, not a paraphrase.

Key terms

micchāpaṇihita / sammāpaṇihita — “wrongly aimed” and “rightly aimed,” from a root meaning to set down or place. The rice-spike simile turns entirely on this, and on nothing about force or effort.
netaṁ ṭhānaṁ vijjati — “this is quite impossible” — literally, this situation is not found. A fixed canonical formula for structural, not merely unlikely, impossibility; the chapter uses it and its positive twin four times.
āvila / anāvila — “clouded” and “unclouded” — the state of the lake water, and by the simile the state of a mind that can or cannot see what is in front of it.
pabhassara — “radiant, luminous” — said of the mind in AN 1.49–50 and again at the head of the next chapter. Read in the Theravāda commentaries as the mind’s resting continuum; read in East Asia as the intrinsically pure mind, 自性清淨心.
āgantuka upakkilesa — “incoming corruptions” — āgantuka is the word for a visitor or newcomer, something not originally of the house. Sujato’s “passing corruptions” keeps the sense that they arrive and can leave.

The text

All ten discourses of the chapter, in order. Each §N below is one complete discourse — §41 is AN 1.41, §50 is AN 1.50. Translation: Bhikkhu Sujato (CC0, SuttaCentral).

The spike (AN 1.41–42)

§41“Mendicants, suppose a spike of rice or barley was pointing the wrong way. If you trod on it with hand or foot, it is impossible for it to break the skin and produce blood. Why is that? Because the spike is pointing the wrong way. In the same way, it is quite impossible for a mendicant whose mind is pointing the wrong way to break ignorance, produce knowledge, and realize extinguishment. Why is that? Because their mind is pointing the wrong way.”

§42“Mendicants, suppose a spike of rice or barley was pointing the right way. If you trod on it with hand or foot, it’s possible it may break the skin and produce blood. Why is that? Because the spike is pointing the right way. In the same way, it is quite possible for a mendicant whose mind is pointing the right way to break ignorance, produce knowledge, and realize extinguishment. Why is that? Because the mind is pointing the right way.”

A corrupted mind and a pure one (AN 1.43–44)

§43“Mendicants, when I’ve comprehended the mind of an individual whose mind is corrupted, I understand: ‘If this individual were to die right now, they would be placed in hell as if delivered there.’ Why is that? Because their mind is corrupted. Corruption of mind is the reason why some sentient beings, when their body breaks up, after death, are reborn in a place of loss, a bad place, the underworld, hell.”

§44“Mendicants, when I’ve comprehended the mind of an individual whose mind is pure, I understand: ‘If this individual were to die right now, they would be placed in heaven as if delivered there.’ Why is that? Because their mind is pure. Purity of mind is the reason why some sentient beings, when their body breaks up, after death, are reborn in a good place, a heavenly realm.”

The lake (AN 1.45–46)

§45“Suppose, mendicants, there was a lake that was cloudy, murky, and muddy. A person with clear eyes standing on the bank would not see the clams and mussels, and pebbles and gravel, and schools of fish swimming about or staying still. Why is that? Because the water is clouded. In the same way, that a mendicant whose mind is clouded would know what’s for their own good, the good of another, or the good of both; or that they would realize any superhuman distinction in knowledge and vision worthy of the noble ones: this is quite impossible. Why is that? Because their mind is clouded.”

§46“Suppose, mendicants, there was a lake that was transparent, clear, and unclouded. A person with clear eyes standing on the bank would see the clams and mussels, and pebbles and gravel, and schools of fish swimming about or staying still. Why is that? Because the water is unclouded. In the same way, that a mendicant whose mind is not clouded would know what’s for their own good, the good of another, or the good of both; or that they would realize any superhuman distinction in knowledge and vision worthy of the noble ones: this is quite possible. Why is that? Because their mind is unclouded.”

Pliable, and quick to change (AN 1.47–48)

§47“Just as, mendicants, the boxwood is said to be the best kind of tree in terms of its pliability and workability, so too, I do not see a single thing that’s as pliable and workable as the mind, when it is developed and cultivated. A mind that is developed and cultivated is pliable and workable.”

§48“Mendicants, I do not see a single thing that’s as quick to change as the mind. So much so that it’s not easy to give a simile for how quickly the mind changes.”

The radiant mind (AN 1.49–50)

§49“This mind, mendicants, is radiant. But it’s corrupted by passing corruptions.”

§50“This mind, mendicants, is radiant. And it is freed from passing corruptions.”
