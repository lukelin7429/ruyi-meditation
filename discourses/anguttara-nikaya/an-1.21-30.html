Aṅguttara Nikāya · Discourses 1.21–30

Unworkable

Akammaniyavagga — the third chapter of the Ones

Setting
None stated; the chapter continues from AN 1.1, whose setting at Sāvatthī is understood to hold
Speakers
The Buddha alone, addressing the mendicants
Form
Five matched pairs on a single subject — the mind — each pair setting the undeveloped against the developed
Length
~2 minutes to read
Northern parallel
The contrast between a developed and an undeveloped mind is pervasive in the Chinese Saṁyukta-āgama (T99) and Ekottarika-āgama (T125); this reading guide does not assert a specific matching sutra number
Difficulty
★☆☆☆☆ — one claim, restated ten times; the difficulty is in believing it, not in parsing it

Why this discourse

The first two chapters of the Ones described what the mind runs into and what clouds it. This one names the mind itself, and does nothing else for ten entries. It is the least varied chapter so far and the most consequential: every later claim in the collection about training, meditation, or awakening assumes what is asserted here, which is that the mind is the one thing whose condition decides the value of everything else. The chapter is also where bhāvanā — “development,” the word that becomes 修 in Chinese and carries the whole of East Asian practice vocabulary behind it — first moves to the center of the collection.

Reading guide

The teaching in one sentence

Nothing you own has as much downside when neglected, or as much upside when developed, as your own mind.

One noun, ten times

The chapter is five pairs, and each pair sets a negative statement beside its exact positive twin: unworkable and workable (21–22), harmful and beneficial (23–24), the same again with untapped or realized potential (25–26), the same again with developed and cultivated (27–28), and finally bringing suffering or bringing happiness (29–30). Only the qualifier moves. The subject of all ten sentences is the same word: citta.

That is not padding, and it is not an accident of oral transmission. The Ones are a collection of superlatives — I do not see a single thing that… — and a superlative is a comparative claim. Saying it once about the mind establishes a ranking; saying it five ways establishes that the ranking holds no matter which axis you measure on. Usable, beneficial, potent, cultivated, conducive to happiness: whichever question you ask, the answer comes back the same.

What development actually means here

Bhāvanā, from a causative of the verb “to be,” means bringing something into being — developing, cultivating, making it become. English translations reach for “meditation” and are not wrong, but the word is broader and more agricultural than that: it is what you do to a field, over seasons, rather than a technique you apply for an hour. AN 1.27–28 pair it with bahulīkata, “made much of,” which is the repetition half of the same idea.

It is worth saying plainly that this is not self-improvement in the modern sense. Nothing here suggests the mind is being upgraded toward a better version of the person who owns it. The claim is narrower and stranger: an undeveloped mind cannot do the work, and a developed one can.

Why “workable” comes first

The first pair is not about good and bad. It is about usability. Kammaniya means fit for work, wieldy, malleable — the word a metalworker would use for gold that has been brought to the right temper and will now take a shape. An akammaniya mind is not a wicked mind; it is a mind that will not do what is asked of it, the way cold metal will not.

Leading with that image sets the tone for everything after. The problem being described is not primarily moral failure but mechanical failure. That distinction matters when this chapter is taught to people who arrive expecting religion to be mainly about being told they are bad.

Reading “very harmful” without moralism

Six of the ten entries say that an undeveloped mind is mahato anatthāya — “for great harm.” Read quickly, in English, that sounds like an accusation. Read with the first pair still in view, it is closer to a statement about leverage. The mind is the highest-variance thing a person has: neglect it and the cost is unbounded, develop it and the return is unbounded. Nothing else on the list of things a person might attend to has that profile.

AN 1.25–26 make the point most sharply with a word Sujato renders as untapped potential, apātubhūta — literally, not made manifest. The harm of the undeveloped mind is not only what it does; it is also everything it never became.

Where this goes afterward

This chapter is the seed of an enormous literature. Its Pāli neighbor is the Dhammapada’s chapter on the mind, which says the same thing in verse and with better images — the fletcher straightening an arrow, the mind that does what an enemy could not. In the Chinese tradition the same conviction becomes 修心 and eventually 觀心, the practice of taking the mind itself as the object of contemplation, which Zhiyi makes the organizing principle of an entire system. Both are linked below.

Key terms

citta — mind or heart — the single subject of all ten entries. Not the intellect specifically; closer to the whole knowing, feeling, intending capacity.
bhāvita / abhāvita — “developed” and “undeveloped,” from bhāvanā, causing to become. An agricultural word before it is a meditative one, and the ancestor of the Chinese 修.
kammaniya / akammaniya — “workable” and “unworkable” — fit for work, wieldy, malleable, the way tempered metal is workable. The chapter’s Pāli name comes from the negative form.
apātubhūta — “not made manifest”; Sujato’s “with untapped potential.” What the undeveloped mind never became counts as part of the harm.
bahulīkata — “made much of,” cultivated by repetition. It travels as a fixed pair with bhāvita throughout the canon: developed and cultivated.

The text

All ten discourses of the chapter, in order. Each §N below is one complete discourse — §21 is AN 1.21, §30 is AN 1.30 — and the entries run as five matched pairs. Translation: Bhikkhu Sujato (CC0, SuttaCentral).

Workable and unworkable (AN 1.21–22)

§21“Mendicants, I do not see a single thing that, when it’s not developed like this, is as unworkable as the mind. An undeveloped mind is unworkable.”

§22“Mendicants, I do not see a single thing that, when it is developed like this, is as workable as the mind. A developed mind is workable.”

Harmful and beneficial (AN 1.23–28)

§23“Mendicants, I do not see a single thing that, when it’s not developed like this, is so very harmful as the mind. An undeveloped mind is very harmful.”

§24“Mendicants, I do not see a single thing that, when it is developed like this, is so very beneficial as the mind. A developed mind is very beneficial.”

§25“Mendicants, I do not see a single thing that, when it’s not developed, with such untapped potential, is so very harmful as the mind. An undeveloped mind, with untapped potential, is very harmful.”

§26“Mendicants, I do not see a single thing that, when it is developed, with its potential realized, is so very beneficial as the mind. A developed mind, with its potential realized, is very beneficial.”

§27“Mendicants, I do not see a single thing that, when it’s not developed and cultivated, is so very harmful as the mind. An undeveloped and uncultivated mind is very harmful.”

§28“Mendicants, I do not see a single thing that, when it is developed and cultivated, is so very beneficial as the mind. A developed and cultivated mind is very beneficial.”

Suffering and happiness (AN 1.29–30)

§29“Mendicants, I do not see a single thing that, when it’s not developed and cultivated, brings such suffering as the mind. An undeveloped and uncultivated mind brings suffering.”

§30“Mendicants, I do not see a single thing that, when it is developed and cultivated, brings such happiness as the mind. A developed and cultivated mind brings happiness.”
