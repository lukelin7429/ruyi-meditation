Aṅguttara Nikāya · Discourse 6.31

A Trainee

Sekhasutta — Devatāvagga — the fourth chapter of the Sixes

Setting
None stated in the source
Speakers
The Buddha alone, addressing the mendicants
Form
Two matched six-item lists, cause and its direct reversal, in a single short discourse
Length
under 1 minute to read
Northern parallel
This theme of decline versus non-decline for a trainee recurs across the Chinese Āgamas; this reading guide does not assert a specific matching sutra number
Difficulty
★☆☆☆☆ — brief and formulaic, opening a chapter that will return to this decline theme repeatedly

Why this discourse

The chapter titled ‘Deities’ opens, somewhat unexpectedly, with no deity at all: a bare six-item list of what causes a sekha, a trainee, to decline, paired immediately with its six-item reversal. The deities the chapter is named for arrive at the very next discourse, AN 6.32, which restates closely related material through a nighttime visitor.

Reading guide

The teaching in one sentence

A trainee mendicant declines by relishing work, talk, sleep, and company, failing to guard the sense doors, and eating too much; they avoid decline by the six direct opposites of these.

A specific target: the trainee, not every mendicant

Unlike AN 6.21's causes of decline, stated for “a mendicant” generally, this discourse specifies sekhassa bhikkhuno, a trainee mendicant — someone who has entered the path (the same term defined at AN 5.1's reading guide earlier in this series) but not yet completed it. The list may be aimed specifically at this population because a trainee, unlike an arahant, still has something at stake in these six habits.

Four familiar items, two new ones

Work, talk, sleep, and company repeat the trio-plus-one already met at AN 6.14, 6.15, 6.17, and 6.21. The two new items — not guarding the sense doors (indriyesu aguttadvāratā) and eating without moderation (bhojane amattaññutā) — extend the pattern from social and attentional habits to the specific discipline of sense-restraint and eating, both recurring elsewhere in the canon's standard descriptions of a well-trained mendicant's daily conduct.

Compression as the discourse's whole method

Nothing here is elaborated: no story, no closing verse, no explanation of why any one item matters. The entire discourse is the bare naming of six causes and six reversals, back to back, trusting the reader to recognize each item from its appearance elsewhere or its self-evident sense.

Opening a chapter that returns to this theme

AN 6.31 is the first of several discourses in this chapter concerned with what preserves or undermines a mendicant's practice — AN 6.32 and 6.33 restate closely related six-item lists via a deity's report, and AN 6.40, later in the chapter, asks the same question of the entire teaching's long-term survival rather than one mendicant's progress.

Key terms

sekha — “trainee” — one who has entered the path but not completed it, already defined at AN 5.1 earlier in this series.
indriyesu aguttadvāratā — “not guarding the sense doors” — the fifth cause of decline, new to this discourse compared to AN 6.14/6.15/6.17/6.21.
bhojane amattaññutā — “not knowing moderation in eating” — the sixth cause, likewise new to this discourse.
parihāna — “decline” — unchanged from AN 6.21/6.22, now applied specifically to a trainee.
aparihāna — “non-decline” — the direct reversal named in this discourse's second half.

The text

The discourse in full: six causes of a trainee's decline, and their six reversals. Translation: Bhikkhu Sujato (CC0, SuttaCentral).

Six causes of a trainee's decline

§1“These six things lead to the decline of a mendicant trainee. What six? They relish work, talk, sleep, and company. They don’t guard the sense doors, and they eat too much. These six things lead to the decline of a mendicant trainee.

Six causes of non-decline

§2These six things don’t lead to the decline of a mendicant trainee. What six? They don’t relish work, talk, sleep, and company. They guard the sense doors, and they don’t eat too much. These six things don’t lead to the decline of a mendicant trainee.”
