Why this discourse

This chapter closes on its most elaborated version of AN 5.229's comparison: a second set of five snake-qualities, now with an explicit gloss attached to three of them — making explicit what the first discourse left merely asserted, and worth engaging with directly rather than passing over.

Reading guide

A second set of five terms

This discourse names five different qualities of a black snake — irritable, holding grudges, venomous, fork-tongued, and treacherous — again applied directly to women with the identical terms and no separate argument.

This time, an explicit gloss

Unlike AN 5.229, this discourse goes further, explaining three of the five terms directly: a woman's ‘venom’ is explained as usually being ‘very lustful’; her ‘forked tongue’ as usually backbiting (pisuṇavācā, the same wrong-speech term named at AN 5.214); and her ‘treachery’ as usually being unfaithful in marriage.

What the specific glosses reveal

Read plainly, the three glossed vices — sexual desire, divisive speech, and marital infidelity — map closely onto exactly what a celibate male monastic community would fear most: sexual temptation itself, gossip threatening communal harmony, and betrayal undermining the lay-patronage relationships monastics depended on. The comparison reveals at least as much about the anxieties of its intended audience as it claims to reveal about its actual subject.

A qualifying word, easy to miss

Each gloss is introduced with yebhuyyena, ‘usually, for the most part’ — a hedge worth noting, even though it does nothing to soften the sweeping, ungrounded nature of the underlying claim.

Closing this chapter, and closing honestly

The chapter ends here, on its harshest note, with the colophon ‘Dasamaṁ. Dīghacārikavaggo tatiyo’ and an uddāna verse naming this chapter's ten discourses in brief, the same mechanism explained in full at AN 5.10. This guide closes its own treatment the same way it has approached every difficult passage across this project: honestly, without smoothing over what the text plainly says.

Key terms

kodhano, upanāhī — “irritable, holding grudges” — the first two of this discourse's second set of five shared terms.
ghoraviso, dujjivho — “venomous, fork-tongued” — the third and fourth terms, each given an explicit gloss below.
tibbarāgā — “very lustful” — the gloss given for ‘venom’.
pisuṇavācā — “backbiting, divisive speech” — the gloss given for ‘forked tongue’, the same wrong-speech term already named at AN 5.214.
yebhuyyena — “usually, for the most part” — the qualifying word introducing each of the three glosses.

The text

The discourse in full, closing this chapter and presented without alteration: a second set of five qualities, glossed. Translation: Bhikkhu Sujato (CC0, SuttaCentral).

Five more drawbacks of a black snake

§1“Mendicants, there are these five drawbacks of a black snake. What five? It’s irritable, acrimonious, venomous, fork-tongued, and treacherous. These are the five dangers of a black snake.

The same five, applied to a woman, and explained

§2In the same way there are five drawbacks of a lady. What five? She’s irritable, acrimonious, venomous, fork-tongued, and treacherous. This is a lady’s venom: usually she’s very lustful. This is a lady’s forked tongue: usually she backbites. This is a lady’s treachery: usually she’s an adulteress. These are the five drawbacks of a lady.”
