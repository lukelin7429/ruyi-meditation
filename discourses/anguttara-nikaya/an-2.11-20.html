Aṅguttara Nikāya · Discourses 2.11–20

Disciplinary Issues

Adhikaraṇavagga — the second chapter of the Twos

Setting
None stated for most; AN 2.16–17 are visits by brahmins and AN 2.18 an exchange with Ānanda
Speakers
The Buddha; an unnamed brahmin at AN 2.16, the brahmin Jānussoṇi at AN 2.17, and Venerable Ānanda at AN 2.18

Ten discourses — three variations on one pair, then a run of short teachings and two conversations with visitors
Length
~9 minutes to read

Jānussoṇi appears repeatedly in the Chinese Madhyama-āgama (T26), and the material on the decline of the teaching through misplaced wording is widespread; this reading guide does not assert a specific matching sutra number
Difficulty
★★☆☆☆ — straightforward, with one discourse whose logic repays going through twice

Why this discourse

This chapter is worth the price of the Twos on its own, because of AN 2.15. Two mendicants are in a dispute: one has committed an offense, the other has accused them of it. The Buddha sets out what each should reflect — and both reflections, given in full and in parallel, arrive at the same sentence. So the mistake is mine alone. Not shared, not apportioned; each party, separately, concludes that the fault lies with them. It is one of the most useful passages in the canon for anyone who works inside an institution, and it is almost never quoted.

Reading guide

The teaching in one sentence

A dispute stops being a dispute when each side, separately, works out how it is their own doing.

Two powers, defined three ways (AN 2.11–13)

The chapter opens with the power of reflection (paṭisaṅkhānabala) and the power of development (bhāvanābala), and then does something structurally interesting: it repeats the pair three times, keeping the first term identical and redefining the second.

The power of reflection is the same each time — considering that bad conduct has a bad result here and hereafter, and adjusting accordingly. Development, however, is given first as the power of the trainees, then as the seven awakening factors, and then as the four absorptions. Three answers to the same question, none cancelling the others.

That is a small but genuine lesson in how to read this literature. A term like “development” is not defined once and applied; it is filled in differently depending on what the discourse needs. A reader who insists on a single fixed definition will find the canon inconsistent. A reader who tracks which definition is in play will find it precise.

“The mistake is mine alone” (AN 2.15)

This is the chapter’s centerpiece and it deserves to be read slowly. The setting is a disciplinary issue — adhikaraṇa, the technical term for a formal dispute within the community. One mendicant has committed an offense; another has seen it and said so. Both got upset; both talked to others. This is exactly how institutional conflicts actually escalate.

The Buddha gives each party a reflection to make, and the two are given in the same words with the roles reversed. The offender traces the chain: I committed the offense, so they saw me, so they were upset, so they spoke, so I got upset, so I told others — so the mistake is mine alone. The accuser traces the same chain from the other end: they committed the offense, so I saw them, so I was upset, so I spoke, so they got upset, so they told others — so the mistake is mine alone.

The simile attached is a customs one: like someone who owes duty on their goods. The sense is of a liability that is simply yours, assessed on what you are carrying, not negotiable and not transferable.

What makes the passage remarkable is that it is not a lesson in humility, and it is not asking either party to accept blame they do not deserve. Each reflection is factually accurate about that party’s own contribution. The offender really did commit the offense. The accuser really did choose to voice unhappiness, and really did set off the second half of the chain. Neither is being asked to lie. They are being asked to trace only their own thread, and the discovery is that each thread is sufficient on its own to explain the mess.

Why this works, and where it fails

It is worth being clear about the mechanism, because “take responsibility” is advice that usually does nothing. What the reflection does is remove the one thing a dispute needs in order to continue: a contested account of whose fault it is. If both parties have privately concluded the fault is their own, there is nothing left to argue about, and the discourse says exactly what follows — the issue does not lead to lasting acrimony and enmity, and the mendicants live comfortably.

It should also be said plainly where this does not apply. The passage concerns a disciplinary matter between peers in a community with shared rules and shared standing. It is not a template for situations involving abuse, coercion, or a serious power imbalance, and asking the injured party in such a case to conclude that the mistake is theirs alone would be a misuse of it. The canon has other resources for those situations. A teacher offering this passage to a group should say so, because someone in the room will need to hear it.

Brief and detailed, and the two brahmins (AN 2.14, 2.16–17)

AN 2.14 states that the Realized One teaches in two ways, in brief and in detail, and the next three discourses happen to demonstrate both. An unnamed brahmin asks why beings are reborn well or badly and gets a compact answer — unprincipled conduct, principled conduct — which satisfies him.

Jānussoṇi, a brahmin who appears repeatedly across the canon, asks the identical question and gets a genuinely cryptic answer: what they’ve done and what they’ve not done. He says outright that he does not understand it and asks for the detail, and receives it. That exchange is a small model of how to be taught: the honest admission of not following comes before the explanation, and the text records it without any suggestion that asking was a failing.

It is possible (AN 2.19)

AN 2.19 makes an argument that is easy to skim and worth slowing down for. Give up the unskillful — and then, twice, the reason for the instruction rather than the instruction itself. It is possible to do this; if it were not possible, I would not tell you to. And doing it leads to welfare and happiness; if it led to harm, I would not tell you to.

Two conditions on any instruction, stated explicitly: that it can be done, and that doing it helps. This is the same standard AN 3.65 offers the Kālāmas for evaluating a teaching, turned around and applied by the teacher to his own instructions before giving them.

AN 2.20 then closes the chapter on what makes the teaching decline: words and phrases misplaced, and the meaning misinterpreted — with the causal direction stated, since the second follows from the first. It is the same concern as the misrepresentation chapters of the Ones, narrowed to a point about transmission rather than about intent. You do not have to mean to distort anything.

Key terms

adhikaraṇa — “disciplinary issue” — the technical Vinaya term for a formal dispute within the community, and the subject of AN 2.15.
paṭisaṅkhānabala / bhāvanābala — “the power of reflection” and “the power of development” — the pair AN 2.11–13 states three times, redefining the second term each time.
sekha — “trainee” — one who has entered the path but not completed it. The first of the three definitions given for the power of development.
suṅka — “customs duty” — the simile in AN 2.15 for a liability that is simply yours, assessed on what you are carrying, neither negotiable nor transferable.
dunnikkhitta padabyañjana — “misplaced words and phrases” — what AN 2.20 names as the first cause of the teaching’s decline, with misinterpreted meaning following from it.

The text

All ten discourses of the chapter, in order. Each §N below is one complete discourse — §11 is AN 2.11, §20 is AN 2.20. Translation: Bhikkhu Sujato (CC0, SuttaCentral).

Two powers, three times (AN 2.11–13)

§11“There are, mendicants, these two powers. What two? The power of reflection and the power of development. And what, mendicants, is the power of reflection? It’s when someone reflects: ‘Bad conduct of body, speech, or mind has a bad, painful result in both this life and the next.’ Reflecting like this, they give up bad conduct by way of body, speech, and mind, and develop good conduct by way of body, speech, and mind, keeping themselves pure. This is called the power of reflection. And what, mendicants, is the power of development? In this context, the power of development is the power of the trainees. For when you rely on the power of a trainee, you give up greed, hate, and delusion. Then you don’t do anything unskillful, or practice anything bad. This is called the power of development. These are the two powers.”

§12“There are, mendicants, these two powers. What two? The power of reflection and the power of development. And what, mendicants, is the power of reflection? It’s when someone reflects: ‘Bad conduct of body, speech, or mind has a bad, painful result in both this life and the next.’ Reflecting like this, they give up bad conduct by way of body, speech, and mind, and develop good conduct by way of body, speech, and mind, keeping themselves pure. This is called the power of reflection. And what, mendicants, is the power of development? It’s when a mendicant develops the awakening factors of mindfulness, investigation of principles, energy, rapture, tranquility, immersion, and equanimity, which rely on seclusion, fading away, and cessation, and ripen as letting go. This is called the power of development. These are the two powers.”

§13“There are, mendicants, these two powers. What two? The power of reflection and the power of development. And what, mendicants, is the power of reflection? It’s when someone reflects: ‘Bad conduct of body, speech, or mind has a bad, painful result in both this life and the next.’ Reflecting like this, they give up bad conduct by way of body, speech, and mind, and develop good conduct by way of body, speech, and mind, keeping themselves pure. This is called the power of reflection. And what, mendicants, is the power of development? It’s when a mendicant, quite secluded from sensual pleasures, secluded from unskillful qualities, enters and remains in the first absorption, which has the rapture and bliss born of seclusion, while placing the mind and keeping it connected. As the placing of the mind and keeping it connected are stilled, they enter and remain in the second absorption, which has the rapture and bliss born of immersion, with internal clarity and mind at one, without placing the mind and keeping it connected. And with the fading away of rapture, they enter and remain in the third absorption, where they meditate with equanimity, mindful and aware, personally experiencing the bliss of which the noble ones declare, ‘Equanimous and mindful, one meditates in bliss.’ With the giving up of pleasure and pain and the disappearance of former happiness and sadness, they enter and remain in the fourth absorption, without pleasure or pain, with pure equanimity and mindfulness. This is called the power of development. These are the two powers.”

In brief and in detail (AN 2.14)

§14“Mendicants, the Realized One has two ways of teaching the Dhamma. What two? In brief and in detail. These are the Realized One’s two ways of teaching the Dhamma.”

The mistake is mine alone (AN 2.15)

§15“Mendicants, in a disciplinary issue, if neither the offending mendicant nor the accusing mendicant carefully checks themselves, you can expect that issue will lead to lasting acrimony and enmity, and the mendicants won’t live comfortably. But in a disciplinary issue, if both the offending mendicant and the accusing mendicant carefully check themselves, you can expect that issue won’t lead to lasting acrimony and enmity, and the mendicants will live comfortably. And how, mendicants, does an offending mendicant carefully check themselves? An offending mendicant reflects: ‘I have committed a certain unskillful offense with the body. That mendicant saw me do this. If I hadn’t committed that offense, they wouldn’t have seen me. But since I did commit that offense, they did see me. When they saw me, they were upset, and they voiced their unhappiness to me. Then I also got upset, so I told others. So the mistake is mine alone, like someone who owes customs duty on their goods.’ That’s how, mendicants, an offending mendicant carefully checks themselves. And how, mendicants, does an accusing mendicant carefully check themselves? An accusing mendicant reflects: ‘This mendicant has committed a certain unskillful offense with the body. I saw them do that. If they hadn’t committed that offense, I wouldn’t have seen them. But since they did commit that offense, I did see them. When I saw them, I was upset, and I voiced my unhappiness to them. Then they also got upset, so they told others. So the mistake is mine alone, like someone who owes customs duty on their goods.’ That’s how, mendicants, an accusing mendicant carefully checks themselves. In a disciplinary issue, if neither the offending mendicant nor the accusing mendicant carefully checks themselves, you can expect that issue will lead to lasting acrimony and enmity, and the mendicants won’t live comfortably. But in a disciplinary issue, if both the offending mendicant and the accusing mendicant carefully checks themselves, you can expect that issue won’t lead to lasting acrimony and enmity, and the mendicants will live comfortably.”

Two brahmins ask the same question (AN 2.16–17)

§16Then a certain brahmin went up to the Buddha and exchanged greetings with him. When the greetings and polite conversation were over, he sat down to one side and said to the Buddha: “What is the cause, worthy Gotama, what is the reason why some sentient beings, when their body breaks up, after death, are reborn in a place of loss, a bad place, the underworld, hell?” “Unprincipled and immoral conduct is the reason why some sentient beings, when their body breaks up, after death, are reborn in a place of loss, a bad place, the underworld, hell.” “What is the cause, worthy Gotama, what is the reason why some sentient beings, when their body breaks up, after death, are reborn in a good place, a heavenly realm?” “Principled and moral conduct is the reason why some sentient beings, when their body breaks up, after death, are reborn in a good place, a heavenly realm.” “Excellent, worthy Gotama! Excellent! As if he was righting the overturned, or revealing the hidden, or pointing out the path to the lost, or lighting a lamp in the dark so people with clear eyes can see what’s there, worthy Gotama has made the teaching clear in many ways. I go for refuge to the worthy Gotama, to the teaching, and to the mendicant Saṅgha. From this day forth, may the worthy Gotama remember me as a lay follower who has gone for refuge for life.”

§17Then the brahmin Jānussoṇi went up to the Buddha, and exchanged greetings with him. When the greetings and polite conversation were over, he sat down to one side and said to the Buddha: “What is the cause, worthy Gotama, what is the reason why some sentient beings, when their body breaks up, after death, are reborn in a place of loss, a bad place, the underworld, hell?” “What they’ve done and what they’ve not done. That’s why some sentient beings, when their body breaks up, after death, are reborn in a place of loss, a bad place, the underworld, hell.” “But what is the cause, worthy Gotama, what is the reason why some sentient beings, when their body breaks up, after death, are reborn in a good place, a heavenly realm?” “What they’ve done and what they’ve not done. That’s why some sentient beings, when their body breaks up, after death, are reborn in a good place, a heavenly realm.” “I don’t understand the meaning of what the worthy Gotama has said in brief, without explaining the details. Worthy Gotama, please teach me this matter in detail so I can understand the meaning.” “Well then, brahmin, listen and apply your mind well, I will speak.” “Yes, worthy sir,” Jānussoṇi replied. The Buddha said this: “Firstly, brahmin, someone has done bad things and not done good things by way of body, speech, and mind. So what they’ve done and what they’ve not done is why some sentient beings, when their body breaks up, after death, are reborn in a place of loss, a bad place, the underworld, hell. Furthermore, brahmin, someone has done good things and not done bad things by way of body, speech, and mind. So what they’ve done and what they’ve not done is why some sentient beings, when their body breaks up, after death, are reborn in a good place, a heavenly realm.” “Excellent, worthy Gotama! … From this day forth, may the worthy Gotama remember me as a lay follower who has gone for refuge for life.”

Ānanda, and two conditions on any instruction (AN 2.18–20)

§18Then Venerable Ānanda went up to the Buddha, bowed, and sat down to one side. The Buddha said to him, “Ānanda, I absolutely say that you should not do bad things by way of body, speech, and mind.” “But, sir, if someone does these things that should not be done, what drawbacks should they expect?” “They should expect these drawbacks. They blame themselves. After examination, sensible people criticize them. They get a bad reputation. They feel lost when they die. And when their body breaks up, after death, they are reborn in a place of loss, a bad place, the underworld, hell. These are the drawbacks they should expect.” “Ānanda, I absolutely say that you should do good things by way of body, speech, and mind.” “But, sir, if someone does these things that should be done, what benefits should they expect?” “They should expect these benefits. They don’t blame themselves. After examination, sensible people praise them. They get a good reputation. They don’t feel lost when they die. And when the body breaks up, after death, they are reborn in a good place, a heavenly realm. These are the benefits they should expect.”

§19“Mendicants, give up the unskillful. It is possible to give up the unskillful. If it wasn’t possible, I wouldn’t say: ‘Give up the unskillful.’ But it is possible, and so I say: ‘Give up the unskillful.’ And if giving up the unskillful led to harm and suffering, I would not say: ‘Give up the unskillful.’ But giving up the unskillful leads to welfare and happiness, so I say: ‘Give up the unskillful.’ Mendicants, develop the skillful. It is possible to develop the skillful. If it wasn’t possible, I wouldn’t say: ‘Develop the skillful.’ But it is possible, and so I say: ‘Develop the skillful.’ If developing the skillful led to harm and suffering I wouldn’t say: ‘Develop the skillful.’ But developing the skillful leads to welfare and happiness, so I say: ‘Develop the skillful.’”

§20“These two things, mendicants, lead to the decline and disappearance of the true teaching. What two? The words and phrases are misplaced, and the meaning is misinterpreted. When the words and phrases are misplaced, the meaning is misinterpreted. These two things lead to the decline and disappearance of the true teaching. These two things lead to the continuation, persistence, and enduring of the true teaching. What two? The words and phrases are well organized, and the meaning is correctly interpreted. When the words and phrases are well organized, the meaning is correctly interpreted. These two things lead to the continuation, persistence, and enduring of the true teaching.”
