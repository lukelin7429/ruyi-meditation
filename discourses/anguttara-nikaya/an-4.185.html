Aṅguttara Nikāya · Discourse 4.185

Truths of the Brahmins

Brāhmaṇasaccasutta — Brāhmaṇavagga — the nineteenth chapter of the Fours

Setting
Rājagaha, on the Vulture’s Peak Mountain, and the wanderers’ monastery on the bank of the Serpentine river
Speakers
The Buddha, addressing a group of wanderers of other religions
Form
A debate interrupted, a question asked, and four truths given in an identical three-part pattern
Length
~3 minutes to read
Northern parallel
Redefining brahmin-hood by insight and conduct rather than birth is a recurring move across the early collections; this reading guide does not assert a specific matching sutra number
Difficulty
★★★☆☆ — a clean structure carrying a genuinely subversive redefinition

Why this discourse

A group of wanderers is in the middle of arguing about what the truths of the brahmins are — this way, that way — when the Buddha arrives and asks what they were discussing. Instead of joining the argument, he declares four truths of the brahmins that he has realized himself, and every one of them comes with the same refusal built in: speaking the truth does not entitle anyone to think of themselves as better, equal, or worse than anyone else.

Reading guide

The teaching in one sentence

Four things are genuinely true and worth calling a brahmin’s truths, and none of them licenses the speaker to claim any status by saying them.

Walking into an unfinished argument

The scene is set with unusual specificity for this chapter: several well-known wanderers, named individually, are sitting at their own monastery arguing about ‘the truths of the brahmins’ when the Buddha arrives from his own retreat and asks what conversation was left unfinished.

He does not settle their argument or adjudicate between the positions they were holding. He redirects entirely: I declare these four truths of the brahmins, having realized them with my own insight. The move is not to win the debate as framed but to answer a different, better version of the question the debate was gesturing toward.

The three-part pattern

Each of the four truths follows the identical shape. First, a statement a brahmin makes, given verbatim. Second, an explicit confirmation that the statement is true — speaks the truth, not lies — followed immediately by a denial: the brahmin does not, because of saying it, think of themselves as an ascetic or a brahmin, and does not think ‘I’m better’, ‘I’m equal’, or ‘I’m worse’. Third, what they do instead: practice, out of direct insight into that very truth.

That middle step is the discourse’s real argument, repeated four times so it cannot be missed. Truth-telling and status-claiming are pried apart completely. A brahmin can say something entirely true about killing, about sensual pleasure, about existence, about ownership, and none of it converts into a claim about their own rank relative to anyone else’s. What converts a truth into practice is insight into it, not the saying of it.

The four truths themselves

No sentient beings should be killed — met not with a claim to purity but with practice grounded in kindness and sympathy for living creatures.

All sensual pleasures are impermanent, suffering, and perishable — met with practice for disillusionment, dispassion, and cessation regarding sensual pleasures.

All states of existence are impermanent, suffering, and perishable — met with the identical practice, aimed now at future lives rather than at present pleasure.

I don’t belong to anyone anywhere, and nothing belongs to me anywhere — met with the practice of the path of nothingness, ākiñcaññaṁyeva paṭipadaṁ.

The fourth truth, and where it leads

The fourth truth deserves particular attention because its practice names a specific meditative attainment rather than a general disposition: ākiñcañña, nothingness, the third of the four formless dimensions.

This chapter’s closing discourse, AN 4.190, names the dimension of nothingness as one of four formless attainments collected under āneñjappatta, ‘attained to the imperturbable’. The claim ‘I don’t belong to anyone, nothing belongs to me’ is not a metaphor for detachment in general; it names the specific insight this discourse says the nothingness attainment is built on.

That is a real connection across the chapter worth drawing explicitly, and this reading guide draws it while being precise about its limits: AN 4.190 lists nothingness as one of four formless states reached in sequence, while AN 4.185 isolates only the insight behind that one state as a truth in its own right. The two discourses are not describing the same thing at the same level of detail.

What ‘brahmin’ means here

The discourse never argues against the category of brahmin, and it never claims the wanderers arguing outside are wrong to care about it. What it does is relocate the content of the category entirely: away from birth, ritual, or doctrine held as a position, and onto four specific truths met with practice rather than with a claim to rank.

That is a considerable claim to make while sitting down among wanderers debating exactly this question, and the discourse makes it without polemic. It does not say the wanderers’ truths are false. It supplies four of its own and lets the structural difference — truth severed from status, four times over — do the work of showing what was missing from the argument it interrupted.

Key terms

brāhmaṇasacca — “truth of the brahmins” — the phrase under debate among the wanderers, which the Buddha redefines rather than joins the debate about.
seyyohamasmi / sadisohamasmi / hīnohamasmi — “I’m better”, “I’m equal”, “I’m worse” — the three comparative judgments explicitly denied after each true statement.
abhiññāya — “having had insight into” — what converts a true statement into practice, in place of the saying of it.
ākiñcañña — “nothingness” — the fourth truth’s practice, and the third of the four formless dimensions named at AN 4.190.
saccaṁ āha, no musā — “speaks the truth, not lies” — the confirmation attached to each of the four statements before its status-claim is denied.

The text

The discourse in full: the interrupted debate, the question, and four truths given in an identical pattern. Translation: Bhikkhu Sujato (CC0, SuttaCentral).

An unfinished argument

§1Once the Buddha was staying near Rājagaha, on the Vulture’s Peak Mountain. Now at that time several very well-known wanderers were residing in the monastery of the wanderers on the bank of the Serpentine river. They included Annabhāra, Varadhara, Sakuludāyī, and other very well-known wanderers. Then in the late afternoon, the Buddha came out of retreat and went to the wanderer’s monastery on the bank of the Serpentine river. Now at that time this discussion came up while those wanderers of other religions were sitting together, “The truths of the brahmins are like this; the truths of the brahmins are like that.” Then the Buddha went up to those wanderers, sat down on the seat spread out, and said to them, “Wanderers, what were you sitting talking about just now? What conversation was left unfinished?” “Well, worthy Gotama, this discussion came up among us while we were sitting together: ‘The truths of the brahmins are like this; the truths of the brahmins are like that.’”

The first truth: no killing

§2“Wanderers, I declare these four truths of the brahmins, having realized them with my own insight. What four? Take a brahmin who says: ‘No sentient beings should be killed.’ In speaking like this, a brahmin speaks the truth, not lies. But they don’t think of themselves as an ‘ascetic’ or ‘brahmin’ because of that. Nor do they think ‘I’m better’ or ‘I’m equal’ or ‘I’m worse’. Rather, they simply practice out of kindness and sympathy for living creatures, having had insight into the truth of that.

The second truth: sensual pleasures are impermanent

§3Take another brahmin who says: ‘All sensual pleasures are impermanent, suffering, and perishable.’ Saying this, a brahmin speaks the truth, not lies. But they don’t think of themselves as an ‘ascetic’ or ‘brahmin’ because of that. Nor do they think ‘I’m better’ or ‘I’m equal’ or ‘I’m worse’. Rather, they simply practice for disillusionment, dispassion, and cessation regarding sensual pleasures, having had insight into the truth of that.

The third truth: all existence is impermanent

§4Take another brahmin who says: ‘All states of existence are impermanent, suffering, and perishable.’ … They simply practice for disillusionment, dispassion, and cessation regarding future lives, having had insight into the truth of that.

The fourth truth: nothing belongs to me

§5Take another brahmin who says: ‘I don’t belong to anyone anywhere. And nothing belongs to me anywhere.’ In speaking like this, a brahmin speaks the truth, not lies. But they don’t think of themselves as an ‘ascetic’ or ‘brahmin’ because of that. Nor do they think ‘I’m better’ or ‘I’m equal’ or ‘I’m worse’. Rather, they simply practice the path of nothingness, having had insight into the truth of that. These are the four truths of the brahmins that I declare, having realized them with my own insight.”
