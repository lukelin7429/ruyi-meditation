Aṅguttara Nikāya · Discourse 4.252

Expressions (3rd)

Why this discourse

Denying having seen, heard, thought, or known something one actually has — the false denial this series first read paired with its opposite at AN 4.218 and AN 4.228, now given its own discourse as an ignoble expression, third in this chapter's set of four.

Reading guide

The teaching in one sentence

Denying that one has seen, heard, thought, or known something one actually has is one of the four ignoble ways of speaking.

The mirror of AN 4.251, independently stated

Diṭṭhe adiṭṭhavāditā, sute asutavāditā, mute amutavāditā, viññāte aviññātavāditā — false denial of genuine experience, the content already read paired with truthful affirmation at AN 4.218 and AN 4.228. As at AN 4.250 and AN 4.251, this chapter separates what those two earlier pairings joined, giving false denial a discourse entirely its own.

A point already established, not reargued

This series noted at AN 4.218 that false denial is held to the same weight as false claim, neither treated as the lesser fault. That point does not need remaking here; it is a feature of the content, not of any particular formula wrapping it, and it carries over unchanged into this chapter’s noble/ignoble frame exactly as it carried over into the khata frame at AN 4.228.

Three of four, now separate

With this discourse, three of the vocabulary’s four combinations have been given independent standing in this chapter: false claim at AN 4.250, honest denial at AN 4.251, and false denial here. One combination remains — honest claim, saying one has experienced what one actually has — and the pattern established over the previous three discourses leaves little doubt about what the final discourse in this set will say.

Key terms

diṭṭhe adiṭṭhavāditā — “denying having seen what one has seen” — identical wording to AN 4.218 and AN 4.228.
anariyavohāra — “ignoble expression” — this discourse's frame, matching AN 4.250's for the false claim.
diṭṭha suta muta viññāta — the fourfold division of experience, unchanged across every appearance of this vocabulary in this series.
musāvāda — “false speech” — the general category this specific false denial narrows to testimony about one's own experience.
vohāra — “expression, way of speaking” — naming this discourse and its three companions in this chapter's atomized set.

The text

The discourse in full: one of the four ignoble expressions, named. Translation: Bhikkhu Sujato (CC0, SuttaCentral).

An ignoble expression

§1“Mendicants, there are these four ignoble expressions. What four? Saying you haven’t seen, heard, thought, or known something, and you have. These are the four ignoble expressions.”
