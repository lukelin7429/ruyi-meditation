Aṅguttara Nikāya · Discourse 4.180

The Four Great References

Mahāpadesasutta — Cetanāvagga — the eighteenth chapter of the Fours

Setting
Near Bhoga City, at the Ānanda Shrine — the only named setting in this chapter
Speakers
The Buddha alone, addressing the mendicants
Form
Four sources of a claim, each tested by the identical two-outcome procedure
Length
~6 minutes to read
Northern parallel
The four great references are foundational to the methodology of textual verification across the tradition and have well-known counterparts in the Chinese Āgamas; this reading guide does not assert a specific matching sutra number
Difficulty
★★★☆☆ — procedurally repetitive by design, and one of the most consequential discourses in the whole collection for how the rest of it should be read

Why this discourse

A mendicant says: I heard this directly from the Buddha. Another says: I heard it from a whole Saṅgha of respected elders. Another: from several learned senior mendicants. Another: from a single learned senior mendicant. In every one of the four cases, the instruction is identical — neither approve nor reject the claim on the spot. Check the words against the discourses and the training. Keep what fits. Discard what does not. The source of a claim, however impressive, is not the test.

Reading guide

The teaching in one sentence

A claim about what the Buddha taught is verified by whether it fits the discourses and the training, not by the authority of whoever reports it.

A named place, for once

Every other discourse in this chapter opens with no location at all. This one gives a specific setting — near Bhoga City, at the Ānanda Shrine — and the specificity suits the content: this is a discourse about how to establish what actually happened and what was actually said, and it begins by being precise about where it happened itself.

Four sources, ranked by proximity

The four are not four kinds of content but four kinds of source, arranged from closest to most distant: heard directly from the Buddha himself; heard from a Saṅgha living together with seniors and leaders; heard from several senior mendicants, learned, inheritors of the heritage; heard from a single senior mendicant of the same description.

It would be natural to expect the test to get stricter as the source gets more remote — that a report traced to the Buddha himself should be trusted more readily than one traced to a single monk several steps removed. The discourse gives no such gradient. Every one of the four is tested exactly the same way, with exactly the same words.

The procedure, identical four times

Neither approve nor reject the mendicant’s statement outright. Memorize the words and phrases carefully. Check whether they fit in the discourses and are exhibited in the training. If they do not fit and are not exhibited: clearly this is not the word of the Blessed One… it has been incorrectly memorized, and it should be discarded. If they do fit and are exhibited: clearly this is the word of the Blessed One… it has been correctly memorized, and it should be remembered.

Sutte otāretabbāni, vinaye sandassetabbāni: to be lowered into the discourses, shown against the training. Two independent checks, both required, neither sufficient alone by the discourse’s own repeated formula.

The refusal to approve or reject on the spot is worth dwelling on. The instruction is not suspicion of the mendicant making the claim, and it is not automatic acceptance either. It is a deferral: hold the claim, apply the test, and let the test decide.

What this makes irrelevant, and what it does not

The discourse is explicit that hearing something directly from the Buddha is not, by itself, sufficient grounds to accept it as his teaching, once the report has passed through a human memory. Even the first and closest of the four sources gets the identical two-outcome test as the fourth and most distant.

This does not mean the discourse treats memory as generally untrustworthy, or encourages a listener to doubt every report as a matter of course. What it does is specify a check that applies regardless of how trustworthy the source seems, on the grounds that trustworthiness of a person is not the same thing as accuracy of a particular memorized statement.

It is a strikingly modern-sounding principle stated in an ancient idiom: the credibility of a source and the correctness of a specific claim are different questions, and only one of them is being tested here.

Reading this against AN 4.160

Seven chapters back, AN 4.160 named four things that lead to the disappearance of the true teaching, and the first of them was mendicants memorizing discourses incorrectly, with misplaced words and phrases, so that the meaning is misinterpreted.

AN 4.180 is the other half of that concern: not a description of the failure mode, but the actual procedure for catching it before it spreads. Where AN 4.160 diagnosed a risk to the tradition’s survival, this discourse hands the community a method.

Read together, the two discourses show a tradition that names its own vulnerability — a teaching held only in memory can be corrupted by an honest mistake, not only by malice — and then builds an explicit, repeatable check against exactly that vulnerability, applied uniformly regardless of how impressive the source of a claim sounds.

Closing the chapter

The Cetanāvagga closes here, on a discourse about testing claims rather than making them, after nine discourses that ranged from the mechanics of intention and rebirth through two philosophical tetralemmas, an exemplary-disciple discourse, an elements meditation, and two matched treatments of the same question about extinguishment.

It is a fitting close for a chapter this dense and this varied: a discourse that does not add new content so much as supply the method by which everything already read, and everything still to come, is supposed to be checked. What follows is the Sappurisavagga, the next chapter of the Fours, opening with the qualities of a true person.

Key terms

mahāpadesa — “great reference” — a source of a claim about the teaching, ranked here not by authority but by the test each is subjected to.
na abhinanditabbaṁ nappaṭikkositabbaṁ — “should be neither approved nor rejected” — the discourse’s opening instruction, a deferral rather than an acceptance or a suspicion.
sutte otāretabbāni — “should be lowered into the discourses” — checked for fit against the existing body of teaching.
vinaye sandassetabbāni — “should be exhibited in the training” — checked against the monastic discipline, the second and independently required test.
duggahitaṁ / suggahitaṁ — “incorrectly memorized” and “correctly memorized” — the two possible verdicts, applied identically regardless of which of the four sources reported the claim.

The text

The discourse in full: the setting, and four sources of a claim each tested by the identical procedure. Translation: Bhikkhu Sujato (CC0, SuttaCentral).

The Buddha will teach the four great references

§1At one time the Buddha was staying near the Bhoga City, at the Ānanda Shrine. There the Buddha addressed the mendicants, “Mendicants!” “Venerable sir,” they replied. The Buddha said this: “Mendicants, I will teach you the four great references. Listen and apply your mind well, I will speak.” “Yes, sir,” they replied. The Buddha said this:

First reference: heard from the Buddha himself

§2“Mendicants, what are the four great references? Take a mendicant who says: ‘Reverend, I have heard and learned this in the presence of the Buddha: this is the teaching, this is the training, this is the Teacher’s instruction.’ You should neither approve nor reject that mendicant’s statement. Instead, having carefully memorized those words and phrases, you should make sure they fit in the discourse and are exhibited in the training. If they do not fit in the discourse and are not exhibited in the training, you should draw the conclusion: ‘Clearly this is not the word of the Blessed One, the perfected one, the fully awakened Buddha. It has been incorrectly memorized by that mendicant.’ And so you should discard it. Take another mendicant who says: ‘Reverend, I have heard and learned this in the presence of the Buddha: this is the teaching, this is the training, this is the Teacher’s instruction.’ You should neither approve nor reject that mendicant’s statement. Instead, having carefully memorized those words and phrases, you should make sure they fit in the discourse and are exhibited in the training. If they fit in the discourse and are exhibited in the training, you should draw the conclusion: ‘Clearly this is the word of the Blessed One, the perfected one, the fully awakened Buddha. It has been correctly memorized by that mendicant.’ You should remember it. This is the first great reference.

Second reference: from a Saṅgha of seniors and leaders

§3Take another mendicant who says: ‘In such-and-such monastery lives a Saṅgha with seniors and leaders. I’ve heard and learned this in the presence of that Saṅgha: this is the teaching, this is the training, this is the Teacher’s instruction.’ You should neither approve nor reject that mendicant’s statement. Instead, having carefully memorized those words and phrases, you should make sure they fit in the discourse and are exhibited in the training. If they do not fit in the discourse and are not exhibited in the training, you should draw the conclusion: ‘Clearly this is not the word of the Blessed One, the perfected one, the fully awakened Buddha. It has been incorrectly memorized by that Saṅgha.’ And so you should discard it. Take another mendicant who says: ‘In such-and-such monastery lives a Saṅgha with seniors and leaders. I’ve heard and learned this in the presence of that Saṅgha: this is the teaching, this is the training, this is the Teacher’s instruction.’ You should neither approve nor reject that mendicant’s statement. Instead, having carefully memorized those words and phrases, you should make sure they fit in the discourse and are exhibited in the training. If they fit in the discourse and are exhibited in the training, you should draw the conclusion: ‘Clearly this is the word of the Blessed One, the perfected one, the fully awakened Buddha. It has been correctly memorized by that Saṅgha.’ You should remember it. This is the second great reference.

Third reference: from several learned senior mendicants

§4Take another mendicant who says: ‘In such-and-such monastery there are several senior mendicants who are very learned, inheritors of the heritage, who remember the teachings, the monastic law, and the outlines. I’ve heard and learned this in the presence of those senior mendicants: this is the teaching, this is the training, this is the Teacher’s instruction.’ You should neither approve nor reject that mendicant’s statement. Instead, having carefully memorized those words and phrases, you should make sure they fit in the discourse and are exhibited in the training. If they do not fit in the discourse and are not exhibited in the training, you should draw the conclusion: ‘Clearly this is not the word of the Blessed One, the perfected one, the fully awakened Buddha. It has been incorrectly memorized by those senior mendicants.’ And so you should discard it. Take another mendicant who says: ‘In such-and-such monastery there are several senior mendicants who are very learned, inheritors of the heritage, who remember the teachings, the monastic law, and the outlines. I’ve heard and learned this in the presence of those senior mendicants: this is the teaching, this is the monastic law, this is the Teacher’s instruction.’ You should neither approve nor reject that mendicant’s statement. Instead, having carefully memorized those words and phrases, you should make sure they fit in the discourse and are exhibited in the training. If they fit in the discourse and are exhibited in the training, you should draw the conclusion: ‘Clearly this is the word of the Blessed One, the perfected one, the fully awakened Buddha. It has been correctly memorized by those senior mendicants.’ You should remember it. This is the third great reference.

Fourth reference: from a single learned senior mendicant

§5Take another mendicant who says: ‘In such-and-such monastery there is a single senior mendicant who is very learned, an inheritor of the heritage, who has memorized the teachings, the monastic law, and the outlines. I’ve heard and learned this in the presence of that senior mendicant: this is the teaching, this is the training, this is the Teacher’s instruction.’ You should neither approve nor reject that mendicant’s statement. Instead, having carefully memorized those words and phrases, you should make sure they fit in the discourse and are exhibited in the training. If they do not fit in the discourse and are not exhibited in the training, you should draw the conclusion: ‘Clearly this is not the word of the Blessed One, the perfected one, the fully awakened Buddha. It has been incorrectly memorized by that senior mendicant.’ And so you should discard it. Take another mendicant who says: ‘In such-and-such monastery there is a single senior mendicant who is very learned, an inheritor of the heritage, who has memorized the teachings, the monastic law, and the outlines. I’ve heard and learned this in the presence of that senior mendicant: this is the teaching, this is the training, this is the Teacher’s instruction.’ You should neither approve nor reject that mendicant’s statement. Instead, having carefully memorized those words and phrases, you should make sure they fit in the discourse and are exhibited in the training. If they fit in the discourse and are exhibited in the training, you should draw the conclusion: ‘Clearly this is the word of the Blessed One, the perfected one, the fully awakened Buddha. It has been correctly memorized by that senior mendicant.’ You should remember it. This is the fourth great reference. These are the four great references.”
