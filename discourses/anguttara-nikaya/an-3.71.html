Aṅguttara Nikāya · Discourse 3.71

With Channa

Channasutta — Ānandavagga — the eighth chapter of the Threes

Setting
Sāvatthī, in Jeta’s Grove, Anāthapiṇḍika’s monastery
Speakers
The wanderer Channa and Venerable Ānanda
Form
A question, a request for reasons, the threefold analysis with an added clause, and a question about method
Length
~3 minutes to read
Northern parallel
Comparable material is preserved in the Chinese Āgamas; this reading guide does not assert a specific matching sutra number
Difficulty
★★☆☆☆ — familiar material with one new sentence in it, and a good closing line

Why this discourse

The chapter named after Ānanda opens with him answering for the tradition. A wanderer asks whether they advocate giving up greed, hate, and delusion; he says yes; the wanderer asks what drawbacks they have seen that they should say so. The answer is the analysis AN 3.54 gave, with one clause added — greed is a destroyer of sight, vision, and knowledge; it blocks wisdom — and it closes with a sentence worth memorizing.

Reading guide

The teaching in one sentence

Greed is objected to because of what it does to seeing, and there is a specified method for removing it.

The shape of the question

Channa does not ask what the teaching is. He asks two things in sequence: do you advocate this, and what drawbacks have you seen that you do?

That second question is the interesting one, and it is the right question to ask any tradition. Not what do you recommend, but what did you observe that made you recommend it. Ānanda answers it as asked — the reply is a list of consequences, not a citation of authority, and he never says that the Buddha taught it.

The wanderer is named Channa, which is also the name of the Buddha’s former charioteer who became a difficult monk. This is a different person; the canon has several Channas and does not distinguish them by epithet here.

The analysis, and the clause that is new

The first three parts are the ones AN 3.54 gave to a wanderer: a greedy person intends harm and suffers mental pain; acts badly in body, speech, and mind; and does not truly understand what is for their own good, another’s, or both. Each with its negation when greed has gone.

Then a fourth clause that the earlier discourses do not have: greed is a destroyer of sight, vision, and knowledge. It blocks wisdom, it’s on the side of distress, and it doesn’t lead to extinguishment.

Three verbs and a direction. Andhakaraṇa, making blind; acakkhukaraṇa, making eyeless; aññāṇakaraṇa, making unknowing. Then paññānirodhika, blocking wisdom, and a placement: on the side of distress, not leading to extinguishment.

This is the same claim AN 3.54 made about judgment, sharpened into a metaphor of blindness and then given a destination. It is worth telling a class that the canon’s central objection to greed is not that it is selfish but that it is a visual impairment — which is why the antidote is described as seeing rather than as restraint.

The question about method

Channa then asks the question that makes the discourse more than a complaint: is there a path and a practice for giving up that greed, hate, and delusion?

Ānanda answers with the noble eightfold path, given in full. Nothing else is offered and no shorter version is provided.

The pairing of these two questions is the discourse’s structure, and it is a good model for teaching anything. First, what is wrong with the current situation, stated as consequences that can be checked; then, what is to be done about it, stated as a method with parts. A diagnosis and a procedure, in that order, and nothing in between.

The closing line

“This is a fine path, a fine practice, for giving up that greed, hate, and delusion. Just this much is enough for diligence.”

Alameva appamādāya — this much is enough to be diligent about. It is Channa’s line, not Ānanda’s, and he is a wanderer of another school.

The sentence is doing something specific. He is not converting; he does not go for refuge, and the discourse does not record him becoming a lay follower, which is unusual in a chapter full of conversions. What he says is that the material he has just been given is sufficient to work on, which is a smaller and in some ways more interesting response.

Appamāda, diligence or heedfulness, is the canon’s great positive term — the opposite of the pamāda that the person in AN 3.36 gave as their excuse to King Yama. A discourse that has spent its length on what greed does to sight ends with somebody saying that this is enough to be going on with.

Using it

This is the version of the greed analysis to teach when the class wants to know why anybody would bother giving something up. AN 3.53 answers the question is this checkable; AN 3.54 adds the damage to judgment; this one adds the blindness clause and, crucially, the method.

Channa’s two questions are also the most transferable thing in the chapter. A teacher who can answer what drawbacks have you seen? and is there a path and a practice? about their own subject has a lesson; a teacher who can answer only the first has a complaint.

Key terms

ādīnava — “drawback, danger” — what Channa asks to have been seen, not taught. The question is about observation rather than authority.
andhakaraṇa acakkhukaraṇa — “making blind, making eyeless” — the new clause’s verbs for what greed does, turning the earlier claim about judgment into a metaphor of sight.
paññānirodhika — “blocking wisdom” — and then a placement: on the side of distress, not leading to extinguishment.
appamāda — “diligence, heedfulness” — the canon’s great positive term, and the opposite of the pamāda offered as an excuse in AN 3.36.
alameva appamādāya — “just this much is enough for diligence” — Channa’s closing line, spoken by a wanderer of another school who does not convert.

The text

The discourse in full. Translation: Bhikkhu Sujato (CC0, SuttaCentral).

Two questions

§1At one time the Buddha was staying near Sāvatthī in Jeta’s Grove, Anāthapiṇḍika’s monastery. Then the wanderer Channa went up to Venerable Ānanda and exchanged greetings with him. When the greetings and polite conversation were over, he sat down to one side and said to Ānanda: “Reverend Ānanda, do you advocate giving up greed, hate, and delusion?” “We do, reverend.”

§2“But what drawbacks have you seen, Reverend Ānanda, that you advocate giving up greed, hate, and delusion?”

What has been seen

§3“A greedy person, overcome by greed, intends to hurt themselves, hurt others, and hurt both. They experience mental pain and sadness. When greed has been given up, they don’t intend to hurt themselves, hurt others, and hurt both. They don’t experience mental pain and sadness. A greedy person does bad things by way of body, speech, and mind. When greed has been given up, they don’t do bad things by way of body, speech, and mind. A greedy person doesn’t truly understand what’s for their own good, the good of another, or the good of both. When greed has been given up, they truly understand what’s for their own good, the good of another, or the good of both. Greed is a destroyer of sight, vision, and knowledge. It blocks wisdom, it’s on the side of distress, and it doesn’t lead to extinguishment.

§4A hateful person, overcome by hate, intends to hurt themselves, hurt others, and hurt both. … A deluded person, overcome by delusion, intends to hurt themselves, hurt others, and hurt both. They experience mental pain and sadness. When delusion has been given up, they don’t intend to hurt themselves, hurt others, and hurt both. They don’t experience mental pain and sadness. A deluded person does bad things by way of body, speech, and mind. When delusion has been given up, they don’t do bad things by way of body, speech, and mind. A deluded person doesn’t truly understand what’s for their own good, the good of another, or the good of both. When delusion has been given up, they truly understand what’s for their own good, the good of another, or the good of both. Delusion is a destroyer of sight, vision, and knowledge; it blocks wisdom, it’s on the side of distress, and it doesn’t lead to extinguishment. This is the drawback we’ve seen in greed, hate, and delusion, and this is why we advocate giving them up.”

And what is to be done

§5“But, reverend, is there a path and a practice for giving up that greed, hate, and delusion?” “There is, reverend, a path and a practice for giving up that greed, hate, and delusion.” “Well, what is it?” “It is simply this noble eightfold path, that is: right view, right purpose, right speech, right action, right livelihood, right effort, right mindfulness, and right immersion. This is the path, this is the practice for giving up that greed, hate, and delusion.” “This is a fine path, a fine practice, for giving up that greed, hate, and delusion. Just this much is enough for diligence.”
