Aṅguttara Nikāya · Discourse 4.184

Fearless

Abhayasutta — Brāhmaṇavagga — the nineteenth chapter of the Fours

Setting
None stated; the Brāhmaṇavagga gives no location for this discourse, and it is addressed to the mendicants directly
Speakers
The brahmin Jānussoṇi and the Buddha
Form
A universal claim refuted, then four causes of fear and their four mirrored absences
Length
~4 minutes to read
Northern parallel
The four causes of fear of death recur across the early collections' treatments of a good death; this reading guide does not assert a specific matching sutra number
Difficulty
★★☆☆☆ — plainly built, and the fourth cause is worth noticing for how it differs from the other three

Why this discourse

Jānussoṇi states a universal claim: everyone liable to death is frightened and terrified of it. The Buddha corrects the universal to a conditional — some are, some aren’t — and gives four things that produce the fear, each mirrored by its absence. Three of the four are about attachment. The fourth is about something else entirely: uncertainty about the teaching itself.

Reading guide

The teaching in one sentence

Fear of death is not universal; it has four specific causes, and each is removable independently of the others.

A universal claim, corrected to a conditional

Jānussoṇi’s doctrine is stated as flatly as Vassakāra’s was three discourses earlier: all those liable to death are frightened and terrified of death. The Buddha’s correction is equally flat and immediate: some… are… but some… are not.

This is a different kind of correction from the one given to Vassakāra. There the Buddha relocated the question to a new axis; here he simply denies the universal quantifier and replaces it with a distribution, then spends the rest of the discourse specifying exactly what the distribution depends on.

Three causes built from attachment

The first three causes of fear share an identical structure: someone not free of greed, desire, fondness, thirst, passion, and craving for something; when seriously ill, they think that thing will leave them and they will leave it; they sorrow, wail, lament, beat their breast, and fall into confusion.

The three objects are sensual pleasures, the body, and one’s own record of past conduct — the third framed not as attachment exactly but as its consequence: someone who has done bad things and not the good things that would keep them safe, facing the thought of where such conduct leads.

Each of the three gets the mirrored positive case: someone rid of that particular craving, or free of that particular guilt, does not have the thought, does not sorrow, does not fall into confusion. The structure is symmetrical and the discourse trusts the reader to see the pattern without further comment after the first pair.

A fourth cause of a different kind

The fourth cause breaks the pattern of the first three. It is not attachment to anything, and it is not a consequence of past conduct. It is kaṅkhī vicikicchī aniṭṭhaṅgato saddhamme — doubtful, uncertain, and undecided about the true teaching.

Someone in this condition, falling seriously ill, thinks simply: I’m doubtful, uncertain, and undecided about the true teaching — and that thought alone produces the same sorrow, wailing, and confusion the other three causes produce. No craving is named, and no bad deed is named. The fear here is generated by an unresolved epistemic state meeting a moment that demands certainty.

This is worth sitting with, because it says something the other three causes do not: fear of death is not only about what a person wants to keep or what they are afraid they deserve. It can also be about not having settled, before the moment arrives, what one actually holds to be true. The mirrored positive case is correspondingly simple — someone not doubtful, not uncertain, decided about the teaching — with no further condition attached.

What the discourse does not resolve

The discourse does not say what makes the fourth condition resolve into confidence, nor does it prescribe a practice for reaching that resolution here. It states the condition and its absence and moves on, in the same register as the other three causes.

That restraint is consistent with how this collection typically handles a list: the classification is given, and the means of moving from one side of it to the other is supplied, if at all, elsewhere. A reader wanting to know how doubt about the teaching is actually resolved will need to look beyond this discourse.

Jānussoṇi’s conversion

The discourse ends where several others involving brahmins in this chapter do not quite reach: Jānussoṇi does not merely approve and agree. He asks to be remembered as a lay follower who has gone for refuge for life, from this day forth.

That is a formal step beyond the polite assent Vassakāra offers at the end of AN 4.183 and AN 4.187. Whether the discourse means to present the correction of a universal claim to a conditional one as itself the persuasive move, or whether it is simply recording what happened without claiming the argument alone caused it, is not stated and this reading guide does not resolve it either way.

Key terms

Jānussoṇi — a brahmin who appears elsewhere in the canon as an interlocutor of the Buddha; here the one whose universal claim about fear of death is corrected.
kāmesu avītarāgo — “not free of greed… for sensual pleasures” — the first of the three attachment-based causes of fear.
kaṅkhī vicikicchī aniṭṭhaṅgato saddhamme — “doubtful, uncertain, and undecided about the true teaching” — the fourth cause, unlike the first three in requiring no craving or bad deed.
saddhamma — “the true teaching” — the object of the fourth cause’s doubt, and the same word used in AN 4.160 for what can decline or endure.
upāsaka — “lay follower” — what Jānussoṇi asks to be remembered as, going for refuge for life at the discourse’s close.

The text

The discourse in full: the universal claim corrected, four causes of fear, their four mirrored absences, and Jānussoṇi's refuge. Translation: Bhikkhu Sujato (CC0, SuttaCentral).

Jānussoṇi’s claim

§1Then the brahmin Jānussoṇi went up to the Buddha, and exchanged greetings with him. When the greetings and polite conversation were over, he sat down to one side and said to the Buddha: “Worthy Gotama, this is my doctrine and view: ‘All those liable to death are frightened and terrified of death.’” “Brahmin, some of those liable to death are frightened and terrified of death. But some of those liable to death are not frightened and terrified of death.

Four who are frightened

§2Who are those frightened of death? It’s someone who isn’t free of greed, desire, fondness, thirst, passion, and craving for sensual pleasures. When they fall seriously ill, they think: ‘The sensual pleasures that I love so much will leave me, and I’ll leave them.’ They sorrow and wail and lament, beating their breast and falling into confusion. This is someone who is frightened of death. Furthermore, it’s someone who isn’t free of greed, desire, fondness, thirst, passion, and craving for the body. When they fall seriously ill, they think: ‘This body that I love so much will leave me, and I’ll leave it.’ They sorrow and wail and lament, beating their breast and falling into confusion. This, too, is someone who is frightened of death. Furthermore, it’s someone who hasn’t done good and skillful things that keep them safe, but has done bad things, violence and sin. When they fall seriously ill, they think: ‘Well, I haven’t done good and skillful things that keep me safe. And I have done bad things, violence and sin. When I depart, I’ll go to the place where people who’ve done such things go.’ They sorrow and wail and lament, beating their breast and falling into confusion. This, too, is someone who is frightened of death. Furthermore, it’s someone who’s doubtful, uncertain, and undecided about the true teaching. When they fall seriously ill, they think: ‘I’m doubtful, uncertain, and undecided about the true teaching.’ They sorrow and wail and lament, beating their breast and falling into confusion. This, too, is someone who is frightened of death. These are the four individuals liable to death who are frightened and terrified of death.

Four who are not frightened

§3Who are those not frightened of death? It’s someone who is rid of greed, desire, fondness, thirst, passion, and craving for sensual pleasures. When they fall seriously ill, they don’t think: ‘The sensual pleasures that I love so much will leave me, and I’ll leave them.’ They don’t sorrow and wail and lament, beating their breast and falling into confusion. This is someone who’s not frightened of death. Furthermore, it’s someone who is rid of greed, desire, fondness, thirst, passion, and craving for the body. When they fall seriously ill, they don’t think: ‘This body that I love so much will leave me, and I’ll leave it.’ They don’t sorrow and wail and lament, beating their breast and falling into confusion. This, too, is someone who’s not frightened of death. Furthermore, it’s someone who hasn’t done bad things, violence and sin, but has done good and skillful deeds that keep them safe. When they fall seriously ill, they think: ‘Well, I haven’t done bad things, violence and sin. And I have done good and skillful deeds that keep me safe. When I depart, I’ll go to the place where people who’ve done such things go.’ They don’t sorrow and wail and lament, beating their breast and falling into confusion. This, too, is someone who’s not frightened of death. Furthermore, it’s someone who’s not doubtful, uncertain, or undecided about the true teaching. When they fall seriously ill, they think: ‘I’m not doubtful, uncertain, or undecided about the true teaching.’ They don’t sorrow and wail and lament, beating their breast and falling into confusion. This, too, is someone who’s not frightened of death. These are the four individuals liable to death who are not frightened and terrified of death.”

Jānussoṇi takes refuge

§4“Excellent, worthy Gotama! … From this day forth, may the worthy Gotama remember me as a lay follower who has gone for refuge for life.”
