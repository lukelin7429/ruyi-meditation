Aṅguttara Nikāya · Discourse 4.259

A Thoroughbred (1st)

Paṭhamaājānīyasutta — Abhiññāvagga — the twenty-sixth chapter of the Fours

Setting
None stated; the Abhiññāvagga gives no location for this discourse, and it is addressed to the mendicants directly
Speakers
The Buddha alone, addressing the mendicants
Form
A simile stated first for a horse, then mapped point by point onto a mendicant
Length
~2 minutes to read
Northern parallel
The thoroughbred simile for an excellent mendicant is a recurring image across the Nikāyas; this reading guide does not assert a specific matching sutra number
Difficulty
★★☆☆☆ — a vivid simile, with one mapping worth reading closely for its looseness

Why this discourse

A fine royal thoroughbred has four qualities that make it fit to serve a king: beauty, strength, speed, and good proportion. This discourse maps all four, one at a time, onto a mendicant worthy of the highest offerings — and while the first three mappings are precise and satisfying, the fourth is looser than the simile leads a reader to expect.

Reading guide

The teaching in one sentence

A mendicant who is ethical, energetic, wise in the four noble truths, and provided with the requisites of monastic life is, like a fine royal thoroughbred, worthy of the highest offerings.

Beautiful: ethical restraint

A thoroughbred’s beauty, vaṇṇasampanno, becomes a mendicant’s ethical conduct: restrained by the monastic code, behaving well, resorting for alms in suitable places, and seeing danger in the slightest fault. The mapping works because both are, in their own registers, what makes something presentable and fit to be seen — a horse’s coat and bearing, a monastic’s visible conduct.

Strong and fast: energy and insight

Strength, balasampanno, becomes sustained energy for abandoning what is unskillful and developing what is skillful — a direct, intuitive mapping. Speed, javasampanno, becomes understanding the four noble truths: suffering, its origin, its cessation, and the practice leading to its cessation. This is a more surprising mapping at first — wisdom as speed — but it holds up: understanding that arrives at the truths directly, without wandering, is exactly what ‘fast’ would mean applied to insight rather than motion.

Well-proportioned: a looser fit

A thoroughbred’s good proportion, ārohapariṇāhasampanno, literally ‘endowed with height and girth’, describes a well-built animal capable of carrying its rider’s weight. Mapped onto the mendicant, this becomes simply receiving robes, almsfood, lodgings, and medicine — not a quality of the mendicant’s own body or character at all, but the material support they receive from others.

This is worth flagging honestly as the simile’s weakest joint. The first three mappings translate an inner or bodily quality of the horse into an inner quality of the mendicant; the fourth switches registers, translating the horse’s physical build into something the mendicant passively receives. Read generously, the logic may be that a well-proportioned animal is naturally well provided for by its owner, so a mendicant who is genuinely worthy attracts the requisites needed to sustain the practice — but the text does not spell this reasoning out, and the fit is looser than the first three.

What the simile is ultimately claiming

The discourse’s closing line is unambiguous about the stakes: such a mendicant is the supreme field of merit for the world, worthy of offerings to the gods, of hospitality, of religious donation, and of veneration. The horse simile is not decoration; it is doing real argumentative work, making the case that spiritual excellence, like equine excellence, is a coherent, recognizable, and valuable thing — even where one of its four features fits the comparison less cleanly than the rest.

Key terms

assājānīya — “a thoroughbred, a horse of good breeding” — the animal supplying this discourse’s central simile.
vaṇṇasampanno — “endowed with beauty” — mapped onto ethical restraint and seeing danger in the slightest fault.
javasampanno — “endowed with speed” — mapped, in this discourse, onto understanding the four noble truths.
ārohapariṇāhasampanno — “endowed with height and girth, well-proportioned” — mapped, more loosely, onto simply receiving the four monastic requisites.
puññakkhetta — “field of merit” — what this discourse's fourfold mendicant is called, the supreme instance of it for the world.

The text

The discourse in full: the thoroughbred's four qualities, then each mapped onto a mendicant. Translation: Bhikkhu Sujato (CC0, SuttaCentral).

The thoroughbred

§1“Mendicants, a fine royal thoroughbred with four factors is worthy of a king, fit to serve a king, and reckoned a factor of kingship. What four? It’s when a fine royal thoroughbred is beautiful, strong, fast, and well-proportioned. A fine royal thoroughbred with these four factors is worthy of a king. …

The mendicant, in the same terms

§2In the same way, a mendicant with four qualities is worthy of offerings dedicated to the gods, worthy of hospitality, worthy of a religious donation, worthy of veneration with cupped palms, and is the supreme field of merit for the world. What four? It’s when a mendicant is beautiful, strong, fast, and well proportioned.

Beautiful

§3And how is a mendicant beautiful? It’s when a mendicant is ethical, restrained in the monastic code, conducting themselves well and resorting for alms in suitable places. Seeing danger in the slightest fault, they keep the rules they’ve undertaken. That’s how a mendicant is beautiful.

Strong

§4And how is a mendicant strong? It’s when a mendicant lives with energy roused up for giving up unskillful qualities and embracing skillful qualities. They are strong, staunchly vigorous, not slacking off when it comes to developing skillful qualities. That’s how a mendicant is strong.

Fast

§5And how is a mendicant fast? It’s when they truly understand: ‘This is suffering’ … ‘This is the origin of suffering’ … ‘This is the cessation of suffering’ … ‘This is the practice that leads to the cessation of suffering’. That’s how a mendicant is fast.

Well proportioned

§6And how is a mendicant well proportioned? It’s when a mendicant receives robes, almsfood, lodgings, and medicines and supplies for the sick. That’s how a mendicant is well proportioned. A mendicant with these four qualities … is the supreme field of merit for the world.”
