Aṅguttara Nikāya · Discourse 4.1

Understood

Anubuddhasutta — Bhaṇḍagāmavagga — the first chapter of the Fours

Setting
Bhaṇḍagāma, ‘Wares Village’, in the land of the Vajjis; stated at the head of AN 4.1 and understood to hold across the chapter
Speakers
The Buddha alone, addressing the mendicants
Form
A statement of what was not understood, the list of four, a declaration that it is now understood, and two closing verses
Length
~2 minutes to read
Northern parallel
The four-item set of ethics, immersion, wisdom, and freedom is widespread in the Chinese Āgamas, and this material is close to the Mahāparinibbāna cycle; this reading guide does not assert a specific matching sutra number
Difficulty
★★☆☆☆ — short, but the first-person admission in it repays slow reading

Why this discourse

The Fours open with a list that will run through the whole collection — noble ethics, immersion, wisdom, and freedom — and with a sentence that is easy to read past. Both you and I have wandered and transmigrated for such a very long time because these four were not understood. The Buddha places himself inside the failure he is describing. Whatever else awakening is on this account, it is not a difference of kind between the teacher and the people he is addressing; it is the same four things, understood.

Reading guide

The teaching in one sentence

Four things — ethics, immersion, wisdom, freedom — are what nobody had understood, and understanding them is the whole difference between wandering on and being finished.

The four, and why they are in this order

Sīla, samādhi, paññā, vimutti: ethical conduct, unification of mind, wisdom, and freedom. The first three are the standard threefold training, and the order is not decorative. Conduct settles the mind, a settled mind can see, and what it sees releases it.

The fourth item is what makes the list belong to the Fours rather than the Threes. Freedom is not a fourth practice alongside the other three; it is what the other three arrive at. The set says that the training has a terminus and names it, which is why the same four recur throughout the collection whenever the path is summarized at speed.

Note the qualifier attached to the first: noble ethics, ariya sīla. Not conduct in general, but the conduct of one on the path. The adjective governs all four items and quietly rules out reading the list as a description of ordinary virtue.

“Both you and I”

The discourse says that not understanding these four is why both you and I have wandered so long. The Pāli puts the teacher and the audience in the same sentence and the same predicament.

This matters for how the rest of the collection should be heard. A great deal of the Aṅguttara consists of the Buddha sorting people into kinds — fools and astute people, those who go with the stream and those who go against it. That sorting could be read as a permanent hierarchy. The opening discourse of the Fours forecloses that reading before it can start: the difference between the teacher and the listener is something that happened, not something that was always so.

What is claimed at the end

The second half of the key paragraph is a declaration, and it is worth reading as one: these four have been understood and comprehended; craving for continued existence has been cut off; the leash to existence is ended; there will be no more future lives.

Four statements, all in the perfect. The discourse does not argue for this or offer evidence; it states it, and then a verse repeats it in the third person. Read honestly, AN 4.1 is a claim on the listener’s confidence at the very moment it has invited them to see themselves as the teacher’s equal in the long past. Both moves are doing work, and they pull in different directions on purpose.

The leash

Bhavanetti — Sujato’s “leash to existence” — is one of the collection’s better images. Netti is a guide-rope, the cord by which an animal is led. What holds a being in the round is not a wall but a tether, and the animal walks after it.

The image explains why the discourse can pair “craving for continued existence has been cut off” with “there will be no more future lives” as if they were one thing said twice. On this picture they are. Cut the rope and nothing else has to be done; the walking simply stops.

Where the chapter goes from here

AN 4.2 takes the same four and states the consequence of lacking them: one has fallen from this teaching and training. AN 4.3 and 4.4 turn to the untrue person and the damage they do to themselves. The chapter is arranged so that the definitive list arrives first and the human material follows.

It is worth reading AN 4.1 and 4.2 together at a single sitting. They share a list, a verse form, and a single argument split across two discourses: here is what has to be understood, and here is what it means to be without it.

Key terms

anubuddha — “understood, awakened to” — the participle the discourse is named for. It is used of the four items, not of a person: what is awakened to.
ariyaṁ sīlaṁ — “noble ethics” — the first of the four, with the adjective that governs the whole list and marks it as the conduct of one on the path.
samādhi — “immersion, unification of mind” — the second, and the standard middle term of the threefold training.
vimutti — “freedom, release” — the fourth, and the item that makes this a set of four. Not a practice alongside the others but what they arrive at.
bhavanetti — “the leash to existence” — netti is a guide-rope. What holds a being in the round is a tether, and cutting it is the whole of what is needed.

The text

The discourse in full: the setting at Wares Village, the four things not understood, the declaration that they now are, and the closing verses. Translation: Bhikkhu Sujato (CC0, SuttaCentral).

At Wares Village

§1So I have heard. At one time the Buddha was staying in the land of the Vajjis at Wares Village. There the Buddha addressed the mendicants, “Mendicants!” “Venerable sir,” they replied. The Buddha said this:

Four things not understood

§2“Mendicants, due to not understanding and not penetrating four things, both you and I have wandered and transmigrated for such a very long time. What four? Noble ethics, immersion, wisdom, and freedom. These noble ethics, immersion, wisdom, and freedom have been understood and comprehended. Craving for continued existence has been cut off; the leash to existence is ended; now there’ll be no more future lives.”

The verses

§3That is what the Buddha said. Then the Holy One, the Teacher, went on to say:

§4“Ethics, immersion, and wisdom, and the supreme freedom: these things have been understood by Gotama the renowned. And so the Buddha, having insight, explained this teaching to the mendicants. The teacher made an end of suffering, seeing clearly, he is fully quenched.”
