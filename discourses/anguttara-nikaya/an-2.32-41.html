Aṅguttara Nikāya · Discourses 2.32–41

The Peaceful Mind

Samacittavagga — the fourth chapter of the Twos

Setting
Various — Sāvatthī, the Eastern Monastery, Varaṇā by the Kaddama Lake, and Madhurā in the Nut Grass Grove
Speakers
The Buddha; Venerable Sāriputta at AN 2.36; Venerable Mahākaccāna at AN 2.37–38, each answering a brahmin visitor
Form
Ten discourses, several of them full narrative scenes with named interlocutors — the most conversational chapter in the first two nipātas
Length
~9 minutes to read
Northern parallel
The passage on repaying parents has a close independent counterpart in the Sūtra on the Difficulty of Repaying Parents’ Kindness (Taishō T684, An Shigao’s 2nd-century Chinese translation) — nearly the same argument, nearly the same shape, preserved separately for well over a millennium before either was fixed in writing. Mahākaccāna’s exchanges with brahmins are also represented more broadly across the Chinese Āgamas; this reading guide does not assert a specific matching sutra number for those
Difficulty
★★☆☆☆ — narrative and readable throughout; AN 2.36 assumes some familiarity with the stages of awakening

Why this discourse

This is the chapter where the Twos stop being a list and start being a book. Half of it is narrative: Anāthapiṇḍika asking where to give, Sāriputta teaching in the Eastern Monastery while the Buddha appears in front of him, Mahākaccāna explaining to one brahmin why people fight and to another why an eighty-year-old may be a child. And it contains AN 2.33, on what it would take to repay one’s parents — a passage that would matter more in China than almost anything else in the Aṅguttara, because it answers, in the Buddha’s own words, the oldest objection Chinese society ever made to Buddhism.

Reading guide

The teaching in one sentence

Gratitude marks a good person; parents cannot be repaid by care alone; householders quarrel over what they want and ascetics over what they think; and an elder is made by conduct, not by years.

Gratitude, and then parents (AN 2.32–33)

AN 2.32 defines the untrue person and the true person by a single quality: being ungrateful and thankless, or grateful and thankful. Two Pāli words are in play, kataññū — knowing what was done for one — and katavedī, acknowledging it. Recognition and its expression, treated as one virtue.

AN 2.33 then applies it at maximum scale. Two people cannot easily be repaid: mother and father. The illustration is deliberately extreme and physically undignified — carry your mother on one shoulder and your father on the other for a hundred years; anoint, massage, bathe, and rub them; and let them defecate and urinate right there on you. Even that is not enough. Even installing them as emperors of the whole earth is not enough. The reason given is that parents raise their children, nurture them, and show them the world.

And then the turn, which is the whole point of the discourse: but you have done enough, more than enough, to repay them if you encourage, settle, and ground unfaithful parents in faith, unethical parents in ethical conduct, stingy parents in generosity, or ignorant parents in wisdom. What cannot be discharged by any amount of physical care is discharged by establishing them in these four.

Why this passage matters so much in Chinese

The standard Confucian objection to Buddhism, made from the Han dynasty onward and never fully retired, is that it is unfilial: it takes sons out of the household, ends the line of descendants, and cuts the hair and beard that were received from one’s parents. That charge did real damage and Chinese Buddhists spent centuries answering it.

AN 2.33 is one of the answers, and it is a strong one because it is early, canonical, and does not diminish the debt in order to escape it. The discourse agrees that the debt is effectively infinite and that ordinary care cannot discharge it. What it says is that there is one thing that can, and that the thing is spiritual rather than material. On that reading a person who leads their parents to faith and ethics has performed the highest filial act available, not abandoned filiality.

The Chinese tradition took exactly this line and pressed it much further. The Brahmā Net Sūtra, on this site, opens its bodhisattva precepts by making filial devotion to parents, teachers, and the Three Jewels the first content of the precepts and then declaring that filial devotion is itself the dharma of the highest way — 孝名為戒, filiality is called precept. The Contemplation Sūtra’s three blessings open with caring for one’s parents before anything about meditation or vows. Both are linked below, and reading them beside AN 2.33 shows a single early discourse becoming the hinge of an entire cultural argument.

Action and inaction (AN 2.34)

A brahmin asks what Gotama teaches, and gets a two-word answer that sounds like a paradox: action and inaction, kiriya and akiriya. The explanation is immediate and deflating — inaction regarding bad conduct, action regarding good conduct.

The joke, if it is one, has an edge. Akiriyavāda, the doctrine of non-action, was a recognized position among rival teachers of the period: the claim that deeds have no moral consequence, which AN 3.61 attacks at length. By answering “I teach action and inaction” the Buddha momentarily sounds like he is claiming that label, and then empties it of the doctrine and refills it with ethics. It is the kind of exchange that only works if both parties know the technical vocabulary.

Sāriputta, and the deities on a needle (AN 2.36)

The longest discourse in the chapter, and it does two things. First, Sāriputta teaches the difference between someone fettered internally — ethical, reborn among the gods, and then a returner who comes back — and someone fettered externally, who reaches a peaceful state of freed mind and does not come back.

Then the frame becomes the point. Deities of peaceful mind come to the Buddha and ask him to go and hear Sāriputta, and he does, appearing in front of him. And he adds a detail: these deities are so refined that ten, twenty, sixty of them can stand on the point of a needle without crowding — and then heads off the obvious inference. You might think they must have developed their minds in that place. You should not see it like this. It was right here that those deities developed their minds.

That is the sentence to take away. Whatever refinement those beings have, they acquired it in an ordinary human life, in a place like this one. The heavens are where the result is enjoyed, not where the work is done. It is a quietly deflationary point in the middle of an otherwise very celestial scene, and it is the reason the discourse ends with a training instruction addressed to people on the ground: we shall have peaceful faculties and peaceful minds… we shall present the gift of peace to our spiritual companions.

Mahākaccāna answers two brahmins (AN 2.37–38)

The first exchange is one of the sharpest diagnoses in the canon. Why do aristocrats fight aristocrats, brahmins fight brahmins, householders fight householders? Insistence on sensual desire. Why do ascetics fight ascetics? Insistence on views. Two populations, two attachments, the same mechanism — and the religious one is not exempt, it has simply swapped the object. Anyone who has watched a doctrinal argument in a spiritual community will recognize the accuracy of it.

The second exchange is about age. A brahmin complains that Kaccāna does not bow to elderly brahmins or rise for them. Kaccāna replies with the Buddha’s distinction: an eighty-, ninety-, or hundred-year-old still consumed by sensual pleasures is reckoned a child, not a senior; a young person with pristine black hair who is not so consumed is reckoned astute, a senior. The brahmin then bows to the young monastics and says the good fellows are the elders and we are the youths.

It is worth noticing that Kaccāna does not deny the social norm or claim exemption from it. He redefines who falls under it. That is a more interesting move than refusal, and it is the one a teacher can actually use with students who assume that respect and seniority must either be automatic or abolished.

When bad monastics are strong (AN 2.39–41)

The chapter closes on institutional health. The simile is political: when bandits are strong, kings are weak, and nobody travels comfortably. In the same way, when bad mendicants are strong, good-hearted ones fall silent in the assembly or move to the borderlands.

That detail is worth sitting with, because it is a precise observation rather than a complaint. The failure mode named is not that good people are driven out but that they stop speaking — they adhere to silence in the midst of the Saṅgha. An institution in which the decent have gone quiet looks, from outside, exactly like one that is fine.

AN 2.40 then refuses to let either party off: wrong practice is not praised in laypeople or renunciates, and right practice is praised in both. And AN 2.41 returns to the collection’s standing worry about transmission, with a new specific: memorizing the discourses incorrectly, taking only a semblance of the phrasing. Not malice, not invention. Getting the wording roughly right, and thereby shutting out the meaning.

Key terms

kataññū katavedī — “grateful and thankful” — knowing what was done for one, and acknowledging it. Recognition and expression treated as a single virtue, and the mark of a true person.
kiriya / akiriya — “action” and “inaction” — also the name of a rival doctrine holding that deeds have no moral consequence, which is what makes AN 2.34’s answer a deliberate borrowing of a loaded term.
samacitta — “peaceful mind” — the epithet of the deities in AN 2.36 and the chapter’s Pāli name; the training instruction it closes on is to have peaceful faculties and a peaceful mind.
kāmarāgābhinivesa / diṭṭhābhinivesa — “insistence on sensual desire” and “insistence on views” — Mahākaccāna’s two diagnoses in AN 2.37, one for householders and one for ascetics.
thera / dahara — “elder” and “youth” — which AN 2.38 reassigns from years to conduct without abolishing the categories.

The text

All ten discourses of the chapter, in order. Each §N below is one complete discourse — §32 is AN 2.32, §41 is AN 2.41 — and several carry their own settings and speakers. Translation: Bhikkhu Sujato (CC0, SuttaCentral).

Gratitude, and parents (AN 2.32–33)

§32“Mendicants, I will teach you the level of the untrue person and the level of the true person. Listen and apply your mind well, I will speak.” “Yes, sir,” they replied. The Buddha said this: “What is the level of the untrue person? The untrue person is ungrateful and thankless, for the wicked only know how to be ungrateful and thankless. It is totally the level of an untrue person to be ungrateful and thankless. The true person is grateful and thankful, for the virtuous only know how to be grateful and thankful. It is totally the level of a true person to be grateful and thankful.”

§33“Mendicants, I say that these two individuals cannot easily be repaid. What two? Mother and father. You would not have done enough to repay your mother and father even if you were to carry your mother around on one shoulder and your father on the other, and if you lived like this for a hundred years, and if you were to anoint, massage, bathe, and rub them; and even if they were to defecate and urinate right there. Even if you were to establish your mother and father as supreme monarchs of this great earth, abounding in the seven treasures, you would still not have done enough to repay them. Why is that? Parents are very helpful to their children: they raise them, nurture them, and show them the world. But you have done enough, more than enough, to repay them if you encourage, settle, and ground unfaithful parents in faith, unethical parents in ethical conduct, stingy parents in generosity, or ignorant parents in wisdom.”

Two visitors (AN 2.34–35)

§34Then a certain brahmin went up to the Buddha, and exchanged greetings with him. When the greetings and polite conversation were over, he sat down to one side and said to the Buddha, “What does the worthy Gotama teach? What does he explain?” “Brahmin, I teach action and inaction.” “But in what way does the worthy Gotama teach action and inaction?” “I teach inaction regarding bad bodily, verbal, and mental conduct, and the many kinds of unskillful things. I teach action regarding good bodily, verbal, and mental conduct, and the many kinds of skillful things. This is the kind of action and inaction that I teach.” “Excellent, worthy Gotama! … From this day forth, may the worthy Gotama remember me as a lay follower who has gone for refuge for life.”

§35Then the householder Anāthapiṇḍika went up to the Buddha, bowed, sat down to one side, and said to the Buddha, “How many in the world are worthy of a religious donation? And where should a gift be given?” “Householder, there are two in the world who are worthy of a religious donation: the trainee and the adept. These two in the world are worthy of a religious donation, and that’s where you should give a gift.” That is what the Buddha said. Then the Holy One, the Teacher, went on to say: “In this world, the trainee and the adept, are worthy of offerings dedicated to the gods. They are sincere in body, speech, and mind. This is the field for sponsors of sacrifice—what’s given here is very fruitful.”

Sāriputta in the Eastern Monastery (AN 2.36)

§36So I have heard. At one time the Buddha was staying near Sāvatthī in Jeta’s Grove, Anāthapiṇḍika’s monastery. Now at that time Venerable Sāriputta was staying near Sāvatthī in the stilt longhouse of Migāra’s mother in the Eastern Monastery. There Sāriputta addressed the mendicants: “Reverends, mendicants!” “Reverend,” they replied. Sāriputta said this: “I will teach you about an individual fettered internally and one fettered externally. Listen and apply your mind well, I will speak.” “Yes, reverend,” they replied. Sāriputta said this: “Who is an individual fettered internally? It’s a mendicant who is ethical, restrained in the monastic code, conducting themselves well and resorting for alms in suitable places. Seeing danger in the slightest fault, they keep the rules they’ve undertaken. When their body breaks up, after death, they’re reborn in one of the orders of gods. When they pass away from there, they’re a returner, who comes back to this place. This is called an individual who is fettered internally, a returner, who comes back to this place. Who is an individual fettered externally? It’s a mendicant who is ethical, restrained in the monastic code, conducting themselves well and resorting for alms in suitable places. Seeing danger in the slightest fault, they keep the rules they’ve undertaken. They enter and remain in a certain peaceful state of freed mind. When their body breaks up, after death, they’re reborn in one of the orders of gods. When they pass away from there, they’re a non-returner, not coming back to this place. This is called an individual who is fettered externally, a non-returner, who does not come back to this place. Furthermore, a mendicant is ethical … they keep the rules they’ve undertaken. They simply practice for disillusionment, dispassion, and cessation regarding sensual pleasures. They simply practice for disillusionment, dispassion, and cessation regarding future lives. They practice for the ending of craving. They practice for the ending of greed. When their body breaks up, after death, they are reborn in one of the orders of gods. When they pass away from there, they are non-returners, not coming back to this place. This is called an individual who is fettered externally, a non-returner, who does not come back to this place.” Then several peaceful-minded deities went up to the Buddha, bowed, stood to one side, and said to the Buddha, “Sir, Venerable Sāriputta is in the stilt longhouse of Migāra’s mother in the Eastern Monastery, where he is teaching the mendicants about an individual with internal fetters and one with external fetters. The assembly is overjoyed! Sir, please go to Venerable Sāriputta out of sympathy.” The Buddha consented with silence. Then the Buddha, as easily as a strong person would extend or contract their arm, vanished from Jeta’s Grove and reappeared in the stilt longhouse of Migāra’s mother in the Eastern Monastery, in front of Sāriputta. He sat on the seat spread out. Sāriputta bowed to the Buddha and sat down to one side. The Buddha said to him: “Just now, Sāriputta, several peaceful-minded deities came up to me, bowed, and stood to one side. Those deities said to me: ‘Sir, Venerable Sāriputta is in the stilt longhouse of Migāra’s mother in the Eastern Monastery, where he is teaching the mendicants about an individual with internal fetters and one with external fetters. The assembly is overjoyed! Sir, please go to Venerable Sāriputta out of sympathy.’ Those deities, though they number ten, twenty, thirty, forty, fifty, or sixty, can stand on the point of a needle without bumping up against each other. Sāriputta, you might think: ‘Surely those deities, since so many of them can stand on the point of a needle without bumping up against each other, must have developed their minds in that place.’ But you should not see it like this. It was right here that those deities developed their minds. So you should train like this: ‘We shall have peaceful faculties and peaceful minds.’ That’s how you should train. When your faculties and mind are peaceful, your acts of body, speech, and mind will be peaceful, thinking: ‘We shall present the gift of peace to our spiritual companions.’ That’s how you should train. Those wanderers of other religions, Sāriputta, who have not heard this exposition of the teaching are lost.”

Mahākaccāna answers two brahmins (AN 2.37–38)

§37So I have heard. At one time Venerable Mahākaccāna was staying at Varaṇā, on the bank of the Kaddama Lake. Then the brahmin Ārāmadaṇḍa went up to Mahākaccāna, and exchanged greetings with him. When the greetings and polite conversation were over, he sat down to one side and said to Mahākaccāna: “What is the cause, worthy Kaccāna, what is the reason why aristocrats fight with aristocrats, brahmins fight with brahmins, and householders fight with householders?” “It is because of their insistence on sensual desire, their shackles, avarice, and attachment, that aristocrats fight with aristocrats, brahmins fight with brahmins, and householders fight with householders.” “What is the cause, worthy Kaccāna, what is the reason why ascetics fight with ascetics?” “It is because of their insistence on views, their shackles, avarice, and attachment, that ascetics fight with ascetics.” “Worthy Kaccāna, is there anyone in the world who has gone beyond the insistence on sensual desire and the insistence on views?” “There is, brahmin.” “Who in the world has gone beyond the insistence on sensual desire and the insistence on views?” “In the eastern lands there is a city named Sāvatthī. There the Blessed One is now staying, the perfected one, the fully awakened Buddha. He, brahmin, has gone beyond the insistence on sensual desire and the insistence on views.” When this was said, the brahmin Ārāmadaṇḍa got up from his seat, arranged his robe over one shoulder, knelt on his right knee, raised his cupped palms toward the Buddha, and expressed this heartfelt sentiment three times: “Homage to him, the blessed one, the perfected one, the fully awakened Buddha! Homage to him, the blessed one, the perfected one, the fully awakened Buddha! Homage to him, the blessed one, the perfected one, the fully awakened Buddha! He who has gone beyond the insistence on sensual desire and the insistence on views. Excellent, worthy Kaccāna! Excellent! As if he were righting the overturned, or revealing the hidden, or pointing out the path to the lost, or lighting a lamp in the dark so people with clear eyes can see what’s there, the worthy Kaccāna has made the teaching clear in many ways. I go for refuge to the worthy Gotama, to the teaching, and to the mendicant Saṅgha. From this day forth, may the worthy Kaccāna remember me as a lay follower who has gone for refuge for life.”

§38At one time Venerable Mahākaccāna was staying near Madhurā, in the Nut Grass Grove. Then the brahmin Kandarāyana went up to Mahākaccāna, and exchanged greetings with him … He sat down to one side and said to Mahākaccāna: “I have heard, worthy Kaccāna, that the ascetic Kaccāna doesn’t bow to old brahmins, the elderly and senior, who are advanced in years and have reached the final stage of life; nor does he rise in their presence or offer them a seat. And this is indeed the case, for the ascetic Kaccāna does not bow to old brahmins, elderly and senior, who are advanced in years and have reached the final stage of life; nor does he rise in their presence or offer them a seat. This is not appropriate, worthy Kaccāna.” “There is the stage of an elder and the stage of youth as explained by the Blessed One, who knows and sees, the perfected one, the fully awakened Buddha. If an elder, though eighty, ninety, or a hundred years old, still dwells in the midst of sensual pleasures, enjoying them, consumed by thoughts of them, burning with fever for them, and eagerly seeking more, they are reckoned as a child, not a senior. If a youth, young, with pristine black hair, blessed with youth, in the prime of life, does not dwell in the midst of sensual pleasures, enjoying them, consumed by thoughts of them, burning with fever for them, and eagerly seeking more, they are reckoned as astute, a senior.” When this was said, the brahmin Kandarāyana got up from his seat, placed his robe over one shoulder, and bowed with his head at the feet of the young mendicants, saying, “The good fellows are elders, at the stage of the elder; we are youths, at the stage of youth. Excellent, worthy Kaccāna! … From this day forth, may the worthy Kaccāna remember me as a lay follower who has gone for refuge for life.”

Bandits, practice, and memorization (AN 2.39–41)

§39“At a time when bandits are strong, kings are weak. Then the king is not at ease when going out or coming back or when touring the provinces. The brahmins and householders, likewise, are not at ease when going out or coming back, or when inspecting their external business activities. In the same way, at a time when bad mendicants are strong, good-hearted mendicants are weak. Then the good-hearted mendicants continually adhere to silence in the midst of the Saṅgha, or they stay in the borderlands. This is for the detriment and suffering of the people, against the people, for the harm, detriment, and suffering of gods and humans. At a time when kings are strong, bandits are weak. Then the king is at ease when going out or coming back or when inspecting the provinces. The brahmins and householders, likewise, are at ease when going out or coming back, or when inspecting their external business activities. In the same way, at a time when good-hearted mendicants are strong, bad mendicants are weak. Then the bad mendicants continually adhere to silence in the midst of the Saṅgha, or they leave for some place or other. This is for the welfare and happiness of the people, for the people, for the benefit, welfare, and happiness of gods and humans.”

§40“Mendicants, I don’t praise wrong practice for these two, for laypeople and renunciates. Because of wrong practice, neither laypeople nor renunciates succeed in completing the system of the skillful teaching. I praise right practice for these two, for laypeople and renunciates. Because of right practice, both laypeople and renunciates succeed in completing the system of the skillful teaching.”

§41“Mendicants, by memorizing the discourses incorrectly, taking only a semblance of the phrasing, some mendicants shut out the meaning and the teaching. They act for the detriment and suffering of the people, against the people, for the harm, detriment, and suffering of gods and humans. They brim with much wickedness and make the true teaching disappear. But by memorizing the discourses well, not taking only a semblance of the phrasing, some mendicants conform to the meaning and the teaching. They act for the welfare and happiness of the people, for the people, for the benefit, welfare, and happiness of gods and humans. They brim with much merit and make the true teaching continue.”
