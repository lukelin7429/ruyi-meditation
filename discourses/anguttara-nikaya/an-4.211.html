Aṅguttara Nikāya · Discourse 4.211

Assembly

Parisāsutta — Parisāvagga — the twenty-second chapter of the Fours

Setting
None stated; the Parisāvagga gives no location for this discourse, and it is addressed to the mendicants directly
Speakers
The Buddha alone, addressing the mendicants
Form
Two parallel statements — four who corrupt an assembly, four who grace it — naming the same fourfold assembly in each
Length
~1 minute to read
Northern parallel
The fourfold assembly is standard across the Chinese Āgamas; this reading guide does not assert a specific matching sutra number
Difficulty
★☆☆☆☆ — very short, and mostly interesting for what it reuses

Why this discourse

A new chapter opens, and its first move is not a new idea but an old one redeployed. The fourfold assembly — monk, nun, layman, laywoman — was set gracing the Saṅgha back at AN 4.7, on five social qualities. Here it is set again, this time on a single criterion, ethics, described in the exact two-word vocabulary — pāpadhamma, kalyāṇadhamma — that the previous chapter spent its last four discourses establishing. The chapter break does not reset the collection’s working vocabulary; it carries it over.

Reading guide

The teaching in one sentence

Four kinds of person — monk, nun, layman, laywoman — corrupt an assembly when unethical and of bad character, and grace it when ethical and of good character.

The fourfold assembly, a second time

Bhikkhu, bhikkhunī, upāsaka, upāsikā was set out at AN 4.7 as the four who grace the Saṅgha, qualified there by five social virtues: competence, training, self-assurance, learning, and memorized teaching. This discourse returns to the same cast for a narrower question — not who graces the community in general, but who corrupts or graces a particular gathering, a parisā — and answers it with one criterion instead of five: ethical conduct alone.

The verbal link between the two discourses is exact rather than approximate. AN 4.7’s closing line named its four the saṅghasobhanā, the graces of the Saṅgha; this discourse names its ethical four the parisasobhanā, the graces of the assembly. Same root, sobhati, to shine — carried from one chapter’s opening run to another’s, twenty-one chapters later.

Vocabulary carried over the chapter break

More striking than the echo of AN 4.7 is what this discourse borrows from the chapter immediately behind it. Dussīlo pāpadhammo, unethical and of bad character; sīlavā kalyāṇadhammo, ethical and of good character — this is precisely the pāpadhamma / kalyāṇadhamma pairing that AN 4.207–210 spent four discourses building into a vocabulary for the ten courses of action and the tenfold path.

Here the content behind the vocabulary has changed — no ten deeds, no path factors, just unqualified ethical conduct — but the words that describe good and bad character have not reset. A reader moving from AN 4.210 straight into AN 4.211 meets a genuinely new topic, the assembly, dressed in the terminology the previous topic had just finished sharpening.

What ‘corrupt’ and ‘grace’ are claims about

Parisadūsana and parisasobhana are not claims about where a person goes after death; nothing here mentions hell or heaven, the formula that will dominate the rest of this chapter starting at the next discourse. They are claims about how a gathering looks and functions while its members are alive in it — whether it is let down or set off by the character of those who compose it.

That keeps faith with AN 4.7’s concern, which was explicitly about what makes the community creditable to those looking at it from outside. An assembly, unlike an individual’s private practice, is a social fact, visible to outsiders and judged as a whole; one unethical member inside it is everyone’s problem in a way one unethical private choice is not.

Setting the chapter’s terms before the register shifts

Read against what follows, this discourse’s job becomes clearer. It names the chapter — Parisāvagga, from its own title — and states the chapter’s concern in the mildest possible form: ethical or unethical, full stop. Starting at AN 4.212 the chapter switches to a much sterner formula, placement in hell or heaven as if delivered there, and runs it across a series of different four-item lists. This opening discourse is the quiet version of the chapter’s question, asked once before the loud version begins.

Key terms

parisā — “assembly, gathering” — the word that names this chapter, and the unit of concern in this discourse: not the individual but the group they belong to.
parisadūsana / parisasobhana — “one who corrupts an assembly” and “one who graces it” — built on the same sobhati root, ‘to shine’, as AN 4.7’s saṅghasobhanā.
pāpadhamma / kalyāṇadhamma — “bad character” and “good character” — the vocabulary AN 4.207–210 built for the ten deeds and the tenfold path, redeployed here for plain ethical conduct at the very start of a new chapter.
dussīla / sīlavā — “unethical” and “ethical, virtuous” — the single criterion this discourse uses, in place of AN 4.7’s five qualities.
bhikkhu, bhikkhunī, upāsaka, upāsikā — monk, nun, layman, laywoman — the fourfold assembly, the same cast named at AN 4.7 and recurring constantly through the collection.

The text

The discourse in full: the four who corrupt an assembly, and the four who grace it. Translation: Bhikkhu Sujato (CC0, SuttaCentral).

Four who corrupt an assembly

§1“Mendicants, these four corrupt an assembly. What four? A monk, nun, layman, or laywoman who is unethical, of bad character. These are the four that corrupt an assembly.

Four who grace an assembly

§2Mendicants, these four grace an assembly. What four? A monk, nun, layman, or laywoman who is ethical, of good character. These are the four that grace an assembly.”
