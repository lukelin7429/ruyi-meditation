Aṅguttara Nikāya · Discourse 4.173

With Mahākoṭṭhita

Mahākoṭṭhikasutta — Cetanāvagga — the eighteenth chapter of the Fours

Setting
None stated; the Cetanāvagga gives no location for this discourse, and it is addressed to the mendicants directly
Speakers
Venerable Mahākoṭṭhita and Venerable Sāriputta
Form
Four questions refused identically, a restatement, and an analysis
Length
~2 minutes to read
Northern parallel
The tetralemma structure applied to what lies beyond the cessation of the six sense fields is shared with material found across the early collections and famously in MN 18; this reading guide does not assert a specific matching sutra number
Difficulty
★★★★★ — the hardest logical structure in the Fours, and one that dismantles its own question rather than answering it

Why this discourse

Mahākoṭṭhita asks Sāriputta a question in four forms: when the six fields of contact have faded away completely, does something else exist? Does it not exist? Does it both exist and not exist? Does it neither exist nor not exist? Four times Sāriputta answers the same way — don’t put it like that — and refuses to be pinned to any of the four positions a person might expect to exhaust the possibilities.

Reading guide

The teaching in one sentence

All four ways of asking what remains beyond the six sense fields are rejected, because the question itself extends a kind of elaboration that has nowhere left to attach once those fields have ceased.

The four horns

The classical tetralemma: exists, does not exist, both exists and does not exist, neither exists nor does not exist. Together these four are usually treated as logically exhaustive — there is, on the face of it, no fifth way to answer a yes-or-no question about existence.

Sāriputta answers mā hevaṁ, don’t put it like that, to every one of the four, without elaboration at the time of asking. That refusal, repeated across an apparently exhaustive set of options, is what makes Mahākoṭṭhita press further: if none of the four descriptions fits, how should the statement be understood at all?

What the refusal is refusing

Sāriputta’s eventual answer does not choose a fifth position. It says that all four ways of putting the question are instances of papañca, proliferation — and that asserting any of them, including the negations, is ‘proliferating the unproliferated’, appapañcaṁ papañceti.

Papañca is the elaborating, expanding tendency of a mind working with concepts — taking a bare experience and spinning it into identity, possession, and argument. The term is best known from its long treatment in MN 18, where perception, thought, and proliferation are traced from contact through to conflict over views.

The claim here is precise: the six fields of contact and proliferation have exactly the same scope. The scope of proliferation extends as far as the scope of the six fields of contact; the scope of the six fields of contact extends as far as the scope of proliferation. Where one operates, so does the other, and neither exceeds the other’s reach.

Why a fifth answer would not help

This is the discourse’s hardest move and it deserves to be stated without softening. If proliferation and the six sense fields are coextensive, then any positive or negative claim about what exists beyond those fields is already using the very apparatus — contact-generated conceptual elaboration — whose cessation the question is asking about.

That is why none of the four horns can be affirmed: each one presupposes that the conceptual machinery for asking about existence is still operating in the space where the question locates its object, and the discourse’s premise is that this machinery has exactly the same boundary as the six fields it is asking past.

A fifth logical option is not available because the problem is not with which answer is correct. It is with what a correct answer, of any of the available shapes, would have to presuppose.

The last line

When the six fields of contact fade away and cease with no residue left behind, proliferation stops and is stilled. This is the discourse’s only positive statement, and it is deliberately not a description of a remaining state. It reports an ending — of proliferation, which is to say of the very activity that was generating the question — without characterizing what, if anything, that leaves.

Read against AN 4.171, two discourses back, this closing line uses the identical phrase for cessation: asesavirāganirodhā, fading away and ceasing with no residue. Both discourses describe an ending in the same technical vocabulary and both decline to fill in what comes after with a positive content, for what this reading guide takes to be the same reason: the vocabulary available for filling it in belongs to the very apparatus that has ceased.

What happens next

AN 4.174 gives what looks, at first glance, like the identical exchange — but with Ānanda asking Mahākoṭṭhita the same four questions, and Mahākoṭṭhita giving Sāriputta’s answer back, word for word. Read together, the two discourses show the teaching passing from one senior disciple to the next intact, refusal for refusal, analysis for analysis.

Key terms

phassāyatana — “field of contact” — the six sense bases through which contact occurs; their complete fading away is the discourse’s starting condition.
mā hevaṁ — “don’t put it like that” — Sāriputta’s identical refusal to all four questions.
papañca — “proliferation” — the elaborating tendency of conceptual mind, given its fullest early treatment in MN 18; here said to share an exact boundary with the six fields of contact.
appapañcaṁ papañceti — “proliferating the unproliferated” — Sāriputta’s diagnosis of each of the four questions, including their negations.
asesavirāganirodha — “fading away and ceasing with no residue” — the same phrase used in AN 4.171 for the ending of the basis of pleasure and pain.

The text

The discourse in full: the four questions refused, restated, and analyzed. Translation: Bhikkhu Sujato (CC0, SuttaCentral).

Mahākoṭṭhita’s question

§1Then Venerable Mahākoṭṭhita went up to Venerable Sāriputta, and exchanged greetings with him. When the greetings and polite conversation were over, Mahākoṭṭhita sat down to one side, and said to Sāriputta: “Reverend, when the six fields of contact have faded away and ceased with no residue left behind, does something else still exist?”

Four refusals

§2“Don’t put it like that, reverend.” “Does something else no longer exist?” “Don’t put it like that, reverend.” “Does something else both still exist and no longer exist?” “Don’t put it like that, reverend.” “Does something else neither still exist nor no longer exist?” “Don’t put it like that, reverend.”

Mahākoṭṭhita restates the puzzle

§3“Reverend, when asked whether—when the six fields of contact have faded away and ceased with no residue left behind—something else still exists, you say ‘don’t put it like that’. When asked whether something else no longer exists, you say ‘don’t put it like that’. When asked whether something else both still exists and no longer exists, you say ‘don’t put it like that’. When asked whether something else neither still exists nor no longer exists, you say ‘don’t put it like that’. How then should we see the meaning of this statement?”

Sāriputta’s answer

§4“In saying that, ‘When the six fields of contact have faded away and ceased with no residue left behind, something else still exists’, you’re proliferating the unproliferated. In saying that, ‘something else no longer exists’, you’re proliferating the unproliferated. In saying that, ‘something else both still exists and no longer exists’, you’re proliferating the unproliferated. In saying that, ‘something else neither still exists nor no longer exists’, you’re proliferating the unproliferated. The scope of proliferation extends as far as the scope of the six fields of contact. The scope of the six fields of contact extends as far as the scope of proliferation. When the six fields of contact fade away and cease with no residue left behind, proliferation stops and is stilled.”
