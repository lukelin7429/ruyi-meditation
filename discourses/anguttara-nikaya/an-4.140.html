Aṅguttara Nikāya · Discourse 4.140

Why this discourse

A speaker can run out of things worth saying while still having ways to say them, or run out of ways to say them while still having something worth saying, or run out of both, or of neither. Four cells, as always in this chapter. Then the discourse does something it has not done once in ten: it makes a claim. It is impossible, it cannot happen, that someone accomplished in the four kinds of textual analysis will ever run out of either.

Reading guide

The teaching in one sentence

Speakers run dry in two different ways, and one accomplishment makes running dry impossible.

Matter and manner

Attha is the meaning, the point, what the words are about. Byañjana is the letter, the phrasing, the way it is put. The pair is one of the most durable in the canon and turns up wherever the tradition talks about how teaching is transmitted.

The verb is pariyādānaṁ gacchati, to come to exhaustion — to be used up, to reach the end of the supply. It is a resource word, and it makes the discourse a description of stock rather than of skill.

That framing is worth holding on to. The question is not whether a speaker is good but whether they have anything left, which is a different question and a more practical one. Anyone who has taught the same material for a long time recognizes both failure modes: nothing more to say, or nothing new to say it with.

The four cells

Runs out of matter but not phrasing. Runs out of phrasing but not matter. Runs out of both. Runs out of neither.

The first is the more familiar failure and the more dangerous one, because it is invisible from inside and often from outside as well — the phrasing is still there, and the phrasing is what an audience hears. This is the same person AN 4.132 called fluent but not on topic, met at a later stage.

The second is uncomfortable but honest: the speaker has the matter and cannot find the words for it. Nothing in the discourse suggests this is worse than the first, and the chapter’s consistent refusal to rank mixed cases holds here too.

The fourth cell is where the discourse breaks its own pattern. Everywhere else in the Puggalavagga the complete case is simply listed and the text stops. Here it is listed, and then a sentence is added about how to occupy it.

The one positive claim in ten discourses

It is impossible, it cannot happen, that someone accomplished in the four kinds of textual analysis will ever run out of meaningful things to say, or ways of phrasing things.

Aṭṭhānametaṁ anavakāso is the canon’s strong impossibility formula, used for things ruled out absolutely rather than merely unlikely. It is a heavy piece of grammar to attach to a chapter that has spent ten discourses declining to say anything at all.

The four paṭisambhidā are the analytical knowledges: of meaning, of the teaching, of language, and of eloquence — attha, dhamma, nirutti, paṭibhāna. Two of those four are the very terms the discourse has just used as its axes, and the fourth is the word AN 4.132 was built on.

So the closing sentence is not a new subject arriving from outside. It names the accomplishment that covers both axes at once, and it says that a person who has it cannot be exhausted. That is the Puggalavagga’s only prescription, and it arrives in the last line of the last discourse.

Why it belongs here and not earlier

A chapter that ranked its cells would have had to argue for the ranking. This chapter does something cheaper and more durable: it lays out ten sets of distinctions without comment and then, once, at the end, names one thing that makes a complete case achievable.

Read that way the closing sentence is the chapter’s signature rather than an afterthought. Everything before it has been description; this is the single line of instruction, placed where a reciter reaching the end of the chapter will hear it last.

It is also worth noting how modest the instruction is even so. It does not say the analytical knowledges make a person wise, or awakened, or a good teacher. It says they make running out impossible. The claim is narrow and it is exactly matched to the discourse it closes.

The end of the chapter

Ten discourses, ten classifications: fetters, eloquence, learners, livelihood, blame, the threefold training twice, seclusion, Dhamma speakers, and speakers. Almost no narrative, no setting, no interlocutors, and one sentence of advice.

This is the Aṅguttara showing its working. A collection organized by number needs categories more than it needs stories, and the Puggalavagga is where the categories are kept for the number four applied to persons.

What follows is the Ābhāvagga, the chapter of light, which drops persons entirely and organizes by image instead — four kinds of radiance, of splendor, of light, of brightness. The Fours are about to become considerably less dry.

Key terms

attha — “meaning”, the point — what words are about; first of the pair, and first of the four analytical knowledges.
byañjana — “phrasing”, the letter — how a thing is put; the standing partner of attha throughout the canon.
pariyādānaṁ gacchati — “comes to exhaustion” — a resource word, which makes the discourse a description of stock rather than of skill.
paṭisambhidā — “analytical knowledge” — the four are of meaning, of the teaching, of language, and of eloquence.
aṭṭhānametaṁ anavakāso — “it is impossible, it cannot happen” — the canon’s strong impossibility formula, used once in this whole chapter.

The text

The discourse in full: four speakers, and then the chapter’s only positive claim. Translation: Bhikkhu Sujato (CC0, SuttaCentral).

Four speakers, and what cannot happen

§1“Mendicants, there are these four speakers. What four? There’s a speaker who runs out of meaningful things to say, but not of ways of phrasing things. There’s a speaker who runs out of ways of phrasing things, but not of meaningful things to say. There’s a speaker who runs out of both meaningful things to say, and ways of phrasing things. There’s a speaker who never runs out of meaningful things to say, or ways of phrasing things. These are the four speakers. It is impossible, it cannot happen that someone accomplished in the four kinds of textual analysis will ever run out of meaningful things to say, or ways of phrasing things.”
