Aṅguttara Nikāya · Discourse 4.186

Approach

Ummaggasutta — Brāhmaṇavagga — the nineteenth chapter of the Fours

Setting
None stated; the Brāhmaṇavagga gives no location for this discourse, and it is addressed to the mendicants directly
Speakers
An unnamed mendicant and the Buddha
Form
Three questions, each praised identically before being answered
Length
~3 minutes to read
Northern parallel
The mind as what leads and drags the world is widely echoed in opening material across the early collections; this reading guide does not assert a specific matching sutra number
Difficulty
★★★☆☆ — three short exchanges, and the second directly complicates what ‘learned’ usually seems to mean

Why this discourse

One mendicant asks three questions in a row, and each time the Buddha praises the question in the same words before answering it: what leads the world on, what drags it around, what arises and takes control? What defines a learned memorizer of the teaching? What defines wisdom, and then great wisdom? The answers move from the mind, to a single verse rightly understood and practiced, to an entirely ethical definition of what counts as wise.

Reading guide

The teaching in one sentence

The mind runs the world; being learned is measured by understanding and practicing even a little correctly rather than by quantity memorized; and wisdom, at its greatest, is defined by the intention to harm no one.

A formula of praise, repeated three times

Each of the mendicant’s three questions receives the identical opening: good, good, mendicant! Your approach and eloquence are excellent, and it’s a good question. Ummagga, the word rendered ‘approach’ here and giving the discourse its Pāli title, is an unusual one — literally a tunnel or an underground passage, and by extension a way of getting at something, a line of reasoning that finds its way through.

The other praised quality, paṭibhāna, is the same term AN 4.132 and AN 4.140 used for eloquence and analytical readiness. Here it is applied not to a discourse the mendicant gives but to the questions he asks — a reminder that the qualities praised elsewhere as marks of a good speaker apply equally to a good questioner.

What leads the world

The first question is stated as three phrasings of one thing: what leads the world on, what drags it around, what arises and takes control? The answer is a single word, repeated in the same three verbs: the mind leads the world on. The mind drags it around. When the mind arises, it takes control.

No mechanism is given, and no elaboration follows. The claim is stated with the same flatness the question was asked with, and the discourse moves immediately to the next exchange. Whatever explanatory work this claim is meant to do, it is left to the listener to work out from the bare statement itself.

A learned memorizer of the teaching, redefined

The second question asks what makes someone bahussuto dhammadharo, a learned memorizer of the teaching — a title that sounds, on its face, like a matter of volume. The Buddha’s answer refuses that reading directly.

He first names the range of what he has taught — statements, mixed prose and verse, discussions, verses, inspired exclamations, legends, stories of past lives, amazing stories, elaborations — a substantial list, seemingly building toward a claim about quantity. Then the answer inverts the expectation: if anyone understands the meaning and the text of even a four-line verse, and if they practice in line with that teaching, they’re qualified to be called a learned memorizer of the teaching.

This bears directly on AN 4.6, several chapters back, which crossed hearing much or little with holding much or little and refused to rank the combinations. This discourse goes further: it names the specific threshold at which the title applies, and the threshold is understanding and practice, not volume at all. A person who has heard everything the Buddha taught but understood and practiced none of it does not qualify; a person who has grasped and lived out four lines does.

Penetrating wisdom, and the marksman again

The third question asks about paññāvantatā, penetrating wisdom, and the answer is the four noble truths, seen with penetrating wisdom in each case: this is suffering, this is its origin, this is its cessation, this is the practice leading to its cessation.

The same four truths defined marksmanship at AN 4.181, earlier in this chapter. Read together, the two discourses supply each other’s missing half: AN 4.181 gave the image — hitting a vital point precisely — without naming what ‘wisdom’ means in the abstract; this discourse gives the abstract definition its concrete picture back.

Great wisdom, defined by harmlessness

The fourth question asks about paṇḍito mahāpañño, an astute person with great wisdom, and the answer breaks entirely from the pattern of the previous two — no doctrine is named, no teaching is recited. The definition is ethical: someone with no intention to hurt themselves, or others, or both, whose thinking is only ever of benefit — to themselves, to others, to both, and to the whole world.

That final phrase, sabbalokahitameva cintayamāno cinteti, only ever thinking of benefit for the whole world, is the widest scope given to any single quality in this chapter. It also echoes, in vocabulary if not in exact content, the fourfold self/other benefit classification found elsewhere in the Fours at AN 4.97, which sorts individuals by whether their practice benefits themselves, others, both, or neither. Here the same four terms — self, other, both, and (uniquely) the whole world — are folded into a single positive definition rather than kept as four separate cases.

The progression across the mendicant’s three questions is worth naming directly: from a metaphysical claim about what drives the world, to an epistemic redefinition of what counts as learned, to a wholly ethical account of what counts as truly wise. The questions get harder to answer with a formula and easier to recognize when actually met.

Key terms

ummagga — “approach” — literally a tunnel or underground passage; a line of reasoning that finds its way through, and the discourse’s Pāli title.
paṭibhāna — “eloquence”, analytical readiness — the same term from AN 4.132 and AN 4.140, applied here to the mendicant’s questions rather than to a speaker’s answers.
bahussuto dhammadharo — “a learned memorizer of the teaching” — redefined by understanding and practice of even a four-line verse, not by quantity heard.
paññāvantatā — “penetrating wisdom” — defined by the four noble truths, echoing the marksman image of AN 4.181.
paṇḍito mahāpañño — “an astute person with great wisdom” — defined ethically, by the absence of any intention to harm and the presence only of thoughts of benefit.

The text

The discourse in full: three questions, each praised, and answered in turn. Translation: Bhikkhu Sujato (CC0, SuttaCentral).

What leads the world?

§1Then one of the mendicants went up to the Buddha, bowed, sat down to one side, and said to him: “Sir, what leads the world on? What drags it around? What arises and takes control?” “Good, good, mendicant! Your approach and eloquence are excellent, and it’s a good question. For you asked: ‘What leads the world on? What drags it around? What arises and takes control?’” “Yes, sir.” “Mendicant, the mind leads the world on. The mind drags it around. When the mind arises, it takes control.”

A learned memorizer of the teaching

§2Saying “Good, sir”, that mendicant approved and agreed with what the Buddha said. Then he asked another question: “Sir, they speak of ‘a learned memorizer of the teaching’. How is a learned memorizer of the teaching defined?” “Good, good, mendicant! Your approach and eloquence are excellent, and it’s a good question. … I have taught many teachings: statements, mixed prose & verse, discussions, verses, inspired exclamations, legends, stories of past lives, amazing stories, and elaborations. But if anyone understands the meaning and the text of even a four-line verse, and if they practice in line with that teaching, they’re qualified to be called a ‘learned memorizer of the teaching’.”

Penetrating wisdom

§3Saying “Good, sir”, that mendicant approved and agreed with what the Buddha said. Then he asked another question: “Sir, they speak of ‘a learned person with penetrating wisdom’. How is a learned person with penetrating wisdom defined?” “Good, good, mendicant! Your approach and eloquence are excellent, and it’s a good question. … Take a mendicant who has heard: ‘This is suffering.’ They see what it means with penetrating wisdom. They’ve heard: ‘This is the origin of suffering’ … ‘This is the cessation of suffering’ … ‘This is the practice that leads to the cessation of suffering.’ They see what it means with penetrating wisdom. That’s how an individual is learned, with penetrating wisdom.”

Great wisdom

§4Saying “Good, sir”, that mendicant approved and agreed with what the Buddha said. Then he asked another question: “Sir, they speak of ‘an astute person with great wisdom’. How is an astute person with great wisdom defined?” “Good, good, mendicant! Your approach and eloquence are excellent, and it’s a good question. … An astute person with great wisdom is one who has no intention to hurt themselves, or to hurt others, or to hurt both. When they think, they only think of the benefit for themselves, for others, for both, and for the whole world. That’s how an individual is astute, with great wisdom.”
