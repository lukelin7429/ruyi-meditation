Aṅguttara Nikāya · Discourse 6.14

A Good Death

Bhaddakasutta — Sāraṇīyavagga — the second chapter of the Sixes

Setting
None stated in the source
Speakers
Sāriputta, addressing the mendicants
Form
Two matched formulas — how life is lived so as not to have a good death, and so as to have one — closed with four verse lines
Length
~2 minutes to read
Northern parallel
The theme of a death free of regret through non-attachment recurs in related forms across the Chinese Āgamas; this reading guide does not assert a specific matching sutra number
Difficulty
★★☆☆☆ — the central term, papañca, carries real philosophical weight and resists a single English word

Why this discourse

AN 6.14 is spoken by Sāriputta, not the Buddha — the first discourse in this chapter with a named speaker. His subject is blunt: what determines whether a mendicant has a “good death,” bhaddaka maraṇa. His answer turns on a single Pāli term, papañca, usually rendered “proliferation” — the mind's tendency to elaborate, embellish, and multiply itself around whatever it engages with. A mendicant who relishes six things, culminating in this proliferation, does not die well; one who does not relish them does.

Reading guide

The teaching in one sentence

A mendicant who relishes work, talk, sleep, company, closeness, and proliferation does not have a good death; one who does not relish these six things does.

Six items building toward one culmination

The list moves from concrete activities to something more abstract: work (kamma), talk (bhassa), sleep (niddā), company (saṅgaṇikā), closeness (saṁsagga), and finally papañca, proliferation. The first five are recognizable distractions from meditative life; the sixth names something subtler happening within the mind itself, and the discourse's own closing verse singles it out as the decisive term, not merely the sixth item in a list.

What 'proliferation' names

Papañca is one of the canon's harder technical terms to render in English. It names the mind's tendency, once a perception has arisen, to spin outward into elaboration — associations, conceptual complications, and the sense of a self standing at the center of it all. Elsewhere in the canon (notably MN 18) it is explicitly linked to how ordinary perception generates the very concepts a person then becomes entangled in and fights over. Here, it is simply named as the last and most consequential of six things worth not relishing.

Two labels for two ways of living

The discourse gives each way of living its own name: a mendicant who relishes these six is called one “who enjoys substantial reality (sakkāya),” who has not given it up “to rightly make an end of suffering”; one who does not relish them is called one “who delights in extinguishment,” who has given up substantial reality for that same end. Sakkāya, usually translated “identity” or “substantial reality,” is the same term at the root of sakkāyadiṭṭhi, identity view, one of the fetters binding beings to repeated existence.

A death that is 'good' in a specific, technical sense

The discourse is not offering general advice about a peaceful passing in the ordinary sense. “Good death” here is inseparable from what has or has not been given up during life; it names whether one dies still entangled in proliferation and identity, or dies having already let them go. The closing verse makes this explicit, calling extinguishment “the supreme sanctuary from the yoke,” not a description of a serene final moment for its own sake.

Key terms

bhaddaka maraṇa — “a good death” — the discourse's own title and subject, defined entirely in terms of what has been relinquished during life.
papañca — “proliferation” — the mind's tendency to elaborate and multiply around what it perceives; the sixth and culminating item in this discourse's list.
sakkāya — “substantial reality,” “identity” — what one who relishes the six listed things is said to enjoy and not give up.
nibbāna — “extinguishment” — what one who does not relish the six things is said to delight in instead, named in the closing verse as the supreme sanctuary.
yogakkhema — “sanctuary from the yoke,” “security from bondage” — the closing verse's description of what proliferation, given up, makes possible.

The text

The discourse in full: Sāriputta on what makes a mendicant's death good or otherwise, closing in verse. Translation: Bhikkhu Sujato (CC0, SuttaCentral).

Sāriputta addresses the mendicants

§1There Sāriputta addressed the mendicants: “Reverends, mendicants!” “Reverend,” they replied. Sāriputta said this:

Living so as not to have a good death

§2“A mendicant lives life so as to not have a good death. And how do they live life so as to not have a good death? Take a mendicant who relishes work, talk, sleep, company, closeness, and proliferation. They love these things and like to relish them. A mendicant who lives life like this does not have a good death. This is called a mendicant who enjoys substantial reality, who hasn’t given up substantial reality to rightly make an end of suffering.

Living so as to have a good death

§3A mendicant lives life so as to have a good death. And how do they live life so as to have a good death? Take a mendicant who doesn’t relish work, talk, sleep, company, closeness, and proliferation. They don’t love these things or like to relish them. A mendicant who lives life like this has a good death. This is called a mendicant who delights in extinguishment, who has given up substantial reality to rightly make an end of suffering.

The closing verses

§4A beast who likes to proliferate, enjoying proliferation, fails to win extinguishment, the supreme sanctuary from the yoke. But one who gives up proliferation, enjoying the state of non-proliferation, wins extinguishment, the supreme sanctuary from the yoke.”
