Aṅguttara Nikāya · Discourse 3.42

Three Grounds

Tiṭhānasutta — Cūḷavagga — the fifth chapter of the Threes

Setting
None stated; the discourse continues from AN 3.1, whose setting at Sāvatthī is understood to hold
Speakers
The Buddha alone, addressing the mendicants
Form
A statement, three grounds, the statement repeated, and one closing verse
Length
~1 minute to read
Northern parallel
Comparable lists of the marks of a lay follower’s faith appear in the Chinese Āgamas; this reading guide does not assert a specific matching sutra number
Difficulty
★☆☆☆☆ — plain, and more radical than it looks

Why this discourse

How do you tell whether someone has faith? The discourse gives three grounds and not one of them is something a person says, believes, or affirms. They like the company of ethical people, they like hearing the teaching, and they live at home without stinginess. Faith, on this account, is diagnosed the way a doctor diagnoses: by looking at what the patient does.

Reading guide

The teaching in one sentence

Faith shows up as three preferences and one habit, all of them visible from outside.

Three grounds, none of them a belief

Ṭhāna is a place, a ground, a basis — here, a basis on which a judgment can be made. The discourse promises that a person with faith and confidence can be known, and then delivers three observable facts.

They like to see ethical people. They like to hear the true teaching. They live at home rid of the stain of stinginess, freely generous, open-handed, loving to let go, committed to charity, loving to give and to share — six phrases for the third ground, against a handful of words for the other two.

What is absent is worth naming to a class before anything else. There is no creed here. Nobody is asked what they think about rebirth, about the Buddha’s awakening, or about the status of the teaching. A person could recite every doctrinal formula correctly and fail all three tests, and a person who could not explain a single point of doctrine could pass all three.

The first two are about appetite

Both of the first two grounds are stated as liking, not as doing. It is not they visit ethical people but they like to see them; not they attend teachings but they like to hear the true teaching.

That is a sharper test than the behavioral one and harder to fake. Attendance can be produced by duty, habit, or social pressure. Wanting to be there cannot, and it is something a person can check about themselves in a few seconds.

It also tells you what this tradition thinks faith is. Not certainty about propositions, and not obedience, but a settled attraction — the state of finding a certain kind of person and a certain kind of conversation genuinely appealing. On that account faith is closer to taste than to opinion, and it is worth telling students that this is not a modern softening but what the third ground’s six phrases are also describing.

The stain of stinginess

Macchera, stinginess or meanness, is treated in the canon as a stain or blemish, and it is the only one of the three grounds stated in the negative and then expanded positively at length.

The expansion is precise about where the test applies: they live at home. Not in a monastery, not on retreat, not when a collection plate is going around, but in the ordinary running of a household, where nobody is watching and the small decisions get made.

And the six phrases are not synonyms padded for rhythm. Muttacāga, loving to let go, is about the hand opening. Payatapāṇi, open-handed, is a posture. Dānasaṁvibhāgarata, delighting in giving and sharing, adds distribution to donation — sharing among equals rather than only giving downward. Together they describe a temperament rather than a series of acts.

Why the third ground is the one that closes the verse

The verse restates the three and then names the result: that’s who’s called an individual of faith. The order is unchanged and the third is again given the emphatic position — they’ve driven out the stain of stinginess.

There is a plain reason for the weighting. The first two grounds cost nothing. Liking good company and good teaching is agreeable, and a person can have both while remaining exactly as tight-fisted as they were. The third is the one that shows up in the accounts.

This is also why AN 3.41 and AN 3.42 sit next to each other. The first says merit needs an actual gift; the second says faith is recognized by whether a household actually gives. Neither discourse is interested in what anyone reports about their own inner state.

Using it

The discourse is short enough to use as an exercise. Read it, then ask a class how they would tell whether a person is religious, and compare their list with these three. Most lists that get produced are made of professions and observances; this one is made of appetites and expenditure.

For a teacher there is a second use. Applied to a classroom rather than a household, the same three grounds ask whether students want to be around people who do the thing well, want to hear it explained, and are willing to give something away — time, credit, or help. The structure transfers cleanly, and it is a better diagnostic than enthusiasm.

Key terms

ṭhāna — “ground, basis, place” — here a basis on which a judgment about someone can be made. The discourse promises the person can be known, and delivers observable facts.
macchera — “stinginess” — treated in the canon as a stain, mala. The only one of the three grounds stated in the negative and then expanded at length.
muttacāga — “loving to let go” — literally with released giving. One of six phrases the discourse spends on the third ground alone.
payatapāṇi — “open-handed” — a posture rather than an act: the hand held out ready rather than the gift already made.
dānasaṁvibhāgarata — “delighting in giving and sharing” — saṁvibhāga adds distribution among equals to dāna, donation.

The text

The discourse in full, with its closing verse. Translation: Bhikkhu Sujato (CC0, SuttaCentral).

Three grounds

§1“There are three grounds, mendicants, by which a person with faith and confidence can be known. What three? They like to see ethical people. They like to hear the true teaching. And they live at home rid of the stain of stinginess, freely generous, open-handed, loving to let go, committed to charity, loving to give and to share. These are the three grounds by which a person with faith and confidence can be known.

In verse

§2They like to see ethical people; they want to hear the true teaching; they’ve driven out the stain of stinginess: that’s who’s called an individual of faith.”
