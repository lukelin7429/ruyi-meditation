Aṅguttara Nikāya · Discourse 4.122

The Danger of Waves

Ūmibhayasutta — Bhayavagga — the thirteenth chapter of the Fours

Setting
None stated; the Bhayavagga gives no location for this discourse, and it is addressed to the mendicants directly
Speakers
The Buddha alone, addressing the mendicants
Form
A simile of four water hazards, then four matching scenes of a mendicant disrobing, each closed by a one-line gloss
Length
~6 minutes to read
Northern parallel
The four-hazard simile for those who go forth is known in the Chinese Āgamas; this reading guide does not assert a specific matching sutra number
Difficulty
★★★☆☆ — the scenes are vivid and easy, and the fourth gloss is one of the hardest lines in the Fours

Why this discourse

Anyone entering the water should expect waves, gharials, whirlpools, and sharks. Anyone entering the homeless life should expect four dangers of the same names, and the discourse gives each one a scene rather than a definition — a man told how to hold his bowl by someone young enough to be his grandson, a man forbidden an evening meal he can see being offered, a man on alms round doing the arithmetic of his family’s wealth. Three of the four are named for states of mind. The fourth is named for a class of people, and that is the discourse’s hardest fact.

Reading guide

The teaching in one sentence

Four things reliably end a monastic life, and the discourse describes each of them as a scene ending in the same words: they resign the training and return to a lesser life.

How the simile is built

The frame takes four seconds. Someone going into the water should anticipate four dangers — pāṭikaṅkhitabbāni, things to be expected rather than things that might happen. Then the same four names are transferred whole to the person who has gone forth.

What follows is not an allegory to be decoded. Each danger gets a narrative — a specific man, a specific provocation, a specific thought in the first person — and then a single flat line telling you what the water-word stands for. The scene carries the weight; the gloss is only an index entry.

Notice also who the subject is throughout: a kulaputta, a son of a good family, who has gone forth out of faith. The discourse is not describing people who never meant it. It is describing the failure of people who did.

Waves: being taught by children

The first scene is entirely social. The man has gone forth with the full formula of motivation — swamped by rebirth, old age, and death, hoping to make an end of suffering — and then his companions begin instructing him: how to go out and come back, how to look ahead and to the side, how to draw in and stretch out his limbs, how to carry his robes and bowl.

His thought is quoted exactly: formerly, as laypeople, we advised and instructed others. And now these mendicants — who you’d think were our children or grandchildren — imagine they can advise and instruct us!

The observation is precise, and it is about seniority rather than about the rules. Monastic seniority runs from ordination, not from age, so a man who arrives at fifty is junior to everyone who arrived at twenty. What is being described is that inversion landing on someone who was used to being the one who spoke.

The gloss is kodhūpāyāsa, anger and distress. It is worth noting that the scene reads more like injured pride than like anger, and the text labels the outcome rather than the root.

Gharials and whirlpools: food, and money

The second scene is about eating. The instruction concerns what may be consumed and when, and the complaint again arrives in the man’s own voice: as laypeople we ate what we wanted, when we wanted, and these faithful householders give us delicious fresh and cooked foods at the wrong time of day. But these guys imagine they can gag our mouths! The gloss is odarikatta, belly-ness, gluttony.

The specific irritation is worth spelling out for a class: the food is there, it is given willingly, and the only thing preventing him from eating it is a rule about the hour. That is a harder discipline than simple scarcity, and the discourse knows it.

The third scene changes register. The mendicant goes for alms without guarding body, speech, and mind, without establishing mindfulness, and without restraining the sense faculties — the failure is stated before anything is seen. Then he sees a householder enjoying himself, and reasons: my family is wealthy, I could both enjoy my wealth and make merit, so why not go back?

That reasoning is the subtlest thing in the discourse, because it is not obviously wrong. AN 4.61 says almost exactly this — that wealth properly used makes the giver happy and produces merit. The discourse does not answer the argument. It shows where the argument came from, which was an unguarded alms round, and lets the reader draw the conclusion. The gloss is kāmaguṇa, the five kinds of sensual stimulation.

Sharks: what the fourth gloss says

The fourth scene is four lines long, the shortest of the four. The mendicant goes on alms round in the same unguarded way, sees a woman lightly dressed, lust infects his mind, and he disrobes. Then: ‘Danger of sharks’ is a term for ladies.

The Pāli is mātugāma, womankind, the general term for women as a class. The line should be quoted as it stands rather than softened, because softening it hides what is actually odd about it.

Here is the oddity. The first three glosses name states in the person: anger and distress, gluttony, sensual stimulation. All three sit inside the man being described. The fourth breaks the pattern and names other people — and it does so immediately after a scene in which the discourse has already told us the mechanism is internal, since the failure of sense restraint is stated before the woman appears at all. The text supplies its own better answer and then labels the danger with the wrong noun.

It should also be said that the discourse is addressed to men who have gone forth, and no counterpart naming men as a danger to nuns is given here. That asymmetry is not hidden in the text and it should not be hidden in the teaching of it.

This is not the collection’s only voice on the subject, and the Fours contain flatly different material — AN 4.55 has a husband and wife matched as equals in faith, ethics, generosity, and wisdom, and addressed as such by the Buddha in person. Both passages are in the same book. Neither cancels the other, and a reader is not obliged to harmonize them.

What the four are for

Every one of the four ends identically: they resign the training and return to a lesser life. The discourse is about attrition — not about failure in meditation, but about the ordinary reasons people leave.

Read as a piece of observation it is shrewd. Status, appetite, money, and sex, in that order, and each caught at the moment of self-justification rather than at the moment of leaving. Every scene turns on a sentence the man says to himself, and in three of the four that sentence begins formerly, as laypeople…

The thing being lost, in each case, is not the practice but the willingness to be a beginner. That is the reading worth carrying out of this discourse, and it does not depend on accepting the fourth gloss.

Key terms

kulaputta — “gentleman”, son of a good family — the subject of all four scenes, and someone who went forth out of faith, not carelessly.
kodhūpāyāsa — “anger and distress” — what ‘the danger of waves’ is said to mean, though the scene itself describes injured seniority.
odarikatta — “gluttony” — from udara, belly; literally something like belly-ness, the state of being ruled by the stomach.
mātugāma — “womankind” — the general term for women as a class; the word the discourse uses to gloss ‘the danger of sharks’.
adhivacana — “is a term for” — the formula that closes each section, marking the water-word as a label rather than a description.

The text

The discourse in full: the water simile, then the four scenes of leaving, each with its closing gloss. Translation: Bhikkhu Sujato (CC0, SuttaCentral).

Four dangers in the water

§1“Mendicants, anyone who enters the water should anticipate four dangers. What four? The dangers of waves, gharials, whirlpools, and sharks. These are the four dangers that anyone who enters the water should anticipate. In the same way, a gentleman gone forth from the lay life to homelessness in this teaching and training should anticipate four dangers. What four? The dangers of waves, gharials, whirlpools, and sharks.

The danger of waves

§2And what, mendicants, is the danger of waves? It’s when a gentleman has gone forth out of faith from the lay life to homelessness, thinking: ‘I’m swamped by rebirth, old age, and death; by sorrow, lamentation, pain, sadness, and distress. I’m swamped by suffering, mired in suffering. Hopefully I can make an end to this entire mass of suffering.’ When they’ve gone forth, their spiritual companions advise and instruct them: ‘You should go out like this, and come back like that. You should look to the front like this, and to the side like that. You should contract your limbs like this, and extend them like that. This is how you should bear your outer robe, bowl, and robes.’ They think: ‘Formerly, as laypeople, we advised and instructed others. And now these mendicants—who you’d think were our children or grandchildren—imagine they can advise and instruct us!’ Angry and upset, they resign the training and return to a lesser life. This is called a mendicant who disavows the training and returns to a lesser life for fear of the danger of waves. ‘Danger of waves’ is a term for anger and distress. This is called the danger of waves.

The danger of gharials

§3And what, mendicants, is the danger of gharials? It’s when a gentleman has gone forth out of faith from the lay life to homelessness … When they’ve gone forth, their spiritual companions advise and instruct them: ‘You may eat, consume, taste, and drink these things, but not those. You may eat what’s allowable, but not what’s unallowable. You may eat at the right time, but not at the wrong time.’ They think: ‘Formerly, as laypeople, we used to eat, consume, taste, and drink what we wanted, not what we didn’t want. We ate and drank both allowable and unallowable things, at the right time and the wrong time. And these faithful householders give us delicious fresh and cooked foods at the wrong time of day. But these guys imagine they can gag our mouths!’ Angry and upset, they resign the training and return to a lesser life. This is called a mendicant who disavows the training and returns to a lesser life for fear of the danger of gharials. ‘Danger of gharials’ is a term for gluttony. This is called the danger of gharials.

The danger of whirlpools

§4And what, mendicants, is the danger of whirlpools? It’s when a gentleman has gone forth out of faith from the lay life to homelessness … When they’ve gone forth, they robe up in the morning and, taking their bowl and robe, enter a village or town for alms without guarding body, speech, and mind, without establishing mindfulness, and without restraining the sense faculties. There they see a householder or their child amusing themselves, supplied and provided with the five kinds of sensual stimulation. They think: ‘Formerly, as laypeople, we amused ourselves, supplied and provided with the five kinds of sensual stimulation. And it’s true that my family is wealthy. I can both enjoy my wealth and make merit. Why don’t I resign the training and return to a lesser life, so I can enjoy my wealth and make merit?’ They resign the training and return to a lesser life. This is called a mendicant who disavows the training and returns to a lesser life for fear of the danger of whirlpools. ‘Danger of whirlpools’ is a term for the five kinds of sensual stimulation. This is called the danger of whirlpools.

The danger of sharks

§5And what, mendicants, is the danger of sharks? It’s when a gentleman has gone forth out of faith from the lay life to homelessness … When they’ve gone forth, they robe up in the morning and, taking their bowl and robe, enter a village or town for alms without guarding body, speech, and mind, without establishing mindfulness, and without restraining the sense faculties. There they see a lady scantily clad, with revealing clothes. Lust infects their mind, so they resign the training and return to a lesser life. This is called a mendicant who disavows the training and returns to a lesser life because they’re afraid of the danger of sharks. ‘Danger of sharks’ is a term for ladies. This is called the danger of sharks. These are the four dangers that a gentleman gone forth from the lay life to homelessness in this teaching and training should anticipate.”
