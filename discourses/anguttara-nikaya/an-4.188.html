Aṅguttara Nikāya · Discourse 4.188

With Upaka

Upakasutta — Brāhmaṇavagga — the nineteenth chapter of the Fours

Setting
Rājagaha, on the Vulture’s Peak Mountain
Speakers
Upaka, son of Maṇḍikā, the Buddha, and King Ajātasattu
Form
A stated principle applied reflexively, a teaching on paraphrase, and a narrative coda involving a king
Length
~3 minutes to read
Northern parallel
The invariance of ethical content across paraphrase is a recurring theme in the early collections' accounts of the Buddha's teaching method; this reading guide does not assert a specific matching sutra number
Difficulty
★★★☆☆ — a short exchange with a coda that should be read exactly as plainly as it is written

Why this discourse

Upaka states a principle — complaining about others without giving reasons is reprehensible — and the Buddha turns it back on him immediately: that’s exactly what you’re doing. Upaka, caught, compares himself to a fish rising into a trap. The Buddha then explains that his own teaching, however phrased, always comes down to four declarations. Upaka approves and leaves respectfully — and is then savaged by his king for having dared to speak with the Buddha at all.

Reading guide

The teaching in one sentence

A principle applies to the person stating it as much as to anyone else, and a single meaning survives any number of different phrasings.

A trap sprung immediately

Upaka states his doctrine and view without preamble: whoever goes on complaining about others without giving any reasons is reprehensible and at fault. The Buddha’s reply does not evaluate the principle at all. It applies it: that’s what you do, so you’re reprehensible and at fault.

No argument is offered for why Upaka’s statement to the Buddha itself counts as an instance of complaining without reasons — the text gives no setup explaining what prompted Upaka to come with this particular grievance. What the discourse preserves is only the reflexive turn: state a principle broad enough to be universal, and it will be broad enough to catch its own speaker.

The fish in the trap

Upaka’s own image for what just happened to him is vivid and self-deprecating rather than defensive: like a fish caught in a big trap just as it rises, so the Buddha caught me in a big trap of words just as I rose up.

The simile is worth noting for what it does not do. Upaka does not protest the move as unfair or claim he was tricked by wordplay. He describes it as a trap that worked exactly as a trap should, catching him at the precise moment — ‘rising’ — that made him vulnerable to it. There is no resentment in the image, only acknowledgment.

Limitless phrasings for one meaning

Rather than press the reflexive point further, the Buddha shifts to a different teaching entirely: I’ve declared, ‘this is unskillful.’ And for that there are limitless words, phrasings, and teachings of the Realized One: ‘this is another way of saying that this is unskillful.’ The same is said of what should be given up, what is skillful, and what should be developed — four fixed declarations, each capable of being said in unlimited ways without changing what is meant.

This connects directly to AN 4.183, earlier in this chapter, where the criterion for whether to speak of something was located in its effect on skillful and unskillful qualities rather than in its source or its exact wording. This discourse extends that logic: not only does the effect matter more than the wording, but the wording itself is fundamentally variable — countless different sentences can carry the identical content, and none of them is more truly ‘the teaching’ than any other, provided the content survives the paraphrase intact.

That is a substantial claim about how the Buddha’s own teaching should be understood, offered here almost as an aside after a sharp personal exchange rather than as a formal doctrinal statement in its own right.

Upaka’s departure, and the king’s fury

Upaka approves, rises, bows, and circles the Buddha respectfully — the standard gesture of high regard, not of someone leaving in defeat or resentment. He then goes directly to King Ajātasattu and reports the entire exchange.

The king’s reaction is fury, and it is worth reading exactly as the text gives it, without softening: he calls Upaka a lavaṇakāradāraka, a salt-maker’s boy — a caste-based insult naming Upaka’s low occupational origin — and calls him scurrilous and impudent for having dared to attack the Buddha, then banishes him from his presence.

This ending deserves to be read plainly rather than explained away. Nothing in the exchange Upaka reported was an attack; he stated a principle, had it turned on him, accepted the correction without complaint, and left with every sign of respect. The king’s fury is not a response to what actually happened but to his own assumption about what an exchange with the Buddha must have been — and it arrives with a slur the text records without comment, attributing it to the king’s own words rather than presenting it as the discourse’s judgment.

This reading guide does not supply a moral for this coda that the text does not state. What can be said is that the discourse ends on a note of human misjudgment rather than resolution — a king reacting to a story rather than to the events it reported, and a young man cast out for a conversation that, on the discourse’s own account, went well.

Key terms

parūpārambha — “complaining about others” — the fault Upaka names in his opening statement, before it is turned back on him.
gārayha / upavajja — “reprehensible” and “at fault” — the two terms Upaka uses of the fault, returned to him unchanged.
aparimāṇā padā aparimāṇā byañjanā — “limitless words, limitless phrasings” — the Buddha’s description of how many ways a single teaching can be expressed.
Ajātasattu — the King of Magadha, son of the princess of Videha, whose fury at Upaka closes the discourse.
lavaṇakāradāraka — “salt-maker’s boy” — the caste-based insult the king uses against Upaka, recorded as the king’s own word rather than the discourse’s judgment.

The text

The discourse in full: the reflexive correction, the fish in the trap, the teaching on paraphrase, and the king's fury. Translation: Bhikkhu Sujato (CC0, SuttaCentral).

Upaka’s claim

§1Once the Buddha was staying near Rājagaha, on the Vulture’s Peak Mountain. Then Upaka the son of Maṇḍikā went up to the Buddha, bowed, sat down to one side, and said to him: “Sir, this is my doctrine and view: ‘Whoever goes on complaining about others without giving any reasons is reprehensible and at fault.’” “Upaka, if someone goes on complaining about others without giving a reason, they’re reprehensible and at fault. But that’s what you do, so you’re reprehensible and at fault!” “Sir, like a fish caught in a big trap just as it rises, so the Buddha caught me in a big trap of words just as I rose up.”

Limitless words for one meaning

§2“Upaka, I’ve declared: ‘This is unskillful.’ And for that there are limitless words, phrasings, and teachings of the Realized One: ‘This is another way of saying that this is unskillful.’ I’ve declared: ‘The unskillful should be given up.’ And for that there are limitless words, phrasings, and teachings of the Realized One: ‘This is another way of saying that the unskillful should be given up.’ I’ve declared that: ‘This is skillful.’ And for that there are limitless words, phrasings, and teachings of the Realized One: ‘This is another way of saying that this is skillful.’ I’ve declared: ‘The skillful should be developed.’ And for that there are limitless words, phrasings, and teachings of the Realized One: ‘This is another way of saying that the skillful should be developed.’”

Upaka before the king

§3And then Upaka son of Maṇḍikā approved and agreed with what the Buddha said. He got up from his seat, bowed, and respectfully circled the Buddha, keeping him on his right. Then he went up to King Ajātasattu of Magadha, son of the princess of Videha. He told the King of all they had discussed. But Ajātasattu became angry and upset, and said to Upaka, “How rude of this salt-maker’s boy! How scurrilous and impudent of him to imagine he could attack the Blessed One, the perfected one, the fully awakened Buddha! Get out, Upaka, go away! Don’t let me see you again.”
