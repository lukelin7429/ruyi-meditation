Why this discourse

The previous chapter divided ten words for giving into material and Dhamma. This one carries straight on with twelve more, and the chapter break falls in the middle of a run of twenty-two — another reminder that the vagga divisions are counts, not themes. What makes this half worth a separate page is that its twelve words are no longer about giving at all. Welcome, hospitality, quest, search, seeking, worship, serving guests, success, growth, treasure, accumulation, increase: the distinction has quietly expanded from what you hand over to how you receive people, what you go looking for, and what you have.

Reading guide

The teaching in one sentence

The material and Dhamma division applies not only to giving but to hospitality, seeking, worship, and everything a person accumulates.

A run of twenty-two, split by a chapter break

AN 2.141–162 is a single stretch. The template does not change at AN 2.151 and the subject does not restart; the Pāli chapter simply ends and another begins, because a vagga holds roughly ten to twelve items and this run has twenty-two.

Readers who have come through the Ones will recognize the pattern — the same thing happened at AN 1.70, where the pair of good and bad friends was split across a chapter boundary. It is worth pointing out once and then not making anything of it. The practical consequence is only that these two pages should be read together.

The four groups of twelve

The twelve fall into recognizable clusters. Welcome, hospitality, serving guests (AN 2.151–152, 157) are about receiving people. What is a welcome in the teaching? Presumably: what a visitor is actually given when they arrive, beyond a seat and a meal.

Quest, search, seeking (AN 2.153–155) are about what a person goes after. This group connects to a substantial theme elsewhere in the canon, the distinction between the noble search and the ignoble search — looking, while subject to old age and death, for things that are themselves subject to old age and death.

Worship (AN 2.156) stands alone and is the strangest of the twelve. A material worship and a worship in the teaching: offerings and flowers on one side, and on the other something the discourse does not describe. The canon elsewhere gives the content — the Buddha, near his death, is said to have told Ānanda that he is not honored by flowers and music but by a disciple who practices in accordance with the teaching. That is worship in the teaching, and it is worth having ready when this one-line discourse is read.

Success, growth, treasure, accumulation, increase (AN 2.158–162) are about what a person has and what it is doing. This is the group that lands hardest on a modern reader, because every one of the five words is one a person might use of a career.

Why extend the distinction this far

A reasonable question is what is gained by running one division through twenty-two nouns. The answer is that the distinction is being generalized rather than illustrated. Applied to gift alone it looks like a claim about generosity; applied to gift, quest, worship, treasure, and increase, it becomes a claim about a whole vocabulary — that every word a person has for wanting, getting, keeping, and honoring divides in the same way.

That is a larger and more interesting proposition, and it puts a demand on the reader that the individual entries do not. It is easy to agree that teaching someone is worth more than feeding them once. It is harder to look at the words one actually uses about one’s own life — success, growth, accumulation — and ask which of the two kinds is meant.

Using these two chapters

Read together, AN 2.141–162 make a single exercise. Take the twenty-two words, go through them slowly, and for each one ask what its Dhamma version would consist of. Some answer themselves. Several do not, and the ones that do not are the useful ones. What is a Dhamma treasure? What is a Dhamma increase?

The canon has answers to some of these elsewhere — there is a standard list of seven spiritual treasures, which AN 7.6 on this site sets out in full — but arriving at the question before receiving the answer is worth more than the answer.

Key terms

santhāra — “welcome” — literally a spreading out, as of a mat for a guest. The first of the twelve and the chapter’s name.
esanā / pariyesanā — “quest” and “search” — connecting to the canon’s distinction between the noble and the ignoble search.
pūjā — “worship, honoring” — the strangest of the twelve, whose Dhamma version is described elsewhere as practicing in accordance with the teaching rather than offering flowers and music.
dhana — “treasure, wealth” — whose Dhamma version the canon elsewhere specifies as a list of seven, set out at AN 7.6.
vuddhi / vepulla — “growth” and “increase” — two of the five words in the final cluster, all of which a modern reader might use of a career.

The text

All twelve discourses of the chapter, in order, continuing the run begun at AN 2.141. Each §N below is one complete discourse — §151 is AN 2.151, §162 is AN 2.162. Translation: Bhikkhu Sujato (CC0, SuttaCentral).

Receiving people (AN 2.151–152)

§151“There are these two kinds of welcome. What two? Welcome in things of the flesh and welcome in the teaching. These are the two kinds of welcome. The better of these two kinds of welcome is the welcome in the teaching.”

§152“There are these two kinds of hospitality. What two? Hospitality in things of the flesh and hospitality in the teaching. These are the two kinds of hospitality. The better of these two kinds of hospitality is hospitality in the teaching.”

What one goes after (AN 2.153–155)

§153“There are these two quests. What two? The quest for things of the flesh and the quest for the teaching. These are the two quests. The better of these two quests is the quest for the teaching.”

§154“There are these two searches. What two? The search for things of the flesh and the search for the teaching. These are the two searches. The better of these two searches is the search for the teaching.”

§155“There are these two kinds of seeking. What two? Seeking for things of the flesh and seeking for the teaching. These are the two kinds of seeking. The better of these two kinds of seeking is seeking for the teaching.”

Worship, and guests (AN 2.156–157)

§156“There are these two kinds of worship. What two? Worship of things of the flesh and worship of the teaching. These are the two kinds of worship. The better of these two kinds of worship is worship of the teaching.”

§157“There are these two ways of serving guests. What two? Serving guests with things of the flesh and serving guests with the teaching. These are the two ways of serving guests. The better of these two ways of serving guests is to serve them with the teaching.”

What one has (AN 2.158–162)

§158“There are, mendicants, these two successes. What two? Success in things of the flesh and success in the teaching. These are the two successes. The better of these two successes is success in the teaching.”

§159“There are, mendicants, these two kinds of growth. What two? Growth in things of the flesh and growth in the teaching. These are the two kinds of growth. The better of these two kinds of growth is growth in the teaching.”

§160“There are these two treasures. What two? The treasure of things of the flesh and the treasure of the teaching. These are the two treasures. The better of these two treasures is the treasure of the teaching.”

§161“There are these two kinds of accumulation. What two? Accumulation of things of the flesh and accumulation of the teaching. These are the two kinds of accumulation. The better of these two kinds of accumulation is the accumulation of the teaching.”

§162“There are, mendicants, these two kinds of increase. What two? Increase in things of the flesh and increase in the teaching. These are the two kinds of increase. The better of these two kinds of increase is increase in the teaching.”
