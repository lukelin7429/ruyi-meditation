Aṅguttara Nikāya · Discourse 7.46

Fires (1st)

Paṭhamaaggisutta — Mahāyaññavagga — the fifth chapter of the Sevens, closing its First Fifty

Why this discourse

AN 7.46 names seven fires in a single breath — three inner fires to be abandoned, three outer fires (or rather, three relationships figured as fires) worth tending, and an actual wood fire needing nothing more than ordinary handling — setting up the fuller treatment AN 7.47 gives the same seven immediately afterward.

Reading guide

The teaching in one sentence

Of seven things this discourse calls fires, three are the fires of greed, hate, and delusion; three are figurative fires naming people worth tending like a sacred flame — parents, one's own household, and worthy ascetics; and the seventh is simply a wood fire, included to round out the list of seven rather than to make any doctrinal point.

Three fires to abandon

The fires of greed, hate, and delusion head the list, matching this book's most familiar three-part diagnosis of what corrupts the mind, here given the specific image of something that burns rather than merely afflicts.

Three fires to tend

The next three are fires only in a figurative sense: the fire of those worthy of offerings dedicated to the gods (one's parents), a householder's fire (one's dependents), and the fire of those worthy of a religious donation (ascetics and brahmins who live well). Naming a relationship a fire worth tending, rather than a duty to discharge, carries its own claim about how seriously these relationships deserve to be taken.

One fire that is just a fire

The seventh, a plain wood fire, breaks the pattern entirely: it is not a metaphor for anything. AN 7.47's fuller version explains that this one alone should simply be fanned, watched over, extinguished, or set aside as the moment requires — the only item on the list that carries no ethical weight at all.

Key terms

aggi — “fire” — this discourse's organizing image, applied across three registers: literal inner defilement, figurative worthy relationship, and literal household fire.
rāgaggi, dosaggi, mohaggi — “the fire of greed, the fire of hate, the fire of delusion” — the three fires to be abandoned, this book's usual three root afflictions given a burning image.
āhuneyyaggi — “the fire of those worthy of offerings dedicated to the gods” — AN 7.47 identifies this as one's own parents.
gahapataggi, dakkhiṇeyyaggi — “a householder's fire, the fire of those worthy of a religious donation” — AN 7.47 identifies these as one's household and self-restrained ascetics and brahmins in turn.
kaṭṭhaggi — “a wood fire” — the seventh and only literal, non-figurative fire on the list.

The text

The discourse in full, a single bare list. Translation: Bhikkhu Sujato (CC0, SuttaCentral).

Seven fires, named

§1“Mendicants, there are these seven fires. What seven? The fires of greed, hate, delusion. The fire of those worthy of offerings dedicated to the gods. A householder’s fire. The fire of those worthy of a religious donation. And a wood fire. These are the seven fires.”
