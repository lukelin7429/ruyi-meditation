Aṅguttara Nikāya · Discourse 4.127

Incredible Things About the Realized One (1st)

Paṭhamatathāgataacchariyasutta — Bhayavagga — the thirteenth chapter of the Fours

Setting
None stated; the Bhayavagga gives no location for this discourse, and it is addressed to the mendicants directly
Speakers
The Buddha alone, addressing the mendicants
Form
Four parallel paragraphs, the first in full and the rest abridged
Length
~3 minutes to read
Northern parallel
The light at the four moments belongs to the shared biographical cycle behind DN 14 and its Āgama counterparts; this reading guide does not assert a specific matching sutra number
Difficulty
★★★☆☆ — simple to read, and it asks the reader to decide what kind of text they are holding

Why this discourse

Four events in a Buddha’s life — conception, birth, awakening, and the first teaching — and at each of them the same thing happens: an immeasurable light appears, surpassing the glory of the gods, and it reaches even the gaps between the world systems where neither sun nor moon can be seen. In that darkness the beings born there see one another for a moment, and the discourse gives them a line: So, it seems other sentient beings have been reborn here!

Reading guide

The teaching in one sentence

The four turning points of a Buddha’s life are marked by a light that reaches where nothing else does.

The four moments

They are the four hinges of the biography, and the selection is worth noticing. The being intent on awakening passes away from the host of joyful gods and is conceived, mindful and aware. He emerges from the womb, mindful and aware. He awakens to the supreme perfect awakening. He rolls forth the supreme Wheel of Dhamma.

Conception, birth, awakening, first teaching. What is absent is the fifth event a reader might expect — the final extinguishment. Elsewhere the tradition treats the parinibbāna as one of the great moments, with its own earthquake in DN 16. Here it is not among the four, and the discourse offers no reason.

One reading is that the four chosen are all beginnings: the beginning of the last life, of the body, of the awakening, and of the teaching. The light is an announcement, and there is nothing to announce at the end. This reading guide offers that as a reading, not as the text’s own explanation.

The repeated phrase sato sampajāno, mindful and aware, attached to both conception and birth, is the discourse’s one doctrinal claim about the events themselves. Everything else it says is about light.

The light and the dark between worlds

The light is described twice each time: it appears in this world with its gods, Māras, and divinities, surpassing the glory of the gods — and then, in the sentence that makes the discourse memorable, it appears in the lokantarika, the intervals between the world systems.

Those intervals are described precisely: boundless gloom, so utterly dark that the luminosity of the moon and the sun, so mighty and powerful, makes no impression there. The Pāli piles the words up — aghā asaṁvutā andhakārā andhakāratimisā, open, unenclosed, dark, dark with the darkness of blackness.

It is an image of maximum isolation, and the cosmology behind it is that the world systems are like bowls set side by side, with unlit space in the joins where certain beings are reborn.

The point of the image is what happens next, and it is not a display of power. And the sentient beings reborn there recognize each other by that light: ‘So, it seems other sentient beings have been reborn here!’

That is the whole miracle as the discourse chooses to describe it. Not that the light is bright, but that beings who did not know anyone else was there find out. The line is given to them in direct speech, which the discourse did not have to do.

What kind of text this is

It should be said plainly that this is mythological material and that reading it well means neither literalizing it nor explaining it away.

The literalizing reading treats the light as a reportable physical event and turns the discourse into a claim about astronomy. The explaining-away reading treats it as a metaphor for the spread of the teaching and quietly discards everything specific in it — the intervals, the beings, the line they speak.

Both lose the same thing, which is the discourse’s actual subject. A tradition chose to mark the four moments of its founder’s life by describing a light that reaches the loneliest place it could imagine and lets the beings there see each other. What that says about how the tradition understood its own arrival is available without settling any question about whether it happened.

This is also, as it happens, the reading that makes the discourse teachable to a mixed audience. Nobody has to agree about cosmology to see what is being valued.

Where it sits in the canon

The material belongs to a well-known cycle. DN 14, the discourse on the great legend, carries a long version of the wonders surrounding a Buddha’s conception and birth, with the same light and the same intervals between the worlds. MN 123 gives Ānanda a list of incredible and amazing qualities of the Buddha built from the same materials.

What the Aṅguttara does with it is what the Aṅguttara does with everything: reduce it to a numbered set. Four moments, one light, and the whole cycle compressed into something a reciter can hold.

The phrase that titles it, acchariya abbhuta, is a fixed pair — wonderful and unprecedented — and it is about to be used three more times in this chapter, of a Buddha again in AN 4.128, of Ānanda in AN 4.129, and of a wheel-turning monarch in AN 4.130. The chapter that began with fears ends with marvels.

Reading it next to AN 4.128

The pairing is the most interesting thing about this discourse. AN 4.127 gives four cosmic marvels. AN 4.128 gives four marvels of an entirely different kind: people love clinging, and yet they listen when a Buddha teaches non-clinging.

Same title, same number, and the second is composed entirely of ordinary human behavior. Whether or not the pairing was deliberate, the effect of reading them in order is a deliberate-looking descent from the intervals between the worlds to a room with people in it.

If only one of the two is to be taught, the second is the more useful. If both are taught, they should be taught in this order, because the second lands as a correction of the first.

Key terms

acchariya abbhuta — “incredible and amazing” — a fixed pair, wonderful and unprecedented; the tag on all four discourses that close this chapter.
lokantarika — “between the worlds” — the unlit intervals between world systems, described here as places where sun and moon make no impression.
bodhisatta — “being intent on awakening” — the term for the Buddha before the awakening; here passing away from the host of joyful gods.
sato sampajāno — “mindful and aware” — attached to both the conception and the birth, and the discourse’s only doctrinal claim about the events.
dhammacakka — “Wheel of Dhamma” — rolled forth at the fourth moment; the first teaching, treated here as an event on the same scale as the awakening.

The text

The discourse in full: the four moments, with the light described at length the first time and abridged after. Translation: Bhikkhu Sujato (CC0, SuttaCentral).

The conception

§1“Mendicants, with the appearance of a Realized One, a perfected one, a fully awakened Buddha, four incredible and amazing things appear. What four? When the being intent on awakening passes away from the host of joyful gods, he’s conceived in his mother’s womb, mindful and aware. And then—in this world with its gods, Māras, and divinities, this population with its ascetics and brahmins, gods and humans—an immeasurable, magnificent light appears, surpassing the glory of the gods. Even in the boundless gloom of interstellar space—so utterly dark that even the luminosity of the moon and the sun, so mighty and powerful, makes no impression—an immeasurable, magnificent light appears, surpassing the glory of the gods. And the sentient beings reborn there recognize each other by that light: ‘So, it seems other sentient beings have been reborn here!’ This is the first incredible and amazing thing that appears with the appearance of a Realized One.

The birth

§2Furthermore, the being intent on awakening emerges from his mother’s womb, mindful and aware. And then … an immeasurable, magnificent light appears … even in the boundless gloom of interstellar space … This is the second incredible and amazing thing that appears with the appearance of a Realized One.

The awakening

§3Furthermore, the Realized One awakens to the supreme perfect awakening. And then … an immeasurable, magnificent light appears … even in the boundless gloom of interstellar space … This is the third incredible and amazing thing that appears with the appearance of a Realized One.

The rolling forth of the Wheel

§4Furthermore, the Realized One rolls forth the supreme Wheel of Dhamma. And then … an immeasurable, magnificent light appears … even in the boundless gloom of interstellar space … This is the fourth incredible and amazing thing that appears with the appearance of a Realized One. With the appearance of a Realized One, the perfected one, the fully awakened Buddha, these four incredible and amazing things appear.”
