Aṅguttara Nikāya · Discourse 4.51

Overflowing Merit

Paṭhamapuññābhisandasutta — Puññābhisandavagga — the sixth chapter of the Fours

Setting
Sāvatthī — the location given in a single word, without the usual full formula
Speakers
The Buddha alone, addressing the mendicants
Form
Four overflowings, an ocean simile, and two verses
Length
~2 minutes to read
Northern parallel
The puññābhisanda set and the ocean simile appear across the Chinese Āgamas; this reading guide does not assert a specific matching sutra number
Difficulty
★★★☆☆ — a claim about giving whose mechanism is easy to miss

Why this discourse

Four streams of merit, one for each of the four requisites — and the condition on all four is the same: the merit is limitless when the mendicant, while using the gift, enters a limitless immersion of heart. The donor’s return is not fixed by the gift. It is fixed by what the recipient does with it, which makes this a discourse about a relationship rather than a transaction.

Reading guide

The teaching in one sentence

What a gift is worth to the giver depends on what the receiver does while using it.

Abhisanda: the word for a flow

Puññābhisanda is a stream, flow, or overflowing of merit. The image is fluid from the start, and the discourse spends its second half developing it: the ocean, its incalculable water, and the rivers that reach it.

That choice of image is not decorative. A stream is continuous, arrives from elsewhere, and accumulates. Merit on this account is not a set of discrete credits but something that keeps arriving, which is why the discourse can move from four occasions of giving to an immeasurable mass without any step of multiplication.

The condition

Each of the four is stated the same way: when a mendicant enters and remains in a limitless immersion of heart (appamāṇa cetosamādhi) while using a robe, or eating almsfood, or using lodgings, or using medicines, the overflowing of merit for the donor is limitless.

The mechanism is worth stating plainly because it is easy to read past. Nothing is said about the size of the gift, the wealth of the donor, or the intention behind it. The variable is the recipient’s state, and specifically a state entered while the gift is in use.

Appamāṇa, limitless or immeasurable, is the technical term used of the four immeasurables — loving-kindness, compassion, rejoicing, equanimity — radiated without boundary in all directions. The commentarial tradition reads the phrase that way here, and the reading fits: a limitless state produces a limitless result because the result takes its measure from the state.

That is the logic of the whole discourse. The donor’s merit is unbounded not because giving is inherently unbounded but because it has been attached to something that has no boundary.

What follows for the giver

Two things, and they pull in opposite directions.

The first is encouraging. An ordinary gift — a meal, a robe — can carry a result out of all proportion to its size, so poverty is no barrier to the practice. The collection says this repeatedly and this discourse gives the reason.

The second is sobering, and it is the part usually left out. If the return depends on the recipient, then the donor does not control the outcome and cannot manufacture it by giving more. What the donor controls is who they give to, which is why the collection attends so closely to the worthiness of the recipient — the field of merit of AN 4.34.

It is worth saying that this can be read uncharitably, as a system for directing lay wealth to monastics. The honest response is that the criterion cuts both ways: it makes the value of what the Saṅgha receives depend entirely on how its members actually practise, which is a demanding condition to have written into the economics of one’s own support.

The ocean

It’s not easy to say how many gallons, how many hundreds, thousands, hundreds of thousands of gallons there are. The point of the simile is not that the ocean is large but that it is not countable in the units one would naturally reach for.

That is a precise thing to say about merit, and more careful than ‘very much’. The claim is that the quantity is of the wrong kind for the question, which is also how the discourse describes the noble disciple’s merit: simply reckoned as an incalculable, immeasurable, great mass.

The verse then supplies the second half of the image. Rivers are many and are used by many people, and all of them reach the sea. Streams of merit reach the giver the way rivers reach the ocean — from many separate sources, continuously, and without any of them needing to be tracked.

Where it sits in the chapter

The Puññābhisandavagga is the Fours’ lay chapter, and this discourse opens it. What follows is a sequence about householders: the four kinds of marriage in AN 4.53 and 4.54, the equality of Nakula’s parents in AN 4.55, Suppavāsā and Anāthapiṇḍika giving food, and finally the four things that constitute lay practice in AN 4.60.

AN 4.52, immediately next, gives a different four — confidence in the Buddha, the teaching, and the Saṅgha, and ethical conduct — under the same heading of overflowing merit. Read together the pair says that merit flows both from what one gives and from what one is.

Key terms

puññābhisanda — “overflowing of merit” — a stream or flow; something continuous that keeps arriving rather than a set of discrete credits.
appamāṇa cetosamādhi — “limitless immersion of heart” — the technical term of the four immeasurables, radiated without boundary; the condition on all four overflowings.
cattāro paccayā — the four requisites — robes, almsfood, lodgings, and medicines; one stream of merit for each.
asaṅkheyya appameyya — “incalculable, immeasurable” — the description of both the ocean’s water and the disciple’s merit: of the wrong kind for the question.
puññakkhetta — “field of merit” — not used here, but the concept this discourse supplies the reason for: the donor controls who they give to, not the size of the return.

The text

The discourse in full: the four overflowings, the ocean simile, and the verses. The ellipses are the Pāli’s own abbreviation. Translation: Bhikkhu Sujato (CC0, SuttaCentral).

Four kinds of overflowing merit

§1At Sāvatthī. “Mendicants, there are these four kinds of overflowing merit, overflowing goodness. They nurture happiness and are conducive to heaven, ripening in happiness and leading to heaven. They lead to what is likable, desirable, agreeable, to welfare and happiness. What four? When a mendicant enters and remains in a limitless immersion of heart while using a robe, the overflowing of merit for the donor is limitless …

§2When a mendicant enters and remains in a limitless immersion of heart while eating almsfood, the overflowing of merit for the donor is limitless … When a mendicant enters and remains in a limitless immersion of heart while using lodgings, the overflowing of merit for the donor is limitless … When a mendicant enters and remains in a limitless immersion of heart while using medicines and supplies for the sick, the overflowing of merit for the donor is limitless … These are the four kinds of overflowing merit, overflowing goodness. They nurture happiness and are conducive to heaven, ripening in happiness and leading to heaven. They lead to what is likable, desirable, agreeable, to welfare and happiness.

Incalculable

§3When a noble disciple has these four kinds of overflowing merit and goodness, it’s not easy to grasp how much merit they have by saying that this is the extent of their overflowing merit … that leads to happiness. It’s simply reckoned as an incalculable, immeasurable, great mass of merit.

§4It’s like trying to grasp how much water is in the ocean. It’s not easy to say how many gallons, how many hundreds, thousands, hundreds of thousands of gallons there are. It’s simply reckoned as an incalculable, immeasurable, great mass of water. In the same way, when a noble disciple has these four kinds of overflowing merit it’s simply reckoned as an incalculable, immeasurable, great mass of merit.

The verses

§5Hosts of people use the rivers, and though the rivers are many, all reach the great deep, the boundless ocean, the cruel sea that’s home to precious gems.

§6In the same way, when a person gives food, drink, and clothes; and they’re a giver of beds, seats, and mats—the streams of merit reach that astute person, as the rivers bring their waters to the sea.”
