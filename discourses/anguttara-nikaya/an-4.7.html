Aṅguttara Nikāya · Discourse 4.7

Grace

Sobhanasutta — Bhaṇḍagāmavagga — the first chapter of the Fours

Setting
None stated; the discourse continues from AN 4.1, whose setting at Wares Village in the Vajjian country is understood to hold
Speakers
The Buddha alone, addressing the mendicants
Form
One statement, the four assemblies named, and two verses
Length
~1 minute to read
Northern parallel
The fourfold assembly is standard across the Chinese Āgamas; this reading guide does not assert a specific matching sutra number
Difficulty
★☆☆☆☆ — very short, and important for what it takes for granted

Why this discourse

Who makes the community look good? The discourse answers: a monk, a nun, a layman, a laywoman — the same five qualities required of each, listed once and applied to all four without variation. Nothing in the discourse is argued, and that is the point. The equality of the four assemblies in this respect is stated as though it were obvious, and the list of qualities is identical in every case.

Reading guide

The teaching in one sentence

Four kinds of person grace the community, and the qualification is the same for all four of them.

The four assemblies

Bhikkhu, bhikkhunī, upāsaka, upāsikā — monk, nun, layman, laywoman. This is the standard fourfold division of the Buddhist community, and it appears constantly in the collection.

What the discourse does with it is the notable part. The qualities are stated once, at the head, and then the four are simply listed. There is no separate standard for lay people, none for women, and no suggestion that one of the four graces the Saṅgha more than another. Where the collection elsewhere distinguishes the duties of the four, here it declines to.

The five qualities

Competent (viyatta), educated (vinīta), self-assured (visārada), learned (bahussuta), having memorized the teachings (dhammadhara) — and practising in line with the teaching.

The list is worth comparing with AN 4.6, which has just been read. There the point was that learning is worthless without practice; here learning and practice are both present, and the discourse adds three social qualities the earlier one did not name. Competence, training, and self-assurance are qualities of how a person carries themselves in company.

That is consistent with what the discourse is about. It is not describing who benefits most from the teaching but who graces the community — who makes it creditable to those looking at it from outside. Composure in public is relevant to that in a way it is not to liberation.

‘Grace’ and what it translates

Sobhati is to shine, to be beautiful, to look well. The noun form appears in the closing line: these four are the graces of the Saṅgha, saṅghasobhanā. Sujato’s ‘grace’ keeps both the verbal and the ornamental sense, which matters because the Pāli image is decorative — these people are what the community is adorned by.

The image recurs in AN 4.6’s closing verse, where the learned and ethical disciple is a pendant of finest gold. Two consecutive discourses reach for ornament as the figure for a person of quality in a group.

What the verses add

The second verse redistributes the qualities slightly: a monk accomplished in ethics, a learned nun, a faithful layman, a faithful laywoman. Where the prose gave all five qualities to all four, the verse gives each a characteristic one.

It would be a mistake to read this as the real ranking beneath the prose’s politeness. Verses in the Aṅguttara are compressed for metre and frequently vary the prose rather than qualifying it. But it is honest to notice that ethics goes to the monk, learning to the nun, and faith to both lay figures — and that the distribution matches what the wider collection tends to expect of each. The prose is the more egalitarian statement, and it is the one with authority here.

Key terms

sobhati / saṅghasobhanā — “to shine, to look well” and “graces of the Saṅgha” — the image is decorative: these people are what the community is adorned by.
bhikkhu, bhikkhunī, upāsaka, upāsikā — monk, nun, layman, laywoman — the fourfold assembly, given here with a single shared standard.
viyatta — “competent, accomplished” — the first quality, and one of three that describe how a person carries themselves in company.
bahussuta — “learned, of much learning” — carried straight over from AN 4.6, where its limits were set out.
dhammadhara — “one who has memorized the teachings” — literally a bearer of the teaching, the role on which oral transmission depended.

The text

The discourse in full: the statement, the four assemblies, and the two verses. Translation: Bhikkhu Sujato (CC0, SuttaCentral).

Four who grace the Saṅgha

§1“Mendicants, these four who are competent, educated, self-assured, and learned, who have memorized the teachings and practice in line with the teaching, grace the Saṅgha. What four? A monk, a nun, a layman, and a laywoman. These four who are competent, educated, self-assured, and learned, who have memorized the teachings and practice in line with the teaching, grace the Saṅgha.

The verses

§2Whoever is competent and self-assured, learned, a memorizer of the teachings, who lives in line with the teaching—such a one is said to grace the Saṅgha.

§3A monk accomplished in ethics, and a learned nun, a faithful layman, and a faithful laywoman, too: these grace the Saṅgha, they are the graces of the Saṅgha.”
