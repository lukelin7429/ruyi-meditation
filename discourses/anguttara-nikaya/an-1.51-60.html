Aṅguttara Nikāya · Discourses 1.51–60

A Finger-Snap

Accharāsaṅghātavagga — the sixth chapter of the Ones

Setting
None stated; the chapter continues from AN 1.1, whose setting at Sāvatthī is understood to hold
Speakers
The Buddha alone, addressing the mendicants
Form
Four short groups — the radiant mind restated with a consequence, three entries on the briefest possible moment of love, two on mind preceding all things, and three on negligence, diligence, and laziness
Length
~3 minutes to read
Northern parallel
The mind-precedes formula opens the Chinese Dharmapada collections as it does the Pāli, and the diligence material is widespread in the Āgamas; this reading guide does not assert a specific matching sutra number
Difficulty
★★☆☆☆ — short and quotable throughout, with one translation choice in AN 1.56–57 worth stopping over

Why this discourse

This is the most quotable chapter in the Ones, and unusually for the Ones it is not built on a single template. It reopens the radiant-mind statement from the previous chapter and attaches a consequence to it; it makes the case that a moment of goodwill lasting no longer than a finger-snap already counts for something; it states in prose the sentence that opens the Dhammapada in verse; and it closes on the three qualities — negligence, diligence, laziness — that determine which way everything else tips. For a class working through the Ones, this is the chapter most likely to be remembered a year later.

Reading guide

The teaching in one sentence

The mind is radiant and comes first — so the smallest genuine moment of goodwill already counts, and diligence, not talent, decides the direction of travel.

The radiant mind, now with a consequence (AN 1.51–52)

The chapter opens by repeating AN 1.49–50 word for word and then adding a sentence to each. The unlearned ordinary person does not truly understand that the mind is radiant and corrupted by incoming corruptions — and therefore, the Buddha says, has no development of the mind. The learned noble disciple does understand it, and therefore has development of the mind.

This addition is the closest thing the canon gives to a gloss on the radiant-mind statement, and it is worth noticing what kind of gloss it is. It is not metaphysical. Nothing is said about what the radiance is, whether it persists, or what it is made of. What is said is practical and almost circular: recognizing this about the mind is what makes development possible, and not recognizing it is why development does not happen. The radiance is not presented as a possession to be enjoyed but as a fact whose recognition changes what a person will attempt.

This is also the honest answer to give when a student asks whether the Pāli canon teaches buddha-nature. In these four entries it teaches something narrower: that the mind’s corruptions are incoming rather than intrinsic, and that knowing this is the precondition of training. Whether that is the seed of the later doctrine or merely its nearest ancestor is a question worth leaving open in front of a class rather than settling for them.

A finger-snap of love (AN 1.53–55)

Three entries make the same claim with three different verbs: if a mendicant cultivates, or develops, or focuses on a mind of love for no longer than the time it takes to snap the fingers, they are already called one who does not lack absorption, who follows the Teacher’s instructions, who responds to advice, and who does not eat the country’s alms in vain.

That last phrase is the sharp one, and it is easy to miss how sharp. A monastic lives entirely on food given by working households. The anxiety of eating that food without having earned it runs all through the monastic literature. What this entry says is that the debt is discharged — not fully repaid, but no longer defaulted on — by a moment of goodwill briefer than a thought. And then it adds the line that keeps it from being an excuse: how much more so those who make much of it.

For lay students this is one of the most usable entries in the collection, because it names a floor rather than a ceiling. Almost every practical objection people raise about practice — no time, no quiet, no aptitude, an interrupted day — is an objection to the ceiling. This entry declines to argue about the ceiling and puts the floor at a finger-snap.

Mind precedes all things (AN 1.56–57)

Sujato renders these two entries with the word “intention”: intention shapes all phenomena that are unskillful, and all that are skillful; intention arises first and the rest follow behind. The Pāli behind “intention shapes” is manopubbaṅgamā — literally, having mind as forerunner — and any reader who knows the Dhammapada will recognize it instantly, because it is the first word of the first verse of that collection.

The translation choice is defensible and worth flagging rather than hiding. Mano here is not intellect but the mind as the initiating faculty, and Sujato’s “intention” brings out that it is the mind’s directedness, not mere awareness, that goes first. Older renderings keep the literal image — “mind precedes all things” — and lose the emphasis. Showing students the same phrase in the Dhammapada page linked below, in a different translator’s hands, is a quick and concrete lesson in what translation does.

Structurally, these two entries are the hinge of the chapter. If mind goes first, then what the last three entries describe — negligence and its opposites — is not a side issue about willpower. It is a description of what happens at the front of the process.

Negligence, diligence, laziness (AN 1.58–60)

The chapter ends on pamāda, appamāda, and kosajja. Negligence gives rise to unskillful qualities and makes skillful ones decline; diligence does the reverse; laziness does what negligence does. The entries return to the exact template of AN 1.11–20 — one thing that most reliably tips a mind one way — applied now not to a single hindrance but to a person’s whole trajectory.

Appamāda is worth naming carefully because it is not quite “diligence” in the sense of working hard. The word is a negative: not-negligent, not-heedless, not letting things slide. It describes someone who does not lose track, rather than someone who strains. That distinction matters, and the canon gives it enormous weight — appamāda is the subject of the Buddha’s reported last words. Reading these three entries beside the Dhammapada’s chapter on diligence, which the same tradition placed second in that collection, shows how much was hung on one negative adjective.

Key terms

accharāsaṅghāta — “a finger-snap” — the canonical unit for the briefest possible interval, and the source of this chapter’s Pāli name.
mettācitta — “a mind of love” — the object of the finger-snap entries. Three verbs are used of it across AN 1.53–55: cultivating, developing, and focusing on.
arittajjhāna — “not lacking absorption” — the striking claim made about someone who has managed only a moment of love. Literally, their jhāna is not empty.
manopubbaṅgama — “having mind as forerunner” — the phrase behind Sujato’s “intention shapes all phenomena,” and the opening words of the Dhammapada.
appamāda / pamāda / kosajja — diligence, negligence, and laziness. Appamāda is a negative — not letting things slide — rather than a word for strenuous effort, and it is the subject of the Buddha’s reported last words.

The text

All ten discourses of the chapter, in order. Each §N below is one complete discourse — §51 is AN 1.51, §60 is AN 1.60. Translation: Bhikkhu Sujato (CC0, SuttaCentral).

The radiant mind, understood or not (AN 1.51–52)

§51“This mind, mendicants, is radiant. But it is corrupted by passing corruptions. An unlearned ordinary person does not truly understand this. So I say that the unlearned ordinary person has no development of the mind.”

§52“This mind, mendicants, is radiant. And it is freed from passing corruptions. A learned noble disciple truly understands this. So I say that the learned noble disciple has development of the mind.”

A finger-snap of love (AN 1.53–55)

§53“If, mendicants, a mendicant cultivates a mind of love even as long as a finger-snap, they’re called a mendicant who does not lack absorption, who follows the Teacher’s instructions, who responds to advice, and who does not eat the country’s alms in vain. How much more so those who make much of it!”

§54“If, mendicants, a mendicant develops a mind of love even as long as a finger-snap, they’re called a mendicant who does not lack absorption, who follows the Teacher’s instructions, who responds to advice, and who does not eat the country’s alms in vain. How much more so those who make much of it!”

§55“If, mendicants, a mendicant focuses on a mind of love even as long as a finger-snap, they’re called a mendicant who does not lack absorption, who follows the Teacher’s instructions, who responds to advice, and who does not eat the country’s alms in vain. How much more so those who make much of it!”

Mind goes first (AN 1.56–57)

§56“Mendicants, intention shapes all phenomena whatsoever that are unskillful, part of the unskillful, on the side of the unskillful. Intention is the first of those phenomena to arise, and unskillful phenomena follow right behind.”

§57“Mendicants, intention shapes all phenomena whatsoever that are skillful, part of the skillful, on the side of the skillful. Intention is the first of those phenomena to arise, and skillful phenomena follow right behind.”

Negligence, diligence, laziness (AN 1.58–60)

§58“Mendicants, I do not see a single thing that gives rise to unskillful qualities, or makes skillful qualities decline like negligence. When you’re negligent, unskillful qualities arise and skillful qualities decline.”

§59“Mendicants, I do not see a single thing that gives rise to skillful qualities, or makes unskillful qualities decline like diligence. When you’re diligent, skillful qualities arise and unskillful qualities decline.”

§60“Mendicants, I do not see a single thing that gives rise to unskillful qualities, or makes skillful qualities decline like laziness. When you’re lazy, unskillful qualities arise and skillful qualities decline.”
