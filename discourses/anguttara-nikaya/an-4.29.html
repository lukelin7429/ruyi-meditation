Aṅguttara Nikāya · Discourse 4.29

Footprints of the Dhamma

Dhammapadasutta — Uruvelavagga — the third chapter of the Fours

Setting
None stated; the Uruvelavagga gives no location for this discourse, and it is addressed to the mendicants directly
Speakers
The Buddha alone, addressing the mendicants
Form
A bare list of four inside a repeated frame, and one verse
Length
under a minute to read
Northern parallel
The four dhammapada recur across the Chinese Āgamas; this reading guide does not assert a specific matching sutra number
Difficulty
★★☆☆☆ — four words in a large frame, and the frame is borrowed

Why this discourse

Four items only: contentment, good will, right mindfulness, right immersion. They arrive inside exactly the frame AN 4.28 used for the noble traditions — primordial, long-standing, traditional, ancient, uncorrupted — and the discourse says nothing further about any of them. The interest is in the selection, and in the word dhammapada, which is more concrete than it looks.

Reading guide

The teaching in one sentence

Four things — contentment, good will, mindfulness, immersion — are where the teaching has left its mark, and nobody sensible disputes them.

What a dhammapada is

Pada is a foot, a footprint, a track, and by extension a step or a line of verse. Sujato’s ‘footprints of the Dhamma’ takes the concrete sense, and it is the right choice for this discourse: these are the places where the teaching shows.

The same word gives the Dhammapada its name, where it is usually rendered by way of the ‘verse’ sense. Knowing both senses is useful. A pada is what something leaves behind, and a line of verse is what a teaching leaves behind in memory.

AN 3.2, in the Threes, said that wisdom shines in its traces — apadāna, from the same root. This discourse names four traces and does not argue for them.

The selection

Contentment (anabhijjhā, literally non-covetousness), good will (abyāpāda, non-ill-will), right mindfulness, and right immersion. Note that the first two are stated negatively in the Pāli and positively in the English.

The set is a compressed path. The first two are the negations of the first two unwholesome roots as they show up in conduct; the second two are the last two factors of the eightfold path. So: what must not be there, and what must be. Greed and hatred absent, mindfulness and immersion present.

Delusion, the third root, is not on the list, and its absence is worth noticing. The counter to delusion is wisdom, which arises from the two positive members rather than standing alongside them. The list is arranged so that the fourth item is the one from which the missing thing comes.

The borrowed frame

Every word of the surrounding formula is AN 4.28’s. Primordial, long-standing, traditional, ancient; uncorrupted since the beginning, not being corrupted now, not going to be; sensible ascetics and brahmins do not look down on them.

Two discourses in a row, the same frame, different contents. This is the Aṅguttara’s method in its clearest form, and the two lists are genuinely different: AN 4.28 gave practices of the monastic life, this gives qualities of any mind. The frame asserts of both that they are the durable part.

The claim that sensible ascetics and brahmins do not look down on them is the most interesting element. It is an appeal to cross-sectarian agreement: whatever else the schools dispute, no reputable teacher of any of them disparages contentment, good will, mindfulness, or composure. AN 4.30, the next discourse, will take that claim and turn it into a challenge.

The abbreviation

The Pāli here is abbreviated almost to nothing — the four are named once and the frame is restated, with the intermediate expansion left out entirely. Sujato’s translation preserves the gaps.

In performance each of the four would have been expanded with its own definition, as AN 4.28’s items are. What survives on the page is a skeleton. That is worth saying plainly rather than treating the brevity as significant in itself: this discourse is short because it was written down short, not because it was taught short.

The verse

One verse, four lines, and it converts the list into an instruction: live with contentment, and a heart of good will, mindful, with unified mind, serene within.

That is the whole discourse made usable, and it is the part worth memorizing. The four items in order, in the imperative, with one addition — ajjhattaṁ susamāhita, well composed within — which restates the fourth item as an interior condition rather than an attainment.

Key terms

dhammapada — “footprint of the Dhamma” — pada is a foot or track, and by extension a step or a line of verse. Here the concrete sense: where the teaching shows.
anabhijjhā — “contentment”, literally non-covetousness — stated negatively in the Pāli, positively in the English.
abyāpāda — “good will”, literally non-ill-will — the second of the pair of absences that opens the list.
sammāsati / sammāsamādhi — “right mindfulness” and “right immersion” — the last two factors of the eightfold path, and the two presences that close the list.
susamāhita — “well composed” — the verse’s addition, restating immersion as an interior condition rather than an attainment.

The text

The discourse in full: the four footprints and the verse. The gaps are the Pāli’s own abbreviation. Translation: Bhikkhu Sujato (CC0, SuttaCentral).

Four footprints of the Dhamma

§1“Mendicants, these four footprints of the Dhamma are primordial, long-standing, traditional, and ancient. They are uncorrupted, as they have been since the beginning. They’re not being corrupted now nor will they be. Sensible ascetics and brahmins don’t look down on them. What four? Contentment, good will, right mindfulness, and right immersion.

§2These four footprints of the Dhamma are primordial, long-standing, traditional, and ancient. They are uncorrupted, as they have been since the beginning. They’re not being corrupted now nor will they be. Sensible ascetics and brahmins don’t look down on them.

The verse

§3You should live with contentment, and a heart of good will, mindful, with unified mind, serene within.”
