Aṅguttara Nikāya · Discourse 3.135

A Friend

Mittasutta — Yodhājīvavagga — the fourteenth chapter of the Threes

Why this discourse

Three tests for a friend worth keeping, and every one of them is a test of difficulty: what is hard to give, what is hard to do, what is hard to bear. A friendship that has never been tested by any of the three has not yet shown what it actually is.

Reading guide

The teaching in one sentence

Associate with a friend who gives what is hard to give, does what is hard to do, and bears what is hard to bear.

A definition built entirely on difficulty

All three qualities share the same shape: something dujjaha, hard to give up; dukkara, hard to do; dukkhama, hard to bear. A friend who is easy to be around when nothing costs anything has not yet met any of the discourse’s three tests. The definition is deliberately built so that it cannot be satisfied by pleasantness alone.

Giving what is hard to give

Not ordinary generosity, but the kind of giving that costs the giver something real — time, comfort, standing, or an outcome they wanted for themselves. A friend who gives easily, from surplus, has not yet demonstrated this quality; a friend who gives from what would otherwise be kept has.

Doing what is hard to do

A second, distinct register: not sacrifice but effort, undertaking something unpleasant, risky, or simply exhausting on another’s behalf, where an easier option was available and declined.

Bearing what is hard to bear

The third is neither giving nor doing but enduring — staying present through something difficult rather than withdrawing from it. A friend who disappears when a situation becomes uncomfortable has failed this test specifically, even if they never fail the first two.

Why the discourse tests for difficulty rather than warmth

Warmth is cheap to perform and easy to mistake for friendship. Difficulty is not; a person will only give what is hard to give, do what is hard to do, and bear what is hard to bear for someone they actually value, which is precisely why the discourse uses difficulty rather than affection as its measure. It is a test that cannot be faked by someone who is merely pleasant to have around.

Key terms

dujjaha — “hard to give up” — what the first quality of a worthwhile friend gives anyway.
dukkara — “hard to do” — what the second quality undertakes anyway.
dukkhama — “hard to bear” — what the third quality endures anyway.
mitta — “friend” — the discourse’s subject, defined entirely by what difficulty a friend is willing to meet.
bhajitabba — “should be associated with” — the discourse’s one instruction, aimed at the listener’s own choice of company.

The text

The discourse in full, a single instruction. Translation: Bhikkhu Sujato (CC0, SuttaCentral).

A friend worth keeping

§1“Mendicants, you should associate with a friend who has three factors. What three? They give what is hard to give, they do what is hard to do, and they bear what is hard to bear. You should associate with a friend who has these three factors.”
