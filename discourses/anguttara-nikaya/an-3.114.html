Aṅguttara Nikāya · Discourse 3.114

Rare

Dullabhasutta — Āpāyikavagga — the twelfth chapter of the Threes

Setting
None stated; the discourse continues from AN 3.1, whose setting at Sāvatthī is understood to hold
Speakers
The Buddha alone, addressing the mendicants
Form
A statement and a list of three
Length
under a minute to read
Northern parallel
Comparable lists of what is rare in the world are preserved in the Chinese Āgamas; this reading guide does not assert a specific matching sutra number
Difficulty
★☆☆☆☆ — four lines with one surprise

Why this discourse

Three individuals whose appearance in the world is rare: a fully awakened Buddha; somebody who teaches the teaching a Buddha proclaimed; and — third, in the same list — an individual who is grateful and thankful. The first two are what anyone would expect. The third is a person who says thank you.

Reading guide

The teaching in one sentence

The three rarest things in the world are a Buddha, somebody who can teach what a Buddha taught, and somebody who is grateful.

The escalation, and where it stops

The list is built to make the third item land. A Buddha is rare by definition; the canon elsewhere counts the eons between them. A competent teacher of that teaching is rare in an obvious way — it requires the teaching to exist and somebody to have understood it well enough to pass it on.

And then, in the same breath and with no change of register: an individual who is grateful and thankful.

The effect depends entirely on the reader noticing that the third item is not of the same order. Nobody has to wait an eon for a grateful person, and there is no institution required to produce one. The discourse puts them on the same list anyway.

Kataññū katavedī

The pair is standard and both halves are worth having. Kataññū is literally “knowing what was done” — recognizing that something was done for you. Katavedī is “making known what was done” — acknowledging it, declaring it, acting on it.

So the Pāli separates two things English runs together. The first is a perception: noticing that a benefit came from somewhere. The second is a response: making that visible. A person can have the first without the second, and the discourse requires both.

The canon uses this pair often, and AN 3.31’s treatment of parents rests on it: the reason parents are called Brahmā is that they raise them, nurture them, and show them the world, which is a statement about what was done. Gratitude in this tradition is treated as accuracy about the past.

Why it is rare

The discourse gives no reason, and it is worth letting a class supply one before offering any.

The most defensible answer follows from the vocabulary. If gratitude requires knowing what was done, then it requires a kind of attention to one’s own history that most people do not maintain. Benefits received early, received continuously, or received from people one dislikes are all easy not to notice, and what is not noticed cannot be acknowledged.

That makes the rarity claim considerably more plausible than it first sounds. The discourse is not saying that few people ever say thank you. It is saying that few people reliably see what was done for them, which is a different and harder thing.

Using it

This discourse takes thirty seconds and it is one of the best openings in the collection for a lay audience, because the surprise does the work. Read the three items and stop.

The second use is for teachers. The list places the person who teaches the Dhamma between a Buddha and a grateful person, which is a striking piece of company. Whatever else it says, it says that transmitting something well is closer in kind to ordinary human decency than to attainment.

Key terms

dullabha — “rare, hard to find” — said of all three appearances, without distinguishing between them.
kataññū — “knowing what was done” — the perceptual half of gratitude: noticing that a benefit came from somewhere.
katavedī — “making known what was done” — the responsive half: acknowledging it and acting on it. The discourse requires both.
tathāgatappavedita dhammavinaya — “the teaching and training proclaimed by a Realized One” — what the second rare individual teaches, which is why they depend on the first.
pātubhāva — “appearance, manifestation” — the word applied equally to a Buddha and to a grateful person.

The text

The discourse in full. Translation: Bhikkhu Sujato (CC0, SuttaCentral).

§1“Mendicants, the appearance of three individuals is rare in the world. What three? A Realized One, a perfected one, a fully awakened Buddha. A teacher of the teaching and training proclaimed by a Realized One. An individual who is grateful and thankful. The appearance of these three individuals is rare in the world.”
