Aṅguttara Nikāya · Discourse 4.178

Billabong

Jambālīsutta — Cetanāvagga — the eighteenth chapter of the Fours

Setting
None stated; the Cetanāvagga gives no location for this discourse, and it is addressed to the mendicants directly
Speakers
The Buddha alone, addressing the mendicants
Form
Four individuals, each carried by a simile, in two matched pairs
Length
~3 minutes to read
Northern parallel
The billabong simile for readiness to break through is distinctive to this discourse among the texts checked for this guide; this reading guide does not assert a specific matching sutra number
Difficulty
★★★☆☆ — two vivid similes doing exactly the same logical work

Why this discourse

Four mendicants, all in a peaceful release of the heart, all focused on the same objects — the cessation of identity, the smashing of ignorance. What separates them is not the attainment or the focus but whether the mind leaps forth, gains confidence, settles down, and becomes decided. A hand smeared with glue sticks to a branch; a clean hand does not. A billabong sealed against rain for years will not break its banks; one thrown open to a downpour will.

Reading guide

The teaching in one sentence

Two mendicants can share the identical meditative state and the identical object of attention, and differ only in whether the mind commits — and that difference alone predicts whether the breakthrough follows.

Four individuals, two similes, one structure

Each of the four sections repeats the same three moves: a mendicant in a peaceful release of the heart focuses on something; the mind either does, or does not, leap forth, gain confidence, settle down, and become decided; and a simile confirms the expected outcome.

The first pair shares one object, sakkāyanirodha, the cessation of identity; the second pair shares another, avijjāppabheda, the smashing of ignorance. Within each pair, only the fourfold verb of commitment changes. Nothing else does.

The glued hand and the clean one

A hand smeared with glue that grabs a branch sticks, holds, and binds to it. A clean hand grabbing the same branch does not. The simile is domestic and immediate, and it maps directly: a mind that has not leapt forth toward the cessation of identity clings to what it touches in the same unwilled way a sticky hand clings to bark.

What the simile does not say is worth noting. It does not say the glued-hand mendicant has failed, or practiced wrongly, or lacks the attainment described — a peaceful release of the heart is granted to both. The stickiness is specifically about the mind’s relationship to the object of focus, not about the quality of the concentration surrounding it.

A billabong kept dry, and one let loose

The second simile works on a larger scale and takes longer to set up, which suits the larger claim it is illustrating — not identity’s cessation but the smashing of ignorance itself.

A billabong stagnant for years, its inlets closed and its drains opened, given no help from the sky: it will not break its banks, however long it sits. The same billabong, inlets opened and drains closed, given plenty of rain: it will.

Every element of the simile is under someone’s control except the rain, and that detail matters. Inlets and drains can be managed by a person — effort, attention, arrangement — but the rain either comes or it does not. The simile builds in a factor beyond deliberate control, and the discourse does not resolve what that factor corresponds to on the meditator’s side. It leaves the parallel to the reader rather than spelling out what plays the rain’s part in a person’s own case.

What the four are actually being sorted by

It is worth being precise about the variable, because it is easy to misread this discourse as being about effort or diligence. It is not. All four mendicants have reached a peaceful release of the heart and are focused on the correct object. The sorting variable is a specific description of what the mind does with what it is given: leaps forth, gains confidence, settles, becomes decided — or does not.

That is closer to a report on readiness than an instruction for producing it. The discourse tells a mendicant what the presence or absence of this leaping-forth predicts, and does not tell them how to make it happen. In that sense it functions as a diagnostic, in the same register as AN 4.158’s marks of decline: read your own mind and draw the indicated conclusion, rather than being handed a technique.

Where it sits

Ninth of the chapter’s ten, and the most image-driven discourse in a chapter otherwise built from dialogue and dense analysis. What follows, AN 4.179, returns to dialogue with a much shorter exchange between Ānanda and Sāriputta on the same general question this discourse has just illustrated: why some are not fully extinguished in this very life, and some are.

Key terms

cetovimutti — “release of the heart” — here qualified as peaceful, santa, and granted equally to all four individuals in the discourse.
sakkāyanirodha — “cessation of identity”, of substantial reality — the object of focus for the first pair of mendicants.
avijjāppabheda — “smashing of ignorance” — literally breaking it open; the object of focus for the second pair.
pakkhandati pasīdati santiṭṭhati adhimuccati — “leaps forth, gains confidence, settles down, becomes decided” — the fourfold description of commitment that alone distinguishes each pair.
jambālī — “billabong”, a stagnant backwater — the simile that gives this discourse its Pāli title.

The text

The discourse in full: four individuals, two similes, and the same structure repeated twice. Translation: Bhikkhu Sujato (CC0, SuttaCentral).

Sticking to the branch

§1“Mendicants, these four individuals are found in the world. What four? Take a mendicant who enters and remains in a peaceful release of the heart. They focus on the cessation of substantial reality, but on that their mind does not leap forth, gain confidence, settle down, and become decided. You wouldn’t expect that mendicant to realize the cessation of substantial reality. Suppose a person were to grab a branch with a glue-smeared hand. Their hand would stick, hold, and bind to it. In the same way, take a mendicant who enters and remains in a peaceful release of the heart. They focus on the cessation of substantial reality, but on that their mind does not leap forth, gain confidence, settle down, and become decided. You wouldn’t expect that mendicant to realize the cessation of substantial reality.

A clean hand

§2Next, take a mendicant who enters and remains in a peaceful release of the heart. They focus on the cessation of substantial reality, and on that their mind leaps forth, gains confidence, settles down, and becomes decided. You would expect that mendicant to realize the cessation of substantial reality. Suppose a person were to grab a branch with a clean hand. Their hand wouldn’t stick, hold, or bind to it. In the same way, take a mendicant who enters and remains in a peaceful release of the heart. They focus on the cessation of substantial reality, and on that their mind leaps forth, gains confidence, settles down, and becomes decided. You would expect that mendicant to realize the cessation of substantial reality.

A billabong kept stagnant

§3Next, take a mendicant who enters and remains in a peaceful release of the heart. They focus on smashing ignorance, but on that their mind does not leap forth, gain confidence, settle down, and become decided. You wouldn’t expect that mendicant to smash ignorance. Suppose there was a billabong that had been stagnant for many years. And someone was to close off the inlets and open up the drains, and the heavens didn’t provide enough rain. You wouldn’t expect that billabong to break its banks. In the same way, take a mendicant who enters and remains in a certain peaceful release of the heart. They focus on smashing ignorance, but on that their mind does not leap forth, gain confidence, settle down, and become decided. You wouldn’t expect that mendicant to smash ignorance.

A billabong let loose

§4Next, take a mendicant who enters and remains in a peaceful release of the heart. They focus on smashing ignorance, and on that their mind leaps forth, gains confidence, settles down, and becomes decided. You would expect that mendicant to smash ignorance. Suppose there was a billabong that had been stagnant for many years. And someone was to open up the inlets and close off the drains, and the heavens provided plenty of rain. You would expect that billabong to break its banks. In the same way, take a mendicant who enters and remains in a certain peaceful release of the heart. They focus on smashing ignorance, and on that their mind leaps forth, gains confidence, settles down, and becomes decided. You would expect that mendicant to smash ignorance. These are the four individuals found in the world.”
