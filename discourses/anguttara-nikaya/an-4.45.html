Aṅguttara Nikāya · Discourse 4.45

With Rohitassa

Rohitassasutta — Rohitassavagga — the fifth chapter of the Fours

Setting
Sāvatthī, in Jeta’s Grove, Anāthapiṇḍika’s monastery, late at night
Speakers
The Buddha and the godling Rohitassa
Form
A question, a refusal, a first-person story, a qualification, and two verses
Length
~3 minutes to read
Northern parallel
The Rohitassa episode appears at SN 2.26 and across the Chinese Āgamas; this reading guide does not assert a specific matching sutra number
Difficulty
★★★☆☆ — one of the most quoted discourses in the canon, and usually quoted at half length

Why this discourse

A god arrives at night and asks whether one can reach the end of the world by walking. The answer is no, and the god knows it: he tried, at a stride spanning ocean to ocean, for a hundred years, and died on the way. Then comes the sentence the discourse is famous for — and the sentence before it, which is almost always dropped and which changes what the famous one means.

Reading guide

The teaching in one sentence

The end of the world cannot be walked to, and cannot be skipped either: it is to be reached in this body, because this body with its perception and mind is where the world is.

The question

Rohitassa asks whether it is possible to know, see, or reach the end of the world by traveling to a place where there’s no being born, growing old, dying, passing away, or being reborn.

The question is precise and it is not naive. He is not asking about geography; he is asking whether there is a location exempt from birth and death, and whether it can be gone to. That is a reasonable question within his cosmology, which contains many realms of very different durations.

The answer is a flat no, given twice, and the second time with the qualification that makes the discourse.

The story

Rohitassa was a seer with psychic powers, a sky-walker, as fast as an arrow shot across the shadow of a palm tree by an expert archer, with a stride spanning the eastern ocean to the western.

He walked for a full hundred-year lifespan, pausing only to eat and drink, go to the toilet, and sleep to dispel weariness, and died on the way without reaching the end of the world.

The specificity of that list of pauses is what makes the story land. It is not a fable about a lazy attempt. He gave the whole of a life to it, at a speed no ordinary being could approach, and stopped only for what a body cannot avoid. The failure is not a failure of effort, and the discourse takes trouble to establish that before drawing its conclusion.

The sentence that is usually dropped

But I also say there’s no making an end of suffering without reaching the end of the world.

This is the half of the answer that gets lost when the discourse is quoted, and without it the teaching is unrecognizable. The Buddha does not say the end of the world is a false goal. He says it is necessary and that walking is the wrong method.

Read with this sentence in place, the famous line that follows is not a redefinition away from cosmology into psychology. It is an answer to the question ‘where is it?’, given to someone who has already accepted that he has to get there.

The fathom-long carcass

For it is in this fathom-long carcass with its perception and mind that I describe the world, its origin, its cessation, and the practice that leads to its cessation.

Byāmamatta kaḷevara — a body a fathom in length, the span of the outstretched arms. Kaḷevara is a blunt word, used of a corpse as readily as of a living body; Sujato’s ‘carcass’ keeps that edge, where softer renderings lose it.

The four-truth structure returns, with the world in the first position, as at AN 4.23. What this discourse adds is the location. The world that is to be understood, whose origin is to be given up and whose cessation realized, is found here — and ‘here’ is specified with a measurement and a qualifier: with its perception and mind.

The qualifier matters. Not the body as a lump of matter, which contains no world at all, but the body as a perceiving and thinking thing. The claim is about experience having a location, not about the world being physically inside a person.

What the discourse does and does not claim

It is worth being careful here, because this passage is regularly made to carry more than it says. It does not say there is no external world, that the world is a mental construct, or that cosmology is metaphor. It says that the world as the four truths describe it — the world that can be understood and brought to cessation — is described in this experiencing body.

That is a claim about where the work is done, and it is enough to answer Rohitassa. He wanted a destination; he is told the destination is not a place because the thing to be escaped is not in a place.

The closing verses put it plainly: the end of the world can never be reached by traveling, and without reaching it there is no release from suffering — so an intelligent person, understanding the world, has completed the journey and gone to the end of the world. The traveling vocabulary is kept and the traveling is not.

Key terms

lokanta — “the end of the world” — not denied as a goal but relocated: necessary, and not reachable by walking.
byāmamatta kaḷevara — “this fathom-long carcass” — the span of the outstretched arms; kaḷevara is used of a corpse as readily as a living body.
sasaññimhi samanake — “with its perception and mind” — the qualifier that makes the claim about the experiencing body rather than about matter.
iddhimā — “possessed of psychic powers” — what Rohitassa was as a seer, and the reason his failure is not a failure of effort.
devaputta — “godling” — literally a son of the gods; the questioner’s present rebirth, recalling his life as a human seer.

The text

The discourse in full: the question, the refusal, Rohitassa’s story, the qualification, and the verses. Translation: Bhikkhu Sujato (CC0, SuttaCentral).

A visitor at night

§1At one time the Buddha was staying near Sāvatthī in Jeta’s Grove, Anāthapiṇḍika’s monastery. Then, late at night, the glorious godling Rohitassa, lighting up the entire Jeta’s Grove, went up to the Buddha, bowed, stood to one side, and said to him:

The question

§2“Sir, is it possible to know or see or reach the end of the world by traveling to a place where there’s no being born, growing old, dying, passing away, or being reborn?” “Reverend, I say it’s not possible to know or see or reach the end of the world by traveling to a place where there’s no being born, growing old, dying, passing away, or being reborn.”

Rohitassa’s story

§3“It’s incredible, sir, it’s amazing, how well said this was by the Buddha.

§4Once upon a time, I was a seer called Rohitassa, son of the benefactors. I was a sky-walker with psychic powers. I was as fast as a light arrow easily shot across the shadow of a palm tree by a well-trained expert archer with a strong bow. My stride was such that it could span from the eastern ocean to the western ocean. Having such speed and stride, this wish came to me: ‘I will reach the end of the world by traveling.’ I traveled for my whole lifespan of a hundred years—pausing only to eat and drink, go to the toilet, and sleep to dispel weariness—and I passed away along the way, never reaching the end of the world.

§5It’s incredible, sir, it’s amazing, how well said this was by the Buddha.”

In this fathom-long body

§6“Reverend, I say it’s not possible to know or see or reach the end of the world by traveling to a place where there’s no being born, growing old, dying, passing away, or being reborn. But I also say there’s no making an end of suffering without reaching the end of the world. For it is in this fathom-long carcass with its perception and mind that I describe the world, its origin, its cessation, and the practice that leads to its cessation.

The verses

§7The end of the world can never be reached by traveling. But without reaching the end of the world, there’s no release from suffering.

§8So an intelligent person, understanding the world, has completed the spiritual journey, and gone to the end of the world. Assuaged, knowing the end of the world, they do not hope for this world or the next.”
