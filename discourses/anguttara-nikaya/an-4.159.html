Aṅguttara Nikāya · Discourse 4.159

Nun

Bhikkhunīsutta — Indriyavagga — the sixteenth chapter of the Fours

Setting
Ghosita’s Monastery near Kosambī, and then the nuns’ quarters
Speakers
Venerable Ānanda, an unnamed nun, and her messenger
Form
A narrative frame, a four-part teaching with three of the four expanded, and a confession
Length
~5 minutes to read
Northern parallel
The teaching on relying on food, craving, and conceit is attested in the Chinese Āgamas; this reading guide does not assert a specific matching sutra number
Difficulty
★★★★☆ — the story is easy and the doctrine inside it is one of the sharpest things in the Fours

Why this discourse

A nun sends word that she is gravely ill and asks Ānanda to visit out of sympathy. When she sees him coming she wraps herself from head to foot and lies down on her cot. Ānanda sits and teaches: this body is produced by food, by craving, by conceit, and by sex — and three of those four can be relied on to give themselves up. The fourth cannot, and that asymmetry is the discourse.

Reading guide

The teaching in one sentence

Food, craving, and conceit can each be used to undo themselves; sex cannot, and the difference is where the teaching lands.

What the text says happened

It is worth separating what is stated from what is supplied, because a great deal has been supplied to this story over the centuries.

Stated: a nun sends a man with a message that she is sick, suffering, and gravely ill, and asks Ānanda to come to the nuns’ quarters out of sympathy. Ānanda consents in silence. When she sees him coming in the distance she wraps herself from head to foot and lies down on her cot. He sits and teaches. She then rises, arranges her robe, bows, and says she made a mistake — that it was foolish, stupid, and unskillful of her to act in that way — and asks him to accept it.

Not stated: what she wanted, what she was feeling, or whether she was ill at all. The discourse never gives her motive and never names her; she is a certain nun and the nun named so-and-so throughout.

The commentarial tradition fills this in with an account of infatuation, and that account has shaped how the episode is read. It is commentarial. What the sutta records is a summons under a claim of grave illness, a woman lying covered on a cot, a teaching, and her own statement that she had acted wrongly.

That much is a real event with a real admission in it, and it does not need the commentary to be legible.

Ānanda’s method

The most striking thing about the encounter is what Ānanda does not do. He does not ask about the illness. He does not comment on her being covered up. He does not accuse her of anything, name her conduct, or refer to the situation at all.

He teaches a piece of standard doctrine, complete and impersonal, and then stops. The recognition is entirely hers, and it arrives after the teaching rather than being extracted by it.

This is worth putting to anyone who teaches or counsels. The discourse presents a correction accomplished without a single word of correction, and the mechanism is that the teaching was true generally and the listener was the one who applied it.

It is also, in its own terms, a piece of restraint about privacy. Whatever Ānanda understood on arriving, none of it is spoken aloud, and the record does not contain a diagnosis of her state made by anyone but herself.

Three ladders

The teaching proper has four terms. This body is produced by food; relying on food, give up food. Produced by craving; relying on craving, give up craving. Produced by conceit; relying on conceit, give up conceit. Produced by sex — and here the pattern stops.

The three that are expanded are each a case of using a thing to end itself. Food: the mendicant reflects on eating not for fun, indulgence, adornment, or decoration, but to sustain the body and support the practice, and in time, relying on food, gives up food. Not starvation — the giving up is of food as an object of wanting.

Craving: a mendicant hears that someone has realized freedom in this very life, and thinks oh, when will I too. That wanting is craving, and it is being put to work. In time, relying on craving, they give up craving.

Conceit: the same report reaches a mendicant who thinks that venerable can do it — why can’t I? That is māna, the measuring of oneself against another, and the discourse uses it as fuel without apology. In time, relying on conceit, they give up conceit.

The two middle cases are the collection at its most practical. A person is not required to arrive with pure motives. Wanting what someone else has and refusing to be outdone are both admitted as starting energy, on the explicit condition that they are consumed in the process.

The fourth, which is not a ladder

This body is produced by sex. The Buddha spoke of breaking off everything to do with sex. And that is the whole of the fourth item — no expansion, no ‘relying on… give up…’, no worked example.

The Pāli for breaking off is setughāta, literally the destruction of a bridge. The image is of a crossing being demolished rather than a road being avoided.

The asymmetry is the doctrinal point and it should be stated as flatly as the text does. Three of the four are permitted as instruments. The fourth is not, and the reason given is not that it is worse in kind but simply that the Buddha ruled it out.

It is also, unmistakably, where the teaching meets the situation. The nun asked for a visit and got a discourse whose fourth term is celibacy, delivered without a single second-person pronoun. Whether Ānanda intended the placement cannot be established from the text and this reading guide does not assert it; what can be said is that the fourth item is the shortest, the least explained, and the last thing said before she gets up.

The confession, and the phrase at the end

Her statement is the canonical confession formula, and Ānanda’s reply is the canonical acceptance: yes, it was a mistake; but since you have recognized it and dealt with it properly, I accept it — for it is growth in the training of the Noble One to recognize a mistake for what it is, deal with it properly, and commit to restraint in the future.

That closing phrase is worth holding beside AN 4.111, where the same construction appears with the opposite content: it is killing in the training of the Noble One when a person is no longer thought worth advising. Growth and killing, in the same grammatical frame, defined by whether a person can take a correction.

The acceptance is not softened. Ānanda agrees that it was foolish, stupid, and unskillful before accepting it, and the agreement is part of the formula rather than a rebuke. The tradition’s procedure requires the fault to be named accurately by both parties and then closed.

That is a better piece of institutional design than it is usually credited with. It makes no attempt to minimize what happened and no attempt to keep it open afterward.

Reading it now

Some things should be said plainly. This is one of a number of episodes in which a nun is the one at fault and a monk teaches and receives the confession. She is not named and he is. The Fours contain that, and the Fours also contain AN 4.158 two discourses earlier, addressed explicitly to any monk or nun, and AN 4.129 on the four assemblies each equally uplifted.

Neither pattern cancels the other, and a reader is not required to harmonize them. What a reading guide can do is name both rather than presenting either as the collection’s single voice.

What survives the framing intact is the doctrine. Three motives that can be spent on their own removal and one that cannot; a correction achieved by saying nothing about the person; and a procedure for closing a fault that neither excuses it nor keeps it alive.

Taught carefully, this page carries more usable material than any other discourse in the chapter.

Key terms

setughāta — “breaking off” — literally the destruction of a bridge; used of the fourth item, and the reason it is not a ladder.
māna — “conceit” — the measuring of oneself against another; here used as fuel on the condition that it is consumed.
taṇhā — “craving” — here the wanting of what another has attained, admitted as starting energy.
paṭisaṅkhā yoniso — “reflecting properly” — the formula introducing the reflection on food: not for fun, indulgence, adornment, or decoration.
vuddhi ariyassa vinaye — “growth in the training of the Noble One” — Ānanda’s closing phrase; the same frame AN 4.111 used for its opposite.

The text

The discourse in full: the message, the visit, the four terms with three expanded, and the confession. Translation: Bhikkhu Sujato (CC0, SuttaCentral).

The message

§1So I have heard. At one time Venerable Ānanda was staying near Kosambī, in Ghosita’s Monastery. And then a certain nun addressed a man, “Please, my friend, go to Venerable Ānanda, and in my name bow with your head to his feet. Say to him: ‘Sir, the nun named so-and-so is sick, suffering, and gravely ill. She bows with her head to your feet.’ And then say: ‘Sir, please go to the nuns’ quarters to visit that nun out of sympathy.’” “Yes, ma’am,” that man replied. He did as the nun asked.

§2Ānanda consented with silence.

At the nuns’ quarters

§3Then Ānanda robed up and went to the nuns’ quarters to visit that nun, taking his bowl and robe. That nun saw Ānanda coming off in the distance. She wrapped herself up from head to foot and laid down on her cot. Then Venerable Ānanda went up to her, and sat down on the seat spread out. Then Ānanda said to the nun:

Four things this body is produced by

§4“Sister, this body is produced by food. Relying on food, you should give up food. This body is produced by craving. Relying on craving, you should give up craving. This body is produced by conceit. Relying on conceit, you should give up conceit. This body is produced by sex. The Buddha spoke of breaking off everything to do with sex.

Relying on food

§5‘This body is produced by food. Relying on food, you should give up food.’ This is what I said, but why did I say it? Take a mendicant who reflects rationally on the food that they eat: ‘Not for fun, indulgence, adornment, or decoration, but only to sustain this body, to avoid harm, and to support spiritual practice. In this way, I shall put an end to old discomfort and not give rise to new discomfort, and I will have the means to keep going, blamelessness, and a comfortable abiding.’ After some time, relying on food, they give up food. That’s why I said what I said.

Relying on craving

§6‘This body is produced by craving. Relying on craving, you should give up craving.’ This is what I said, but why did I say it? Take a mendicant who hears this: ‘They say that the mendicant named so-and-so has realized the undefiled freedom of heart and freedom by wisdom in this very life. And they live having realized it with their own insight due to the ending of defilements.’ They think: ‘Oh, when will I too realize the undefiled freedom of heart and freedom by wisdom in this very life. …’ After some time, relying on craving, they give up craving. That’s why I said what I said.

Relying on conceit

§7‘This body is produced by conceit. Relying on conceit, you should give up conceit.’ This is what I said, but why did I say it? Take a mendicant who hears this: ‘They say that the mendicant named so-and-so has realized the undefiled freedom of heart and freedom by wisdom in this very life. And they live having realized it with their own insight due to the ending of defilements.’ They think: ‘Well, that venerable can realize the undefiled freedom of heart and freedom by wisdom in this very life. … Why can’t I?’ After some time, relying on conceit, they give up conceit. That’s why I said what I said.

And the fourth

§8‘This body is produced by sex. The Buddha spoke of breaking off everything to do with sex.’”

The confession

§9Then that nun rose from her cot, placed her robe over one shoulder, bowed with her head at Ānanda’s feet, and said, “I made a mistake, sir. It was foolish, stupid, and unskillful of me to act in that way. Please, sir, accept my mistake for what it is, so I can restrain myself in future.” “Indeed, sister, you made a mistake. It was foolish, stupid, and unskillful of you to act in that way. But since you have recognized your mistake for what it is, and have dealt with it properly, I accept it. For it is growth in the training of the Noble One to recognize a mistake for what it is, deal with it properly, and commit to restraint in the future.”
