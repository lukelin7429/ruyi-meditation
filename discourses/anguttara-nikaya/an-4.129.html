Aṅguttara Nikāya · Discourse 4.129

Incredible Things About Ānanda

Ānandaacchariyasutta — Bhayavagga — the thirteenth chapter of the Fours

Setting
None stated; the Bhayavagga gives no location for this discourse, and it is addressed to the mendicants directly
Speakers
The Buddha alone, addressing the mendicants
Form
One statement repeated across the four assemblies, heavily abridged
Length
~1 minute to read
Northern parallel
The praise of Ānanda’s effect on an assembly belongs to the shared material behind DN 16 and its Āgama counterparts; this reading guide does not assert a specific matching sutra number
Difficulty
★☆☆☆☆ — the shortest page in the chapter and the warmest

Why this discourse

The chapter’s third set of marvels is about a person who is not a Buddha. Monks, nuns, laymen, and laywomen come to see Ānanda; they are uplifted by seeing him and uplifted by hearing him speak; and when he falls silent, they have never had enough. Four assemblies, three clauses, and one of the most affectionate things said about anyone in the collection.

Reading guide

The teaching in one sentence

Four kinds of people come to Ānanda and all four go away wanting more.

What is actually claimed

The praise has three parts and they are carefully distinguished. The assembly is uplifted by seeing him — before he has said anything at all. It is uplifted by hearing him speak. And when he stops, they’ve never had enough.

The Pāli behind the third clause puts it the other way round: atittāva bhikkhave bhikkhuparisā hoti, atha ānando tuṇhī bhavati — the assembly is still unsated, and then Ānanda falls silent. The unsatisfied state is named first and the silence arrives into it.

That is a compliment about judgment as much as about eloquence. Stopping while the room still wants more is a skill, and the discourse is describing someone who does it every time, before four different kinds of audience.

Notice also what is not praised. Not his memory, though the tradition made that his defining feature. Not his attainment, which at this point in the story he does not have. What is praised is the effect he has on a room.

The four assemblies

Bhikkhu, bhikkhunī, upāsaka, upāsikā — monks, nuns, laymen, laywomen. The standard four-fold division of the community, and the reason this praise fits the Fours at all.

The set is doing real work here. A speaker who moves a hall of monastics is not necessarily one who holds a room of laywomen, and the discourse’s claim is specifically that the same three things happen in all four cases with nothing adjusted.

The written text gives the first assembly in full, marks the second and third by name alone, and restores the fourth to full length. That is the ordinary storage format of the collection, and by this point in the chapter a reader should recognize it on sight.

Who this is about

Ānanda is the Buddha’s attendant and cousin, named foremost among those who have learned much, and the person through whom a very large part of the collection is transmitted. The tradition also records, without embarrassment, that he was still unawakened when the Buddha died and reached the goal only on the night before the council convened.

That combination is what makes this discourse worth teaching. The person the four assemblies cannot get enough of is, at the time described, someone still on the way. The collection is willing to say that a room is lifted by someone who has not finished, and to call it incredible and amazing.

It is worth adding, since this chapter has just spent four discourses on what distinguishes a noble disciple from an ordinary person, that AN 4.129 places its marvel in a completely different register. Nothing here is about attainment at all.

Why it is placed here

The chapter’s marvels descend. AN 4.127: a light reaching the darkness between world systems. AN 4.128: a population that listens against its own inclination. AN 4.129: one man, four rooms, and an audience that wants him to keep going.

Three uses of acchariya abbhuta, and the third is small enough to be observed by anyone. Whatever the compilers intended, the effect of reading them in order is that the word is progressively brought down to a scale a reader can check.

AN 4.130 will complete the movement by putting a wheel-turning monarch in the same frame and then repeating this discourse verbatim inside it.

Teaching it

The natural use for this page is as a discussion of what makes a teacher worth listening to, with the discourse’s own three criteria on the table: presence before speaking, effect while speaking, and the timing of stopping.

The third is the one worth pressing, because it is the only one a teacher fully controls. The discourse does not say Ānanda spoke well for a long time. It says the room was still hungry when he stopped.

The chapter ends with the next discourse, and the Fours then move on to the Puggalavagga, the chapter of persons, which sorts people into fours for ten discourses running.

Key terms

acchariya abbhuta — “incredible and amazing” — the same fixed pair used of a Buddha in AN 4.127 and AN 4.128, applied here to a disciple.
catasso parisā — “the four assemblies” — monks, nuns, laymen, laywomen; the standard division of the community and the reason this praise is a set of four.
attamana — “uplifted”, gladdened — literally something like having one’s own mind; the state the assembly is put into by seeing and by hearing.
atitta — “unsated”, not having had enough — the word describing the assembly at the moment Ānanda stops speaking.
bahussuta — “very learned”, one who has heard much — not used here, but the quality for which the tradition names Ānanda foremost.

The text

The discourse in full: the four assemblies, the first and last written out and the middle two named only. Translation: Bhikkhu Sujato (CC0, SuttaCentral).

The assembly of monks

§1“Mendicants, there are these four incredible and amazing things about Ānanda. What four? If an assembly of monks goes to see Ānanda, they’re uplifted by seeing him and uplifted by hearing him speak. And when he falls silent, they’ve never had enough.

Nuns, laymen, and laywomen

§2If an assembly of nuns … laymen … or laywomen goes to see Ānanda, they’re uplifted by seeing him and uplifted by hearing him speak. And when he falls silent, they’ve never had enough. These are the four incredible and amazing things about Ānanda.”
