Aṅguttara Nikāya · Discourses 1.1–10

What Occupies the Mind

Rūpādivagga — the opening chapter of the Ones

Setting
Sāvatthī, in Jeta’s Grove, Anāthapiṇḍika’s monastery; the setting is stated once at the head of AN 1.1 and carries across all ten
Speakers
The Buddha alone, addressing the assembled mendicants; there is no interlocutor
Form
Ten one-line discourses on a single fixed template — five running through the senses for a man’s mind, then the same five, reversed, for a woman’s
Length
~3 minutes to read
Northern parallel
The Chinese Ekottarika-āgama (T125) preserves comparable single-item statements in its own opening chapters, though this reading guide does not assert a specific matching sutra number
Difficulty
★★☆☆☆ — the sentences could not be simpler; the work is entirely in reading them fairly rather than reacting to them

Why this discourse

This is where the Aṅguttara Nikāya begins — and for many modern readers it is a startling place to start. The very first thing the collection says, once the frame story is out of the way, is that nothing takes over a man’s mind like the sight of a woman. Read as a standalone quotation that lands badly, and a reader who stops there will carry a false impression of the text for a long time. Read as what it actually is — the opening panel of a strictly symmetrical ten-part set, spoken to celibate monastics about where their own attention gets caught — it turns out to be a piece of clinical observation about desire, not a verdict on women. Both of those readings deserve to be on the table when this chapter is taught, and this guide puts them there rather than quietly skipping the chapter, as selections of the Aṅguttara very often do.

Reading guide

The teaching in one sentence

For a mind that has not been trained, there is no sense object more totally absorbing than the one it desires — and the chapter demonstrates this by running the same claim through all five senses, first for a man’s mind toward a woman, then for a woman’s mind toward a man.

Where the Numbered Discourses begin

The Aṅguttara Nikāya is organized purely by number: the Ones (ekaka nipāta) collect teachings that turn on a single item, the Twos on pairs, and so on up to the Elevens. Because a “one” can be stated in a sentence, the Ones are made up of very short entries grouped into chapters (vagga) of roughly ten, and they are conventionally read and cited as a block. This first block, the Rūpādivagga — “the chapter on sights, and so on” — is ten discourses long, and Bhikkhu Sujato titles it in English by its actual subject: what occupies the mind.

The frame story appears exactly once, at the head of AN 1.1, and then the remaining nine entries drop straight into the formula with no repetition of the setting. That economy is characteristic of the whole nipāta and worth pointing out to a class early: these are not nine truncated discourses, they are nine complete ones sharing a stated context.

The formula, and what it actually claims

Every entry runs on the same frame: I do not see a single X that occupies a person’s mind like Y, followed by a restatement. The verb Sujato renders as “occupies” is pariyādāya tiṭṭhati — literally something like “having taken it over completely, it stays there.” It is the language of a mind being occupied in the military sense: held, garrisoned, no longer under its own command.

Read closely, the claim is a report about the observer, not a description of the person observed. Nothing here says a woman is impure, or dangerous, or responsible for anyone else’s attention. What is said is that a certain mind, in a certain condition, cannot leave a certain object alone. The subject of every sentence is the grip, and the diagnosis is aimed at the one who is gripped.

Reading the gender framing honestly

It would be dishonest to stop there, so here is the rest of it. The chapter is stated in both directions, and it is stated in the same words: five entries for a man’s mind toward a woman, five for a woman’s mind toward a man. Whatever else the passage is, it is not a text that assigns the problem of desire to one sex. That symmetry is the single most useful thing to point out when the chapter lands badly on first hearing, and it is right there in the text rather than something a commentator has to argue for.

At the same time, the arrangement is not neutral. The men’s five come first, and they come first because the audience being addressed — bhikkhave, “mendicants” — is a body of male monastics. The default listener of the early canon is a monk, and this chapter shows that plainly. Later Buddhist literature, in several traditions, took lines of exactly this shape and pushed them somewhere the symmetrical original does not go: toward warnings about women as such, as a category of hazard. That reception history is real, it has done real damage, and a teacher is better off naming it than hoping nobody in the room already knows about it.

The defensible reading, then, is neither “there is nothing to see here” nor “the tradition is simply misogynistic.” It is that this particular text says something narrow and symmetrical about desire; that its ordering reflects who was in the room; and that a good deal of what got built on top of it later does not follow from it.

Why five senses, and why it matters practically

The five items — sight, sound, smell, taste, touch — are the five external sense fields (rūpa, sadda, gandha, rasa, phoṭṭhabba), and running the claim through all five is not padding. It is a refusal to let the listener localize the problem. A practitioner who decides that the difficulty is “looking” will simply relocate the same attachment into a voice, or a scent, or a remembered touch. The chapter closes off every door in turn.

That is exactly why this apparently trivial chapter is the seed of a large practical literature. The training it points toward is sense restraint (indriya-saṁvara) — not shutting the senses down, but declining to elaborate what arrives at them into a story worth chasing. In the Chinese tradition, Zhiyi gives the same material a full treatment under the heading of censuring the five desires, one of the twenty-five preliminary conditions a meditator settles before formal practice begins; there is a link to that passage in Further reading below.

Teaching this chapter

In a classroom, the chapter works best read whole and read aloud — all ten, in order, so that the repetition and the turn at entry six both land as structure rather than as an accident. The turn is the moment to stop. Asking a group what changed between the fifth discourse and the sixth usually gets the symmetry noticed by the students rather than announced by the teacher, which is a much better outcome. From there the productive question is not whether the Buddha was right about men and women, but the one the text is actually asking: what, for you, is the object your mind will not put down?

Key terms

pariyādāya tiṭṭhati — “occupies and remains” — the mind is taken over and held, as ground is held by an occupying force; Sujato’s “occupies the mind” keeps that sense.
itthirūpa / purisarūpa — “the sight of a woman” / “the sight of a man.” Rūpa here is visible form as a sense object — what the eye receives — not “body” in any loaded sense.
rūpa, sadda, gandha, rasa, phoṭṭhabba — sight, sound, smell, taste, and touch: the five external sense fields the chapter works through in order, twice.
bhikkhave — “mendicants” — the vocative that opens nearly every discourse of this collection. It marks the audience as ordained celibates, which is the context the advice is calibrated to.
Rūpādivagga — “the chapter on sights, and so on” — the Pāli name of this first chapter of the Ones, taken as usual from its opening item.

The text

All ten discourses of the chapter, in order. Each §N below is one complete discourse — §1 is AN 1.1, §10 is AN 1.10 — and the setting stated in §1 is understood to hold for the rest. Translation: Bhikkhu Sujato (CC0, SuttaCentral).

A man’s mind (AN 1.1–5)

§1So I have heard. At one time the Buddha was staying near Sāvatthī in Jeta’s Grove, Anāthapiṇḍika’s monastery. There the Buddha addressed the mendicants, “Mendicants!” “Venerable sir,” they replied. The Buddha said this: “Mendicants, I do not see a single sight that occupies a man’s mind like the sight of a woman. The sight of a woman occupies a man’s mind.”

§2“Mendicants, I do not see a single sound that occupies a man’s mind like the sound of a woman. The sound of a woman occupies a man’s mind.”

§3“Mendicants, I do not see a single smell that occupies a man’s mind like the smell of a woman. The smell of a woman occupies a man’s mind.”

§4“Mendicants, I do not see a single taste that occupies a man’s mind like the taste of a woman. The taste of a woman occupies a man’s mind.”

§5“Mendicants, I do not see a single touch that occupies a man’s mind like the touch of a woman. The touch of a woman occupies a man’s mind.”

A woman’s mind (AN 1.6–10)

§6“Mendicants, I do not see a single sight that occupies a woman’s mind like the sight of a man. The sight of a man occupies a woman’s mind.”

§7“Mendicants, I do not see a single sound that occupies a woman’s mind like the sound of a man. The sound of a man occupies a woman’s mind.”

§8“Mendicants, I do not see a single smell that occupies a woman’s mind like the smell of a man. The smell of a man occupies a woman’s mind.”

§9“Mendicants, I do not see a single taste that occupies a woman’s mind like the taste of a man. The taste of a man occupies a woman’s mind.”

§10“Mendicants, I do not see a single touch that occupies a woman’s mind like the touch of a man. The touch of a man occupies a woman’s mind.”
