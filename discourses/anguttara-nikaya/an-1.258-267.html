Aṅguttara Nikāya · Discourses 1.258–267

Foremost Laywomen

Etadaggavagga — the twentieth chapter of the Ones

Setting
None stated; the chapter continues from AN 1.1, whose setting at Sāvatthī is understood to hold
Speakers
The Buddha alone, naming his own disciples
Form
Ten single-sentence declarations, closing the foremost list and with it the seven chapters of named disciples
Length
~2 minutes to read
Northern parallel
The Chinese Ekottarika-āgama (T125) preserves a directly comparable set of chapters listing foremost disciples in the same four groups — monks, nuns, laymen, and laywomen — though its names and categories do not match the Pāli one for one; this reading guide does not assert a specific matching sutra number
Difficulty
★☆☆☆☆ — the shortest kind of reading, containing at least one entry that changes how the whole list looks

Why this discourse

The foremost list ends here, and it ends with ten laywomen. One of them is a queen’s attendant who was legally enslaved, and she is named foremost in learning — not among servants, not among women of her station, but among all the Buddha’s laywomen disciples. Another is named foremost for caring for the sick. Another for dwelling in love, and the story attached to her is one of the most terrible in the canon. A list that began with Koṇḍañña, the first man to understand, closes on this.

Reading guide

The teaching in one sentence

Ten laywomen close the foremost list, named for learning, love, giving, absorption, nursing, verified confidence, a marriage, and the trust of someone who only ever overheard.

Khujjuttarā (AN 1.260)

Foremost among laywomen who are very learned — bahussutānaṁ, the same term used of Ānanda at AN 1.219. Khujjuttarā was an attendant of Queen Sāmāvatī, and in the tradition’s account she was a slave; her name refers to a curvature of her back. She went daily to buy flowers, heard the Buddha teach, and returned to repeat what she had heard to the women of the palace, who could not leave it.

The tradition takes this further than a single entry. The Itivuttaka, one of the short books of the canon, is by tradition attributed to Khujjuttarā’s transmission — its distinctive formula, “this was said by the Blessed One,” instead of the usual “so I have heard,” is understood as her reporting to others what she had heard directly. Whether or not that attribution is historical, its existence is the point: the tradition was prepared to say that a book of its canon came through an enslaved woman.

This entry is worth reading beside AN 1.219, where Ānanda holds the same category among monks. Both are named for hearing and holding. One was the Buddha’s cousin and constant attendant; the other bought flowers.

Sāmāvatī (AN 1.261)

Foremost among those who dwell in love, mettāvihārī. Sāmāvatī was the queen Khujjuttarā served, and the story the commentaries attach to her is grim: a rival consort’s intrigue ended with Sāmāvatī and her women burned to death in their quarters, and the tradition holds that she maintained goodwill toward those who killed her throughout.

There is a real question about how to teach a story like that, and it should not be waved through. Told carelessly it becomes an argument that victims should be serene, which is not what the canon is doing and is a genuinely harmful thing to imply. What the entry actually names is her practice, in circumstances that give the naming its weight; it does not tell anyone else to endure anything. A teacher unsure of the room is better off reading the entry and saying that the traditional story is severe, and offering it afterward to those who want it, than delivering it as an uplift.

Visākhā, Suppavāsā, Suppiyā

Visākhā, Migāra’s mother, is foremost as a donor — the counterpart of Anāthapiṇḍika, and one of the most vividly drawn characters in the canon, who appears negotiating, arguing, weeping, and getting her way. Suppavāsā the Koliyan is foremost among those who give fine things. Suppiyā is foremost among those who care for the sick, which is the only entry in the entire eighty-plus list for nursing, and the Vinaya records her attending sick monks at considerable personal cost.

That nursing appears once, and appears here, is worth a sentence. The canon elsewhere has the Buddha find a monk lying in his own waste, unattended, wash him himself, and tell the community that whoever would tend him should tend the sick. The category exists; it is named once; and it is named of a laywoman.

Confidence, marriage, and hearsay (AN 1.265–267)

Kātiyānī is foremost in experiential confidence, the same term used of Sūrambaṭṭha in the previous chapter. Nakula’s mother is foremost among those who are intimate, exactly as her husband is — the two entries are the same category, one at the end of each chapter, and reading them together is the intended effect.

The last entry of the entire foremost list is the strangest and the best. Kāḷī of Kuraraghara is foremost among those whose confidence is based on oral transmission — anussava, hearsay, report. She is said to have overheard a conversation between two spirits praising the Buddha, and to have gained confidence from that, before ever meeting him.

What makes this remarkable is that anussava is one of the ten grounds the Buddha tells the Kālāmas not to rely on. The list closes by naming someone foremost in a mode of confidence the canon elsewhere warns against relying on by itself. That is not a contradiction if the two passages are read carefully — AN 3.65 says hearsay does not settle whether a doctrine is true, not that nothing good ever begins in hearsay — but the juxtaposition is deliberate enough to be worth putting in front of a class. Almost everyone’s first contact with anything is hearsay. The question is what they do next.

Key terms

upāsikā — “laywoman” — a lay follower who has gone for refuge. The final substitution in the etadagga formula.
bahussutā — “very learned,” of much hearing — Khujjuttarā’s entry, and the same term used of Ānanda at AN 1.219.
mettāvihārī — “dwelling in love” — Sāmāvatī’s entry. Not a momentary practice but a way of living, which is what makes the commentarial story attached to it so heavy.
gilānupaṭṭhākā — “one who attends the sick” — Suppiyā’s entry, and the only entry for nursing anywhere on the foremost list.
anussava — “oral transmission, report, hearsay” — the basis of Kāḷī’s confidence, and one of the ten grounds AN 3.65 tells the Kālāmas not to rely on by itself.

The text

All ten declarations of the chapter, abbreviated in the Pāli after the first as before. These close the foremost list. Translation: Bhikkhu Sujato (CC0, SuttaCentral).

Refuge, giving, learning, love (AN 1.258–261)

§258“The foremost of my laywomen in first going for refuge is Sujātā the general’s daughter.

§259… as a donor is Visākhā, Migāra’s mother.

§260… who are very learned is Khujjuttarā.

§261… who dwell in love is Sāmāvatī.

Absorption, giving, nursing (AN 1.262–264)

§262… who practice absorption is Uttarā, Nanda’s mother.

§263… who give fine things is Suppavāsā the Koliyan.

§264… who care for the sick is the laywoman Suppiyā.

Confidence, intimacy, and what was overheard (AN 1.265–267)

§265… who have experiential confidence is Kātiyānī.

§266… who are intimate is the householder Nakula’s mother.

§267… whose confidence is based on oral transmission is the laywoman Kāḷī of Kuraraghara.”
