Aṅguttara Nikāya · Discourse 4.79

Business

Vaṇijjasutta — Apaṇṇakavagga — the eighth chapter of the Fours

Setting
None stated; Sāriputta comes to the Buddha and asks
Speakers
The Buddha and Venerable Sāriputta
Form
A question with four outcomes, and four matching causes
Length
~2 minutes to read
Northern parallel
Discourses linking present fortune to past giving appear across the Chinese Āgamas; this reading guide does not assert a specific matching sutra number
Difficulty
★★★☆☆ — the causal claim is specific and has to be handled honestly

Why this discourse

Sāriputta asks a good question: why does the same business venture fail for one person, disappoint another, meet expectations for a third, and exceed them for a fourth? The answer is entirely in terms of past lives — whether one gave what an ascetic or brahmin asked for, and how fully. Four grades of past giving, four grades of present return, matched exactly.

Reading guide

The teaching in one sentence

How a business turns out is determined by how fully one gave what was requested in a previous life.

The question

Sāriputta’s question is precise and is the kind an intelligent observer actually asks: the same kind of business undertaking produces four different outcomes for four different people.

The point of specifying ‘the same kind’ is to rule out the obvious explanations. If the ventures differ, the outcomes might follow from that. Holding the venture constant isolates something about the person.

It is worth crediting the question as a good one before assessing the answer. Variation in outcome under apparently equal conditions is a real phenomenon and it demands an account.

The answer

Someone invites an ascetic or brahmin to ask for what they need, and then: fails to give it; gives it but less than expected; gives as expected; gives more than expected. Each produces, in a later life, a business that respectively fails, disappoints, meets expectations, or exceeds them.

The correspondence is exact and mechanical. It is a proportionality claim: the grade of the past act determines the grade of the present result, in the same four steps.

Note the specific fault in the first case. The person invited the request and then did not fulfil it. That is not stinginess in general; it is the particular act of raising an expectation and then failing it. All four cases share the invitation, and what varies is only the follow-through.

Handling the claim honestly

This is a strong causal claim about rebirth and it should not be quietly converted into something else. The discourse does not say that generous people tend to prosper, or that a habit of following through builds a reputation. It says that a specific past act produces a specific present outcome across lives.

A reader who holds that framework can take the discourse as stated. A reader who does not is entitled to say so, and should not be offered a paraphrase that pretends the text means something more congenial.

What can be said in either case is that the discourse is a piece of moral reasoning about a particular failure. It singles out invited-then-refused as the thing that damages a person, which is a discriminating judgment: of all the ways to be ungenerous, this one involves having created the expectation oneself.

Against AN 4.77

Two discourses earlier the results of deeds were named as one of four things that should not be thought about, on pain of madness or frustration. Here the Buddha gives Sāriputta a detailed account of exactly that.

The tension is only apparent, but it is worth working through rather than passing over. AN 4.77 rules out the exhaustive tracing — accounting for which deed produced which result across the whole field. This discourse answers a specific question with a specific mechanism, on the authority of someone the tradition credits with seeing it.

The distinction the pair establishes is between kamma as a subject one may be taught about and kamma as a calculation one may attempt. The first is throughout the collection; the second is what AN 4.77 warns against.

That distinction is useful in practice. A person who works out that their misfortune must be the result of some particular past act is doing the thing AN 4.77 names, and the predicted outcome — frustration — is what such reasoning reliably produces.

Why Sāriputta

The questioner matters. Sāriputta is the disciple foremost in wisdom and the canon’s usual figure for analytic capacity; questions put by him are generally the ones the compilers considered worth recording as questions rather than as teachings.

That he asks about business outcomes rather than about doctrine is itself informative. The discourse is placed in a chapter of the Fours that contains the Buddha’s deathbed invitation and the four unthinkables, and it sits there as an ordinary question about why the world is uneven.

The collection is full of such questions and answers them at the level they are asked. This one is answered in terms of rebirth because that is where the answer is, on the tradition’s account. It is not offered as consolation and it is not softened.

Key terms

vaṇijjā — “business, trade” — a commercial undertaking; the subject of Sāriputta’s question.
yathādhippāya — “as expected, as intended” — the measure by which the four outcomes and the four past acts are graded.
pavāreti — “invites to ask” — the act all four cases share; what varies is only the follow-through.
Sāriputta — the disciple foremost in wisdom, and the canon’s usual figure for analytic capacity.
kammavipāka — “the results of deeds” — named unthinkable in AN 4.77, and reasoned about in detail here; the distinction is between being taught and calculating.

The text

The discourse in full: the question and the four answers. Translation: Bhikkhu Sujato (CC0, SuttaCentral).

Sāriputta’s question

§1Then Venerable Sāriputta went up to the Buddha, bowed, sat down to one side, and said to him: “Sir, what is the cause, what is the reason why for different people the same kind of business undertaking might fail, while another doesn’t meet expectations, another meets expectations, and another exceeds expectations?”

The four cases

§2“Sāriputta, take a case where someone goes to an ascetic or brahmin and invites them to ask for what they need. But they fail to give what’s requested. When they’ve passed away from that life, if they’re reborn in this place, whatever business they undertake fails.

§3Take a case where someone goes to an ascetic or brahmin and invites them to ask for what they need. They give what’s requested, but don’t meet expectations. When they’ve passed away from that life, if they’re reborn in this place, whatever business they undertake doesn’t meet expectations.

§4Take a case where someone goes to an ascetic or brahmin and invites them to ask for what they need. They give what’s requested, meeting expectations. When they’ve passed away from that life, if they’re reborn in this place, whatever business they undertake meets expectations.

§5Take a case where someone goes to an ascetic or brahmin and invites them to ask for what they need. They give what’s requested, exceeding expectations. When they’ve passed away from that life, if they’re reborn in this place, whatever business they undertake exceeds expectations.

The conclusion

§6This is the cause, this is the reason why for different people the same kind of business undertaking might fail, while another doesn’t meet expectations, another meets expectations, and another exceeds expectations.”
