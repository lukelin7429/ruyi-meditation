Aṅguttara Nikāya · Discourse 4.160

The Training of a Holy One

Sugatavinayasutta — Indriyavagga — the sixteenth chapter of the Fours

Setting
None stated; the Indriyavagga gives no location for this discourse, and it is addressed to the mendicants directly
Speakers
The Buddha alone, addressing the mendicants
Form
A definition, then four causes of decline, then the same four inverted
Length
~5 minutes to read
Northern parallel
Lists of causes for the disappearance of the true teaching are shared with the Chinese Āgamas; this reading guide does not assert a specific matching sutra number
Difficulty
★★★☆☆ — long and entirely concrete, and the most institutionally clear-eyed discourse in this stretch

Why this discourse

What makes a teaching disappear? The discourse gives four answers and every one of them is internal: discourses learned wrong, mendicants who cannot be corrected, learned monastics who do not make others recite, and senior mendicants who are slack and are copied. No persecution, no rival doctrine, no hostile king. The threats to the teaching, on this account, are all in the room.

Reading guide

The teaching in one sentence

A teaching survives or disappears according to how the people carrying it handle it.

The frame

The discourse opens by defining two things. A sugata — Sujato’s ‘Holy One’, more literally the well-gone one — is a Realized One who has arisen in the world, and the definition given is the full ten-epithet formula. The sugatavinaya, the training of such a one, is the teaching proclaimed good in the beginning, middle, and end, meaningful and well-phrased, and the spiritual practice revealed entirely full and pure.

Both remain in the world for the welfare and happiness of the people, out of sympathy for the world. That phrase sets up everything that follows: what is at stake in the teaching’s survival is stated as a benefit to others before any threat to it is named.

It also quietly makes the point that the person and the teaching are two things. The Holy One does not remain in the world indefinitely; the training can. The rest of the discourse is about the conditions under which it does.

Four ways a teaching disappears

First: the mendicants memorize discourses that they learned incorrectly, with misplaced words and phrases. And then the consequence, stated as a mechanism: when the words and phrases are misplaced, the meaning is misinterpreted.

That is a transmission failure, and putting it first is a considerable statement of priorities. The first threat to a teaching is not that people stop believing it but that they learn it slightly wrong and pass on the error.

Second: the mendicants are hard to admonish… impatient, and don’t take instruction respectfully. A community that cannot correct itself cannot repair the first failure, or any other.

Third: the mendicants who are very learned — inheritors of the heritage, who have memorized the teachings, the monastic law, and the outlines — don’t carefully make others recite the discourses. When they pass away, the discourses are cut off at the root, with no-one to preserve them. Knowledge held and not transmitted dies with its holder.

Fourth: the senior mendicants are indulgent and slack, leaders in backsliding, neglecting seclusion… Those who come after them follow their example. This is the only one of the four that names a transmission mechanism for itself: the failure propagates because juniors copy seniors.

What is not on the list

Nothing external. No persecution, no competing teacher, no famine, no war, no corrupting patron, no heresy arriving from outside.

That absence is the discourse’s sharpest feature and it is worth dwelling on, because traditions under pressure habitually explain their decline by pointing outward. This one does not, and it had every opportunity: the Fours elsewhere describe unprincipled kings, hostile ascetics, and the loss of patronage.

Three of the four are about transmission — learning accurately, being correctable, and passing material on. The fourth is about example. All four are things the community does to itself.

There is a second absence worth naming. Nothing here is about practice failing to produce results, or about awakening becoming impossible. The discourse is about the teaching disappearing as a transmitted thing, not about the path stopping working.

The third item, and what it assumes

Don’t carefully make others recite the discourses. The verb is causative: the learned mendicant’s obligation is not to recite but to have others recite, and the failure is one of omission by the most qualified people in the community.

This is the clearest window in the chapter onto how the material was actually kept. There is no library and no archive. A text exists in the memories of the people who have been made to recite it, and the phrase cut off at the root is exact — when the last holder dies, the thing is simply gone.

It also explains a great deal about the collection’s form. The repetitions, the synonym-series of AN 4.141 to AN 4.145, the fixed formulas, the matched pairs that differ by one word — all of them are features of material built to be held in many heads at once.

Read after AN 4.160, the parts of the Aṅguttara that look like padding stop looking like padding. They are the redundancy of a system whose only storage medium is people.

The mirror, and the shape of the whole

The second half is the four inverted, in the same order and the same words: discourses learned correctly, mendicants easy to admonish, the learned making others recite, seniors who take the lead in seclusion and are copied.

The formal choice matters. There is no fifth positive item, no separate account of flourishing, and nothing added. A teaching persists exactly when the four failures do not occur.

This is the same reticence AN 4.158 showed three discourses earlier, where doing well was defined as not declining, and AN 4.37 before it. The Fours describe good states as the failure of bad ones with enough consistency that it should be called characteristic rather than accidental.

It also makes the discourse unusually actionable. Four things to check, and each of them is a practice rather than an attitude: learn it accurately, take correction, make others recite, and be worth copying.

Where it sits, and what follows

AN 4.160 closes the Indriyavagga, a chapter that began with five discourses of a few lines each and ends with this.

Nothing connects the chapter’s halves. The name comes from the first discourse, as always, and a reader who expects the faculties to be its theme will find them abandoned after five pages.

What follows is the Paṭipadāvagga, the chapter of practice, which opens with four ways of practicing — painful with slow insight, painful with swift insight, pleasant with slow insight, pleasant with swift insight — and works through the combinations at length.

For a teacher looking for one discourse from this chapter to use with a community rather than an individual, this is the one.

Key terms

sugata — “Holy One”, well-gone — one of the ten standard epithets, used here as the head-word for both the person and the training.
saddhamma — “the true teaching” — what the four things cause to decline and disappear, or to continue and endure.
dunnikkhitta — “badly placed” — of the words and phrases in a discourse learned incorrectly; the first cause of disappearance.
dubbaca — “hard to admonish” — the second cause; a community that cannot correct itself cannot repair anything else.
mūlacchinna — “cut off at the root” — what happens to discourses whose holders die without having made others recite them.

The text

The discourse in full: the Holy One and the training, four causes of disappearance, and the same four inverted. Translation: Bhikkhu Sujato (CC0, SuttaCentral).

For the welfare of the people

§1“Mendicants, a Holy One or a Holy One’s training remain in the world for the welfare and happiness of the people, out of sympathy for the world, for the benefit, welfare, and happiness of gods and humans.

§2And who is a Holy One? It’s when a Realized One arises in the world, perfected, a fully awakened Buddha, accomplished in knowledge and conduct, holy, knower of the world, supreme guide for those fit for training, teacher of gods and humans, awakened, blessed. This is a Holy One.

§3And what is the training of a Holy One? He proclaims a teaching that is good in the beginning, good in the middle, and good in the end, meaningful and well-phrased. And he reveals a spiritual practice that’s entirely full and pure. This is the training of a Holy One. This is how a Holy One or a Holy One’s training remain in the world for the welfare and happiness of the people, out of sympathy for the world, for the benefit, welfare, and happiness of gods and humans.

Four things that lead to disappearance

§4These four things lead to the decline and disappearance of the true teaching. What four? Firstly, the mendicants memorize discourses that they learned incorrectly, with misplaced words and phrases. When the words and phrases are misplaced, the meaning is misinterpreted. This is the first thing that leads to the decline and disappearance of the true teaching.

§5Furthermore, the mendicants are hard to admonish, having qualities that make them hard to admonish. They’re impatient, and don’t take instruction respectfully. This is the second thing that leads to the decline and disappearance of the true teaching.

§6Furthermore, the mendicants who are very learned—inheritors of the heritage, who have memorized the teachings, the monastic law, and the outlines—don’t carefully make others recite the discourses. When they pass away, the discourses are cut off at the root, with no-one to preserve them. This is the third thing that leads to the decline and disappearance of the true teaching.

§7Furthermore, the senior mendicants are indulgent and slack, leaders in backsliding, neglecting seclusion, not rousing energy for attaining the unattained, achieving the unachieved, and realizing the unrealized. Those who come after them follow their example. They too become indulgent and slack, leaders in backsliding, neglecting seclusion, not rousing energy for attaining the unattained, achieving the unachieved, and realizing the unrealized. This is the fourth thing that leads to the decline and disappearance of the true teaching. These are four things that lead to the decline and disappearance of the true teaching.

Four things that lead to continuation

§8These four things lead to the continuation, persistence, and enduring of the true teaching. What four? Firstly, the mendicants memorize discourses that have been learned correctly, with well placed words and phrases. When the words and phrases are well placed, the meaning is interpreted correctly. This is the first thing that leads to the continuation, persistence, and enduring of the true teaching.

§9Furthermore, the mendicants are easy to admonish, having qualities that make them easy to admonish. They’re patient, and take instruction respectfully. This is the second thing that leads to the continuation, persistence, and enduring of the true teaching.

§10Furthermore, the mendicants who are very learned—inheritors of the heritage, who have memorized the teachings, the monastic law, and the outlines—carefully make others recite the discourses. When they pass away, the discourses aren’t cut off at the root, and they have someone to preserve them. This is the third thing that leads to the continuation, persistence, and enduring of the true teaching.

§11Furthermore, the senior mendicants are not indulgent or slack, nor are they backsliders; instead, they take the lead in seclusion, rousing energy for attaining the unattained, achieving the unachieved, and realizing the unrealized. Those who come after them follow their example. They too aren’t indulgent or slack … This is the fourth thing that leads to the continuation, persistence, and enduring of the true teaching. These are four things that lead to the continuation, persistence, and enduring of the true teaching.”
