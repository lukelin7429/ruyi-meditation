Aṅguttara Nikāya · Discourse 8.33

Reasons to Give

Dānavatthusutta — Dānavagga — the fourth chapter of the Eights

Setting
None stated in the source
Speakers
The Buddha alone, addressing the mendicants
Form

Length
under 1 minute to read

Why this discourse

AN 8.33 names eight grounds for giving — out of favoritism, hostility, stupidity, or cowardice, out of family tradition, in hope of a heavenly rebirth, for the mental clarity and joy giving itself produces, or as equipment for the mind — overlapping with AN 8.31's own eight motives in only a couple of places.

Reading guide

The teaching in one sentence

People give for eight different grounds — the four biases of favoritism, hostility, stupidity, and cowardice; family tradition; hope of heavenly rebirth; the mental clarity and joy giving produces; or as equipment for the mind — named here without ranking, much as AN 8.31 named its own different eight.

Four biases, opening a different list

This discourse's own first four grounds — favoritism (chanda), hostility (dosa), stupidity (moha), and cowardice (bhaya) — are the four classic biases (agati) that corrupt judgment elsewhere in this literature, applied here specifically to the decision of whether and what to give, a starting point AN 8.31's list doesn't share at all.

Where the two lists genuinely converge

Despite their different openings, this discourse and AN 8.31 converge on two points: both name family or social obligation as a motive (framed here as honoring one's father and grandfather's own practice of giving), and both close with the identical final item, giving as “an adornment and requisite for the mind.”

Two lists, one honest catalog of human motive

Read together, AN 8.31 and AN 8.33 don't contradict each other; they simply survey the same broad territory — why people actually give — from two different angles, neither claiming to be exhaustive, both landing on the same final, most reflective motive.

Key terms

chandā, dosā, mohā, bhayā — “out of favoritism, hostility, stupidity, or cowardice” — this discourse's own opening four grounds, the four classic biases (agati) applied here to giving.
pitupitāmahaṁ — “my father and my father's father” — the family-tradition ground, framed here as inherited practice rather than personal conviction.
cittaṁ pasīdati, attamano hoti — “my mind becomes clear, and I become happy and joyful” — a ground focused on the immediate inner effect of giving itself, distinct from any external outcome.
cittālaṅkāraparikkhāranti — “this is an adornment and requisite for the mind” — the eighth and closing ground, identical in wording to AN 8.31's own final item.
dānavatthūni — “grounds for giving” — this discourse's own title term, distinct from AN 8.31's simple dānāni, 'gifts.'

The text

The discourse in full: eight grounds for giving, overlapping only partly with AN 8.31's own eight. Translation: Bhikkhu Sujato (CC0, SuttaCentral).

Eight grounds for giving

§1“Mendicants, there are these eight grounds for giving. What eight? Someone might give a gift out of favoritism or hostility or stupidity or cowardice. Or they give thinking, ‘Giving was practiced by my father and my father’s father. It would not be right for me to abandon this family tradition.’ Or they give thinking, ‘After I’ve given this gift, when my body breaks up, after death, I’ll be reborn in a good place, a heavenly realm.’ Or they give thinking, ‘When giving this gift my mind becomes clear, and I become happy and joyful.’ Or they give a gift thinking, ‘This is an adornment and requisite for the mind.’ These are the eight grounds for giving.”
