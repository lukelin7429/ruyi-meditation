Aṅguttara Nikāya · Discourse 4.123

Difference (1st)

Paṭhamanānākaraṇasutta — Bhayavagga — the thirteenth chapter of the Fours

Setting
None stated; the Bhayavagga gives no location for this discourse, and it is addressed to the mendicants directly
Speakers
The Buddha alone, addressing the mendicants
Form
Four parallel paragraphs, identical but for the absorption, the realm, and the lifespan
Length
~4 minutes to read
Northern parallel
Material on rebirth by absorption is common to the Chinese Āgamas and the Abhidharma cosmologies; this reading guide does not assert a specific matching sutra number
Difficulty
★★★★☆ — the form is mechanical, the claim inside it is not

Why this discourse

Two people reach the first absorption. Both enjoy it, both stay in it, both are reborn among the gods of the Divinity’s host and live there an eon. Then the lifespan runs out. One goes to hell, the animal realm, or the ghost realm; the other is extinguished there and then. The attainment was the same in both cases, so the attainment is not what made the difference — and the discourse repeats the point four times without ever saying what did.

Reading guide

The teaching in one sentence

Absorption produces a long divine life, and a long divine life is not a safe place; what makes it safe is being a noble disciple, which the discourse names but does not explain.

The machinery

Each of the four paragraphs is built the same way. Someone enters an absorption; they enjoy it and like it and find it rewarding; if they abide in it and do not lose it, they are reborn in a matching realm with a stated lifespan; the ordinary person falls afterwards and the Buddha’s disciple does not.

The four pairings are fixed: first absorption to the Divinity’s host, one eon; second to the gods of streaming radiance, two eons; third to the gods of universal beauty, four eons; fourth to the gods of abundant fruit, five hundred eons.

The lifespans are worth pausing on. One, two, four — and then five hundred. Nothing in the discourse accounts for the jump, and the fourth absorption is elsewhere the hinge of the whole system, the state from which the higher knowledges are reached. The numbers are doing what the doctrine does, in a currency a listener can feel.

The condition attached to all four is tattha ṭhito tadadhimutto tabbahulavihārī aparihīno — abiding in it, committed to it, dwelling in it often, not having fallen away from it. Rebirth follows only from an attainment that was kept.

The verb that is doing the damage

They enjoy it and like it and find it rewarding. In Pāli: tadassādeti, taṁ nikāmeti, tena ca vittiṁ āpajjati. Three verbs of relishing, stacked.

This is not incidental. It is the exact phrase AN 4.124 will remove and replace, and the removal is what changes the outcome. Read the two discourses together and the chapter has set up a controlled comparison with one variable: what the mind does with the absorption while it is in it.

So the first thing to say about this discourse is that it is not really about the absorptions. It is about relishing, which is the standing problem of the whole system, and it observes that relishing a very refined state is still relishing.

The fall, stated without cushioning

An ordinary person stays there until the lifespan of those gods is spent, then they go to hell or the animal realm or the ghost realm.

The sentence deserves to be read slowly, because it is doing something that later systematizing usually smooths over. After an eon in a divine realm reached by genuine meditative attainment, the destination named is not a slightly lower heaven or a fortunate human birth. It is the three bad destinations, all three, by name.

The discourse offers no mechanism. It does not say the merit was exhausted or that the person did something wrong up there. It simply reports where the ordinary person goes, and the reason it can report this without argument is that an ordinary person still has the full range of underlying tendencies intact, whatever they attained. The absorption suppressed them; it did not remove them.

The honest way to teach this is to leave the harshness in place. A reader who wants meditative attainment to be permanently safe will not find that here. AN 4.123 says attainment buys time, at an extraordinary rate of exchange, and nothing else.

‘A disciple of the Buddha’

The other half of each pairing is one clause long: but a disciple of the Buddha stays there until the lifespan of those gods is spent, then they’re extinguished in that very life — tasmiṁyeva bhave parinibbāyati, extinguished in that very existence, without a further one.

The summary line names the parties precisely: a learned noble disciple and an unlearned ordinary person, sutavā ariyasāvaka and assutavā puthujjana. Sutavā means having heard, and the difference the discourse insists on is finally a difference in what a person has been told and taken in.

That is thinner than a modern reader expects, and it is deliberately thin. Nowhere here is the noble disciple said to meditate better or longer. The whole distinction rests on having heard the teaching and having become, by that hearing, someone whose view is no longer reversible.

The last clause of every paragraph adds a qualification easy to miss: that is, when there is a place of rebirth, yadidaṁ gatiyā upapattiyā sati. The difference being described holds within the frame of rebirth. Where that frame does not apply, the comparison does not arise.

Reading it as a pair, and as a quartet

AN 4.123 and AN 4.124 are the same discourse with one thing changed, and AN 4.125 and AN 4.126 repeat the identical experiment using the four immeasurables instead of the four absorptions. Four discourses, two variables, a complete grid.

That is how this part of the chapter should be taught: not as four separate teachings but as one teaching stated with the alternatives filled in. The chapter heading calls the discourse Nānākaraṇa, difference-making, and what the four texts together make different is exactly one thing at a time.

For a reader who does not hold the cosmology, there is still something usable here, and it survives translation into any frame: a state that is genuinely good, genuinely reached, and genuinely enjoyed, is not thereby a state that has changed anything permanently. What the next discourse adds is what to do about that.

Key terms

nānākaraṇa — “difference”, literally difference-making — the word that titles this discourse and the next, and names what the pair is designed to isolate.
assādeti — “enjoys” — first of the three relishing verbs, with nikāmeti and vittiṁ āpajjati; the phrase AN 4.124 replaces.
puthujjana — “ordinary person”, literally one of the many folk — here qualified as assutavā, unlearned, one who has not heard.
ariyasāvaka — “noble disciple” — qualified as sutavā, learned, one who has heard; the whole difference in the discourse rests on this word.
parinibbāyati — “is extinguished” — here tasmiṁyeva bhave, in that very existence, so that the divine lifespan is the last one.

The text

The discourse in full: four absorptions, four realms, four lifespans, and the same closing comparison each time. Translation: Bhikkhu Sujato (CC0, SuttaCentral).

The first absorption

§1“Mendicants, these four individuals are found in the world. What four? Take an individual who, quite secluded from sensual pleasures, secluded from unskillful qualities, enters and remains in the first absorption, which has the rapture and bliss born of seclusion, while placing the mind and keeping it connected. They enjoy it and like it and find it rewarding. If they abide in that, are committed to it, and meditate on it often without losing it, when they die they’re reborn in the company of the gods of the Divinity’s host. The lifespan of the gods of the Divinity’s host is one eon. An ordinary person stays there until the lifespan of those gods is spent, then they go to hell or the animal realm or the ghost realm. But a disciple of the Buddha stays there until the lifespan of those gods is spent, then they’re extinguished in that very life. This is the difference between a learned noble disciple and an unlearned ordinary person, that is, when there is a place of rebirth.

The second absorption

§2Furthermore, take an individual who, as the placing of the mind and keeping it connected are stilled, enters and remains in the second absorption, which has the rapture and bliss born of immersion, with internal clarity and mind at one, without placing the mind and keeping it connected. They enjoy it and like it and find it rewarding. If they abide in that, are committed to it, and meditate on it often without losing it, when they die they’re reborn in the company of the gods of streaming radiance. The lifespan of the gods of streaming radiance is two eons. An ordinary person stays there until the lifespan of those gods is spent, then they go to hell or the animal realm or the ghost realm. But a disciple of the Buddha stays there until the lifespan of those gods is spent, then they’re extinguished in that very life. This is the difference between a learned noble disciple and an unlearned ordinary person, that is, when there is a place of rebirth.

The third absorption

§3Furthermore, take an individual who, with the fading away of rapture, enters and remains in the third absorption, where they meditate with equanimity, mindful and aware, personally experiencing the bliss of which the noble ones declare, ‘Equanimous and mindful, one meditates in bliss.’ They enjoy it and like it and find it rewarding. If they abide in that, are committed to it, and meditate on it often without losing it, when they die they’re reborn in the company of the gods of universal beauty. The lifespan of the gods of universal beauty is four eons. An ordinary person stays there until the lifespan of those gods is spent, then they go to hell or the animal realm or the ghost realm. But a disciple of the Buddha stays there until the lifespan of those gods is spent, then they’re extinguished in that very life. This is the difference between a learned noble disciple and an unlearned ordinary person, that is, when there is a place of rebirth.

The fourth absorption

§4Furthermore, take an individual who, giving up pleasure and pain, and ending former happiness and sadness, enters and remains in the fourth absorption, without pleasure or pain, with pure equanimity and mindfulness. They enjoy it and like it and find it rewarding. If they abide in that, are committed to it, and meditate on it often without losing it, when they die they’re reborn in the company of the gods of abundant fruit. The lifespan of the gods of abundant fruit is five hundred eons. An ordinary person stays there until the lifespan of those gods is spent, then they go to hell or the animal realm or the ghost realm. But a disciple of the Buddha stays there until the lifespan of those gods is spent, then they’re extinguished in that very life. This is the difference between a learned noble disciple and an unlearned ordinary person, that is, when there is a place of rebirth. These are the four individuals found in the world.”
