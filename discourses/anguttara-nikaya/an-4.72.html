Aṅguttara Nikāya · Discourse 4.72

Right View

Sammādiṭṭhisutta — Apaṇṇakavagga — the eighth chapter of the Fours

Why this discourse

The same sentence as AN 4.71 with a different four: thoughts of renunciation, good will, and harmlessness, and right view. Three thoughts and one view — and the three thoughts are exactly the negations of the three bad thoughts of AN 4.11. What makes a practice certain, on this version, is what the mind is doing when nobody is watching.

Reading guide

The teaching in one sentence

Three kinds of thought and one view, and having them makes the outcome certain.

Three thoughts, negatively defined

Nekkhammavitakka, abyāpādavitakka, avihiṁsāvitakka — thoughts of renunciation, of good will, of harmlessness. Each is the counterpart of one of the three bad thoughts AN 4.11 named: sensual, malicious, cruel.

Two of the three are grammatically negative in the Pāli. Abyāpāda is non-ill-will and avihiṁsā is non-cruelty; only nekkhamma, renunciation, is a positive term, and even that names a leaving rather than a taking up.

That is worth noticing because it is easy to read this list as three warm mental states to be cultivated. What is being described is more precisely the absence of three specific things, and the absence is what constitutes the presence. A mind not currently wanting, not currently hostile, and not currently wishing to injure is a mind in the three right thoughts.

Right view, and why it is fourth

Sammādiṭṭhi, right view, is the first factor of the eightfold path, and the three thoughts together constitute the second factor, right intention. So this discourse is the first two factors of the path, given as four items with the second factor split into its three components.

The ordering is reversed from the path’s. The path puts view first and intention second; this puts the three thoughts first and view last. The Aṅguttara does this kind of reordering freely and it is usually metrical or mnemonic rather than doctrinal.

What can be said is that view sits at the end as the item that holds the other three together. Renunciation, good will, and harmlessness could each be produced by temperament; right view is what makes them a path rather than a disposition.

Compared with AN 4.71

The two discourses make the same claim with two different fours. AN 4.71: ethical, learned, energetic, wise. AN 4.72: three right thoughts and right view.

The first list describes a person from outside — every item is something an observer could assess. The second describes the same person from inside; not one of the four is visible.

That pairing is probably why the two sit together at the head of the chapter. Between them they say that the sure bet has an outer and an inner description, and that neither is the whole account.

The word vitakka

Vitakka is thought in the sense of what the mind turns toward and settles on — applied thought, the initial movement rather than the elaboration. It is a technical term of the jhāna formulas, where it is present in the first absorption and gone by the second.

Used here it names something more ordinary: the kind of thinking a person does by default. What the discourse is assessing is not the content of a person’s deliberate reflection but where their mind goes when it is not being directed.

That is a demanding criterion and a fair one, and it is the same one AN 4.11 applied across the four postures. A person is not credited with renunciation for deciding to renounce; they are credited when their thoughts run that way unprompted.

Its place in the chapter

The Apaṇṇakavagga opens with these two four-line discourses and then turns to much longer material: how to recognize a true person, the Buddha’s last invitation to ask questions, the four unthinkables, the four purifications of a gift, and two discourses that answer questions about how the world works.

The chapter is one of the least unified in the Fours — its title comes from the first two discourses only, and what follows has no obvious connection to them. That is worth saying plainly rather than manufacturing a theme.

Key terms

nekkhammavitakka — “thought of renunciation” — the only positive term of the three, and even it names a leaving rather than a taking up.
abyāpāda — “good will”, literally non-ill-will — grammatically negative in the Pāli.
avihiṁsā — “harmlessness”, literally non-cruelty — the counterpart of the cruel thought of AN 4.11.
vitakka — “thought” — what the mind turns toward and settles on; the initial movement rather than the elaboration.
sammādiṭṭhi — “right view” — the first factor of the eightfold path, placed last here, and what makes the three thoughts a path rather than a disposition.

The text

The discourse in full. Translation: Bhikkhu Sujato (CC0, SuttaCentral).

Four things

§1“Mendicants, when a mendicant has four things their practice is a sure bet, and they have laid the groundwork for ending the defilements. What four? Thoughts of renunciation, good will, and harmlessness; and right view. When a mendicant possesses these four things their practice is a sure bet, and they have laid the groundwork for ending the defilements.”
