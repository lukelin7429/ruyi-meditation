Why this text

Chapter Eight opens with a change of register from Chapter Seven's riddles: a straightforward teaching verse naming three qualities — subtle and fine insight, calm and skillful thought, and mature, well-cultivated ethics — that together make extinguishment ‘not hard to gain’. It is also the first verse in this collection so far to name Nibbāna itself, rather than only gesturing toward it through a synonym.

Reading guide

Three qualities, one easy outcome

The verse is built as a single compound description, in the instrumental case, of a certain kind of person: one who sees the meaning ‘so very subtle and fine’, who is ‘skilled in thought and placid in manner’, and who has ‘cultivated mature ethics’. For a person of this profile, the verse concludes, extinguishment is not hard to gain. Unlike much of the preceding chapter, this is not a first-person testimony or a riddle to be puzzled out — it is a straightforward teaching about what makes the goal reachable.

The collection's first named Nibbāna

Across the seventy poems already covered in this collection, the goal has been named only through synonyms and cognates — ‘the state that does not pass’ in the collection's own opening frame (Thag 1.1), or the participle nibbuta, ‘quenched’, used by several elders earlier in the Book of the Ones. This verse is the first to use the noun Nibbāna itself. The word choice is not treated here as doctrinally significant in itself — the earlier poems clearly meant the same goal — but it is the first plain naming of it.

A name that echoes, cautiously

Vacchapāla's name reads literally as vaccha (‘calf’) plus pāla (‘guardian, herder’) — ‘calf-herder’, an occupation-flavored name. It sits alongside, but should not be folded into, the clan name Vaccha already carried by four monks earlier in this collection: Cūḷavaccha, Mahāvaccha, and Vanavaccha in Chapter Two, and Ukkhepakatavaccha in Chapter Seven. Vaccha the common noun for ‘calf’ and Vaccha the gotta (clan) name are different senses of the same word, and nothing here confirms that this monk's name draws on the clan sense at all.

An echo with the previous chapter's opening riddle

Chapter Seven opened with Vappa's verse (Thag 1.61), built entirely from four forms of passati, ‘sees’. This verse, opening Chapter Eight, is built around dassin, ‘one who sees’ — a word from the same suppletive verb ‘to see’ in Pali. The two chapter-openers otherwise share no content, but both reach for the same root at the start of a new chapter.

Key terms

dassin — “one who sees” — from the same suppletive verb as passati, the word Vappa's chapter-opening riddle (Thag 1.61) was built from.
matikusala — “skilled in thought” or “skilled in judgment” — one of the three qualities this verse names.
nivātavutti — “of placid manner”, literally “living without wind” — a calm, undisturbed way of conducting oneself.
saṁsevitavuddhasīlin — “one who has cultivated mature ethics” — the third of the verse's three qualities.
Nibbāna — “extinguishment” — the first time this word itself, rather than the cognate participle nibbuta, ‘quenched’, appears in this collection.

The text

The text in full: one verse, with no separate attribution line in this chapter. Translation: Bhikkhu Sujato (CC0, SuttaCentral).

§1For one who sees the meaning so very subtle and fine; who is skilled in thought and placid in manner; who has cultivated mature ethics, it’s not hard to gain extinguishment.
