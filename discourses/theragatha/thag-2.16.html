Why this text

Mahākāḷa describes a large, dark, crow-shaped woman breaking apart thigh-bones, arm-bones, and a skull, arranging them and sitting nearby — then reflects that an ignorant person who builds up attachments returns to suffering again and again, closing with a personal wish never to ‘lie with a broken skull’ again.

Reading guide

A charnel-ground image, stated plainly

The first verse describes a scene in the register of asubha, ‘foulness’, contemplation common to early Buddhist meditation practice: bones broken apart one by one and arranged nearby. The poem doesn't specify whether this is witnessed directly, recalled, or envisioned — this guide states only what the text itself describes, without adding narrative detail the poem doesn't supply.

A five-line verse, a third structural variation

This poem's first verse runs to five lines, a further variation from the book's standard four-line unit — distinct from the six-line extensions already seen at Thag 2.12 and Thag 2.14. Chapter Two's poems vary in length more freely than Chapter One's did.

Attachment, and a personal wish never to return

The second verse turns from image to reflection: an ignorant person who ‘builds up attachments’ (upadhi) ‘returns to suffering again and again’. The poem closes on a first-person wish — ‘may I never again lie with a broken skull’ — which this guide reads as an aspiration never to undergo this fate again through continued rebirth, though the poem itself doesn't spell out the connection explicitly.

A shared root between the speaker's name and the vision

Mahākāḷa's own name means ‘Great Dark One’, and the figure his verse describes is called ‘Kāḷī’, ‘the dark woman’ — the same root, masculine and feminine. Nothing in the text or in bilara-data comments on this shared root; this guide notes it only as an observable textual fact, not a claim about deliberate wordplay.

Key terms

dhaṅkarūpā — “crow-shaped” — describing the dark woman's appearance in the poem's opening line.
bhetvā — “having broken” — the repeated verb describing bones broken apart, one by one, across the first verse.
upadhiṁ — “attachment” or accumulation — what the second verse says an ignorant person builds up, leading to repeated suffering.
manda — “dullard” — who the second verse says returns to suffering again and again.
bhinnasiro — “with a broken skull” — the closing image of the poem's personal, first-person wish.

The text

The text in full: a five-line verse followed by a four-line verse. Translation: Bhikkhu Sujato (CC0, SuttaCentral).

§1There’s a big black woman who looks like a crow. She broke off thigh-bones, first one then another; she broke off arm-bones, first one then another; she broke off a skull like a curd-bowl, and then arranged them and sat nearby.

§2When an ignorant person builds up attachments, that dullard returns to suffering again and again. So let one who understands not build up attachments: may I never again lie with a broken skull!
