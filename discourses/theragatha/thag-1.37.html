Kumāputtasahāyaka

Why this text

Placed directly after Kumāputta's own verse, this poem belongs to a monk named only as his companion. Its warning — undisciplined wandering that loses meditation — is followed at once by a remedy: ‘dispel aggression, practicing absorption undistracted’.

Reading guide

A name built entirely from relationship

Kumāputtasahāyaka means, literally, ‘Kumāputta's companion’ — this monk carries no independent name of his own in this collection, placed directly after Kumāputta's own verse as a linked pair.

A warning echoing this project's earlier cautionary verse

Like Dāsaka's verse in Chapter Two, this poem criticizes a failing rather than praising an attainment — here, undisciplined wandering ‘to different countries’ that ends in losing meditation altogether, asking pointedly what such conduct could possibly achieve.

From warning to remedy, within the same verse

Unlike Dāsaka's verse, which ends on the warning itself, this one moves directly to a prescribed response: ‘dispel aggression, practicing absorption undistracted’ — the failing named, then immediately answered.

Key terms

nānājanapada — “various countries” — where the verse says undisciplined wanderers travel.
asaññata — “undisciplined” or “unrestrained” — describing this wandering.
samādhi — “immersion” or meditative concentration — what such wandering is said to lose.
sārambha — “aggression” or vehemence — what the verse's remedy instructs dispelling.
apurakkhata — “undistracted” — describing how absorption should be practiced, per this verse's closing instruction.

The text

The text in full: one verse, with no separate attribution line in this chapter. Translation: Bhikkhu Sujato (CC0, SuttaCentral).

§1Some travel to different countries, wandering undisciplined. If they lose their meditation, what will such rotten conduct achieve? So you should dispel aggression, practicing absorption undistracted.
