Why this text

‘There is no greater pleasure than this,’ Bhūta declares — nine times over, closing every single verse of the only poem in the entire Book of the Nines. What precedes that refrain shifts constantly: understanding suffering, abandoning craving, a thunderstorm over a mountain cave, a flower-strewn riverbank, lions roaring at midnight in the rain — nine entirely different occasions for the identical claim.

Reading guide

One poem, one book, nine verses, one refrain

The Book of the Nines is this collection's shortest yet — a single elder's single poem, nine verses of four lines each. Every verse, regardless of its content, closes on the identical line: ‘tato ratiṅ paramataraṅ na vindati’, ‘there is no greater pleasure than this’. This is the collection's most complete refrain yet — not five of eight verses, as in Thag 8.2, but all nine of nine, spanning the poem's entire length.

Four doctrinal verses, stated abstractly

The first four verses name philosophical content directly and without narrative: understanding that old age and death are suffering, destroying craving, seeing the eightfold path with wisdom, and developing the unconditioned, sorrowless state. Each is a complete teaching in miniature, joined only by the shared closing claim that follows it.

Three verses of vivid natural imagery

The fifth through seventh verses turn from doctrine to scene: a thundercloud rumbling while a monk meditates in a mountain cave; sitting on a riverbank ‘covered in flowers, garlanded with brightly colored forest plants’; midnight in a lonely forest, ‘lions roar as the heavens pour’. This is some of the most concretely pictorial writing in this collection — not illustrating a doctrinal point so much as simply presenting a scene, then attaching the same refrain regardless.

A structure that refuses to rank its own occasions

By repeating an identical superlative claim across content this varied — abstract philosophy and storm-lashed mountainsides given exactly the same praise — the poem declines to establish any hierarchy among its nine occasions. Understanding suffering and sitting by a flowering riverbank are not ranked against each other; both simply receive the same words.

A rhinoceros alone: the book's own closing epithet

The closing Tatruddāna, unusually for a book with only one poem, still supplies an epithet: ‘Bhūto tathaddaso thero, eko khaggavisāṇavā’, ‘the elder Bhūta, seer of things as they are, alone like a rhinoceros horn’ — directly invoking the solitary-wandering imagery of this site's own Snp 1.3, The Horned Rhino. The book then closes immediately, ‘Navakanipāto niṭṭhito’, with no separate chapter marker, and states its own tally directly: nine verses, matching the poem exactly.

Key terms

tato ratiṅ paramataraṅ na vindati — “there is no greater pleasure than this” — the refrain closing all nine verses without exception.
jhāyati — “practicing absorption” — the recurring verb naming the activity common to every verse's occasion.
magguttamaṅ sabbakilesasodhanaṅ — “the supreme path, cleanser of all corruptions” — the third verse's description of the eightfold way.
eko khaggavisāṇavā — “alone like a rhinoceros horn” — the closing uddāna's epithet for Bhūta, invoking this site's own Snp 1.3.
byantikatova jhāyati — “with all defilements annihilated, practicing absorption” — the closing image of the ninth and final verse.

The text

The text in full: nine verses, with no separate attribution line in this chapter. Translation: Bhikkhu Sujato (CC0, SuttaCentral).

§1When an astute person knows, “Old age and death are suffering; yet an ignorant ordinary person is bound to them”, completely understanding suffering, being mindful, practicing absorption: there is no greater pleasure than this.

§2When clinging, the carrier of suffering, and craving, the carrier of this painful mass of proliferation, are destroyed, and one is mindful, practicing absorption: there is no greater pleasure than this.

§3When the eightfold way, so full of grace, the supreme path, cleanser of all corruptions, is seen with wisdom; and one is mindful, practicing absorption: there is no greater pleasure than this.

§4When one develops that peaceful state, sorrowless, stainless, unconditioned, cleanser of all corruptions, cutter of fetters and bonds: there is no greater pleasure than this.

§5When the thundercloud rumbles in the sky, while the rain pours on the path of birds all around, and a monk has gone to a mountain cave, practicing absorption: there is no greater pleasure than this.

§6When sitting on a riverbank covered in flowers, garlanded with brightly colored forest plants, one is truly happy, practicing absorption: there is no greater pleasure than this.

§7When it is midnight in a lonely forest, and the lions roar as the heavens pour, and a monk has gone to a mountain cave, practicing absorption: there is no greater pleasure than this.

§8When one’s own thoughts have been cut off, between the mountains, sheltered inside a cleft, without stress or heartlessness, practicing absorption: there is no greater pleasure than this.

§9When one is happy, destroyer of stains, heartlessness, and sorrow, free of obstructions, snarls, and thorns, and with all defilements annihilated, practicing absorption: there is no greater pleasure than this.
