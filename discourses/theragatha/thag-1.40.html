Vaḍḍhamāna

Why this text

This verse repeats Tissa's opening almost exactly — struck by a sword, head on fire — but redirects its urgency toward a different target: not sensual desire, but bhavarāga, desire for continued existence. Sujato's note adds a commentarial story behind the verse, and a cautious aside connecting Vaḍḍhamāna's name to the founder of Jainism.

Reading guide

The same template as the poem just before it, one word changed

‘Struck by a sword, head on fire, a mendicant should wander mindful’ matches Tissa's verse (Thag 1.39) almost word for word. Only the final target changes: kāmarāga, sensual desire, becomes bhavarāga, desire for continued existence — two of early Buddhism's three classic cravings, named in sequence across these two neighboring poems.

A commentarial story explaining the verse's occasion

Sujato's note reports that, per the commentary, Vaḍḍhamāna committed some offense as a layman serious enough that the Buddha overturned his alms bowl against him — a formal act of censure the note describes as ‘like stepping on a fire’. He repented, asked forgiveness, and later ordained — but grew lazy, prompting the Buddha to address him with this verse.

A cautious aside about the Jain founder

The note also observes that the commentary places Vaḍḍhamāna's birth in the royal Licchavī family of Vesālī — the same clan and region traditionally associated with the Jain founder Mahāvīra Vaḍḍhamāna. Sujato raises the possibility that this verse was meant to needle the Jain founder by name, while stating plainly that the commentary itself never draws this connection — a speculation offered, not a claim confirmed.

A monk otherwise unknown

Unlike Gavampati in the poem just two before this one, praised for a specific named feat, Sujato's note describes this Vaḍḍhamāna as ‘unknown elsewhere’ in the canon — his only surviving trace outside the commentarial story is this single verse.

A chapter's own close, left untranslated

As at the end of Chapters One through Three, the Pali text here carries vaggo catuttho, ‘the fourth chapter is finished’, followed by an uddāna naming all ten monks of this chapter in sequence: Gahvaratīriya, Suppiya, Sopāka, Posiya, Sāmaññakāni, Kumāputta, Kumāputtasahāyaka, Gavampati, Tissa, and Vaḍḍhamāna. Sujato's translation leaves both untranslated, and neither appears in this page's text below.

Key terms

bhavarāga — “desire for continued existence” — the target this verse redirects Tissa's urgency toward.
pattanikkujjana — the formal act of “overturning the bowl” against someone — the censure the commentary says the Buddha used against Vaḍḍhamāna as a layman.
Licchavī — the clan the commentary associates with Vaḍḍhamāna's birth — the same clan traditionally linked to the Jain founder Mahāvīra.
paribbaje — “should wander” — the verse's closing instruction, shared word for word with Tissa's verse (Thag 1.39).
vaggo catuttho — “the fourth chapter is finished” — the untranslated Pali colophon closing this chapter.

The text

The text in full: one verse, with no separate attribution line in this chapter. Translation: Bhikkhu Sujato (CC0, SuttaCentral).

§1Like they’re struck by a sword, like their head was on fire, a mendicant should wander mindful, to give up desire for rebirth.
