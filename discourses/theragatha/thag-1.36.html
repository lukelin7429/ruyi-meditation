Why this text

‘Learning is good, living well is good, the life without abode is always good’ — three short claims, each opening with the same word, before the verse narrows to something precise: ‘questions on the meaning, actions that are skillful’, named directly as ‘the ascetic life for one who has nothing’.

Reading guide

A threefold refrain, then a precise definition

Sādhu, ‘good’, opens all three of the verse's first claims — learning, living well, homelessness — before the final two lines shift register entirely, defining exactly what ‘the ascetic life’ consists of rather than simply praising it further.

Homelessness named as a virtue in itself

Aniketavihāra, ‘dwelling without a fixed abode’, is called good ‘always’ — not merely a circumstance forced on a renunciant, but a quality actively valued in its own right.

A term shared with Gosāla's verse earlier in this chapter

‘Skillful action’ translates padakkhiṇakamma, sharing its root with padakkhiṇaṁ, the term Sujato's note on Gosāla's verse (Thag 1.23) explained as playing on dakkha, ‘skilled’ — the same word family surfacing a second time within this same chapter.

Key terms

sādhu — “good” — the word opening all three of this verse's first claims.
aniketavihāra — “dwelling without a fixed abode” — homelessness, named as a virtue in its own right.
atthapucchana — “questioning about the meaning” — the first of two activities this verse defines the ascetic life by.
padakkhiṇakamma — “skillful action” — sharing its root with a term already noted in Gosāla's verse (Thag 1.23) earlier in this chapter.
akiñcana — “one who has nothing” — the verse's closing description of the ascetic life's practitioner.

The text

The text in full: one verse, with no separate attribution line in this chapter. Translation: Bhikkhu Sujato (CC0, SuttaCentral).

§1Learning is good, living well is good, the life without abode is always good. Questions on the meaning, actions that are skillful: this is the ascetic life for one who has nothing.
