Theragatha · 12.2

Sunīta

Sunīta — The Book of the Twelves · Chapter One · Poem 2 of 2

Setting
A roadside encounter with the Buddha entering the Magadhan capital, then a night of solitary practice
Speaker
Sunīta, closing this book's single chapter
Form
Twelve four-line verses, in clear chronological sequence
Length
under 1 minute to read
Northern parallel
This reading guide does not assert a specific matching text for this poem in other Buddhist literatures.
Difficulty
★★☆☆☆ — plain narrative vocabulary, one of this collection's most striking social reversals

Why this text

‘I was born in a low-class family. We were poor, with little to eat. My job was lowly — I threw out the old flowers,’ Sunīta opens — ‘shunned by people... disregarded and held in contempt’. What follows is one of this collection's most complete reversals: a personal welcome from the Buddha himself, awakening within a single night, and homage from Indra and Brahmā, closing on the Buddha's own declaration of what actually makes someone a brahmin.

Reading guide

A despised occupation, named without euphemism

The opening two verses state Sunīta's circumstances plainly: low birth, poverty, ‘I threw out the old flowers’, ‘shunned... disregarded and held in contempt’. Rather than softening this description, the poem states it directly as the starting condition the rest of the narrative will answer.

The supreme man stops, out of sympathy for the lowest

The fourth verse's central action is small but significant: Sunīta drops the tool of his despised work to approach the passing Buddha, and ‘out of sympathy for me, the supreme man stood still’. The Buddha, entering a capital city at the head of the Saṅgha, pauses specifically for the person society has placed lowest.

Ordination in four words

‘Come, monk!’ — ‘that was my ordination’, Sunīta reports, echoing the same immediate, spoken formula already found elsewhere in this collection (Thag 7.3, Thag 7.4). No distinction of birth interrupts or delays the process; the invitation is identical to the one given to anyone else the Buddha ordains this way.

Full awakening across the three watches of a single night

The eighth verse structures Sunīta's attainment precisely: past lives recollected in the first watch, clairvoyance purified in the middle watch, ‘the mass of darkness’ shattered in the last watch — the classical three-stage sequence of a single night's breakthrough, achieved ‘staying alone in the wilderness’ with no further training period described.

Homage from the king of the gods himself

At dawn, ‘Indra and the Divinity’ (Brahmā) come in person to revere the newly awakened Sunīta — ‘homage to you, supreme among men... you, good fellow, are worthy of a religious donation’. The poem places the very deities that traditional brahminical religion held supreme in the position of worshippers before a man that same social order had placed at its lowest rank.

A closing verse redefining brahminhood itself

Seeing Sunīta honored by the assembly of gods, the Buddha smiles and declares: ‘by fervor and spiritual practice, by restraint and by self-control: that's how to become a brahmin, this is the supreme brahmin’. This directly echoes the argument already found on this site in Snp 1.7, The Lowlife, and throughout Dhammapada Chapter 26, Brahmins — that birth determines nothing about spiritual worth, and the word ‘brahmin’ itself, the highest social rank in the culture surrounding this teaching, is reclaimed here for conduct alone.

Closing the Book of the Twelves, with a collective epithet

This single-chapter book closes, like every book since the Threes, moving directly from Sunīta's own self-naming to ‘Dvādasakanipāto niṭṭhito’, ‘the Book of the Twelves is concluded’, with no separate chapter marker. The Tatruddāna names both elders together — Sīlava and Sunīta — under a single shared epithet, ‘therā dve te mahiddhikā’, ‘these two elders, of great psychic power’, rather than naming either individually: a ninth distinct epithet-placement pattern in this collection, and the first to describe both elders of a book with one collective phrase instead of separate epithets. The stated tally, ‘twenty-four verses’, matches two poems of twelve verses each exactly, a fifth consecutive book with a closing count that adds up cleanly.

Key terms

pupphachaḍḍako — “flower-disposer” — Sunīta's own description of his low-status occupation before going forth.
‘ehi bhikkhū’ti — “Come, monk!” — the Buddha's entire ordination formula for Sunīta, identical to the invitation given elsewhere in this collection regardless of birth.
rattiyā paṭhamaṁ yāmaṁ... pacchime yāme — “the first watch of the night... the last watch” — the three-stage structure of Sunīta's single-night awakening.
Indo brahmā ca — “Indra and the Divinity [Brahmā]” — the two deities who come personally to honor the newly awakened Sunīta at dawn.
etaṁ brāhmaṇamuttamaṁ — “this is the supreme brahmin” — the Buddha's own closing redefinition, echoing this site's Snp 1.7 and Dhammapada Chapter 26.

The text

The text in full: twelve verses, with no separate attribution line in this chapter. Translation: Bhikkhu Sujato (CC0, SuttaCentral).

§1I was born in a low-class family. We were poor, with little to eat. My job was lowly—I threw out the old flowers.

§2Shunned by people, I was disregarded and held in contempt. I humbled my heart and paid respects to many people.

§3Then I saw the Buddha at the fore of the mendicant Saṅgha; the great hero was entering the capital city of the Magadhans.

§4I dropped my flail and approached to pay homage. Out of sympathy for me, the supreme man stood still.

§5When I had paid homage at the Teacher’s feet, I stood to one side and asked the supreme being for the going-forth.

§6Then the Teacher, being sympathetic, and having sympathy for the whole world, said to me, “Come, monk!” That was my ordination.

§7Staying alone in the wilderness, meditating tirelessly, I have completed what the Teacher taught, just as the victor advised me.

§8In the first watch of the night, I recollected my past lives. In the middle watch, I purified my clairvoyance. In the last watch, I shattered the mass of darkness.

§9At the end of the night, as the sunrise drew near, Indra and the Divinity came and revered me with joined hands.

§10“Homage to you, O thoroughbred! Homage to you, supreme among men! Since your defilements are ended, you, good fellow, are worthy of a religious donation.”

§11When he saw me honored by the assembly of gods, the Teacher smiled and said the following:

§12“By fervor and spiritual practice, by restraint and by self-control: that’s how to become a brahmin, this is the supreme brahmin.”
