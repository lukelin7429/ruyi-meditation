Theragatha · 16.8

Aṅgulimāla

Aṅgulimāla — The Book of the Twenties · Chapter One · Poem 8 of 10

Setting
A chase through the wilderness, resolving into immediate ordination
Speaker
Two voices at first — Aṅgulimāla and the Buddha — then Aṅgulimāla alone for the poem's remainder
Form
Twenty-six verses, the second half extending beyond the episode most commonly told elsewhere
Length
1–2 minutes to read
Northern parallel
This reading guide does not assert a specific matching text for this poem in other Buddhist literatures.
Difficulty
★★★☆☆ — one of the canon's most famous exchanges, with substantial material beyond it

Why this text

‘While walking, ascetic, you say “I've stopped.” And I have stopped, but you tell me I've not,’ the pursuing bandit demands — and receives one of the canon's most celebrated replies: ‘I have forever stopped — I've laid aside violence towards all creatures. But you can't stop yourself from harming living creatures; that's why I've stopped, but you have not.’ Sujato's comment notes that everything after verse twenty-one — Aṅgulimāla's own account of his life after ordination — isn't found in this site's own more detailed prose telling at all.

Reading guide

A pun resolved into the poem's whole meaning

The opening exchange turns entirely on one word: ‘ṭhita’, ‘stopped’. Aṅgulimāla means it literally — the ascetic he's chasing claims to have stopped while still walking. The Buddha's answer redefines the word entirely: he has ‘forever stopped’ harming living beings, while Aṅgulimāla, mid-pursuit with a sword in hand, has not. Sujato's comment notes this same story is told in far more narrative detail, including most of this poem's verses, on this site's own MN 86, With Aṅgulimāla.

Weapons thrown into an abyss, ordination granted on the spot

Four short verses carry the entire conversion: Aṅgulimāla hurls his sword down a cliff, bows at the Buddha's feet, asks for the going-forth immediately, and receives it in four words, ‘Come, monk!’ — the same instant, spoken formula already found elsewhere in this collection (Thag 7.3, Thag 7.4, Thag 12.2), here granted to a man whose name was, until moments before, a byword for terror.

A wish for well-being extended specifically to his own enemies

Verses nine through eleven make an extraordinary request: ‘may even my enemies hear a Dhamma talk... may even my enemies devote themselves to the Buddha's teaching’, so that ‘they'd never wish harm upon myself or others’. Given Aṅgulimāla's own history, this is loving-kindness extended precisely toward the people with the strongest possible grievance against him.

Karmic debt described as already paid, in his own words

The seventeenth verse states plainly: ‘the result of my deeds has already struck me, so I enjoy my food free of debt’ — a first-person acknowledgment that consequences for his former violence had already found him, spoken without elaboration on what those consequences were, in the same matter-of-fact register as the rest of his testimony.

A wordplay on his own new name

The fourteenth verse plays directly on identity: ‘my name is “Harmless” (Ahiṁsaka), though I used to be harmful. The name I bear today is true, for I do no harm to anyone’ — Aṅgulimāla's actual given name, ironic in his bandit years, finally accurate.

Verses that go beyond the story told elsewhere on this site

Sujato's comment states directly that the verses from twenty-two onward ‘are not found in MN 86’. This closing section reveals new material: an anxious mind ‘wherever he stood’ in his early days as a monk, later resolving into genuinely happy, untroubled living; and a detail absent from the more famous telling entirely — that Aṅgulimāla was ‘of brahmin birth, highborn on both sides’ before his life as a bandit.

Key terms

ṭhito — “stopped” — the single word the opening exchange's entire meaning turns on, redefined from literal motion to the cessation of harm.
‘tamehi bhikkhū’ti — “Come, monk!” — the Buddha's entire ordination formula, the same instant pattern found elsewhere in this collection.
disāpi me... hiṁse — “may even my enemies... never wish harm” — loving-kindness extended toward those with the strongest grievance against him.
phuṭṭho kammavipākena — “the result of my deeds has already struck me” — his own first-person acknowledgment of karmic consequence already experienced.
‘Ahiṁsako’ti me nāmaṁ — “my name is ‘Harmless’” — his own given name, ironic through his bandit years, finally made accurate.

The text

The text in full: twenty-six verses, with no separate attribution line in this chapter. Translation: Bhikkhu Sujato (CC0, SuttaCentral).

§1“While walking, ascetic, you say ‘I’ve stopped.’ And I have stopped, but you tell me I’ve not. I’m asking you this, ascetic: how is it you’ve stopped and I have not?”

§2“Aṅgulimāla, I have forever stopped—I’ve laid aside violence towards all creatures. But you can’t stop yourself from harming living creatures; that’s why I’ve stopped, but you have not.”

§3“Oh, at long last a renowned great seer, an ascetic has followed me into this deep wood. Now that I’ve heard your verse on Dhamma, I shall discard a thousand evils.”

§4With these words, the bandit hurled his sword and weapons down a cliff into an abyss. He venerated the Holy One’s feet, and asked the Buddha for the going forth right away.

§5Then the Buddha, the compassionate great seer, the teacher of the world with its gods, said to him, “Come, monk!” And with that he became a monk.

§6“He who once was heedless, but turned to heedfulness, lights up the world, like the moon freed from clouds.

§7Someone whose bad deed is supplanted by the good, lights up the world, like the moon freed from clouds.

§8A young mendicant devoted to the Buddha’s teaching, lights up the world, like the moon freed from clouds.

§9May even my enemies hear a Dhamma talk! May even my enemies devote themselves to the Buddha’s teaching! May even my enemies associate with those good men who establish others in the Dhamma!

§10May even my enemies hear Dhamma at the right time, from those who teach acceptance, praising acquiescence; and may they follow that path!

§11For then they’d never wish harm upon myself or others. Having arrived at ultimate peace, they’d look after creatures firm and frail.

§12For irrigators guide the water, and fletchers straighten arrows; carpenters carve timber—but the astute tame themselves.

§13Some tame by using the rod, some with goads, and some with whips. But the unaffected one tamed me without rod or sword.

§14My name is ‘Harmless’, though I used to be harmful. The name I bear today is true, for I do no harm to anyone.

§15I used to be a bandit, the notorious Aṅgulimāla. Swept away in a great flood, I went to the Buddha for refuge.

§16I used to have blood on my hands, the notorious Aṅgulimāla. See the refuge I’ve found—the leash to existence is eradicated.

§17I’ve done many of the sort of deeds that lead to a bad destination. The result of my deeds has already struck me, so I enjoy my food free of debt.

§18Fools and simpletons devote themselves to negligence. But the wise protect diligence as their best treasure.

§19Don’t devote yourself to negligence, or delight in erotic intimacy. For if you’re diligent and practice absorption, you’ll attain ultimate happiness.

§20It was welcome, not unwelcome, the advice I got was good. Of the well-explained teachings, I arrived at the best.

§21It was welcome, not unwelcome, the advice I got was good. I’ve attained the three knowledges, and fulfilled the Buddha’s instructions.”

§22“In the wilderness, at a tree’s root, on mountains, or in caves—it used to be that wherever I stood, my mind was anxious.

§23But now I lie down happily and stand up happily, I live my life happily, out of Māra’s reach; the teacher had sympathy for me.

§24I used to be of brahmin birth, highborn on both sides, now I’m a son of the Holy One, the Teacher, King of Dhamma.

§25I am rid of craving, free of grasping, my sense doors are guarded and well-restrained. I’ve destroyed the root of gloom, and attained the ending of defilements.

§26I’ve served the teacher and fulfilled the Buddha’s instructions. The heavy burden is laid down, the leash to existence is eradicated.”
