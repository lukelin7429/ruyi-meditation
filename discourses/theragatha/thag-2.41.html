Why this text

Kassapa the Prince opens Chapter Five not with a first-person report of his own attainment, but with an exclamation — ‘Oh, the Buddhas! Oh, the Dhammas! Oh, the accomplishments of the Teacher!’ — before widening into a general statement about disciples who, after countless eons, reach their very last body and see transmigration through births and deaths come to an end.

Reading guide

An exclamation, not a declaration

Most poems in this collection report the speaker's own attainment directly. This one opens instead with three exclamations in a row — praising the Buddhas, the teachings, and the Teacher's accomplishments — before naming what makes this teaching remarkable: ‘here a disciple may realize such a teaching for themselves’. The register is closer to celebration than autobiography, fitting for a monk this site can independently confirm was known for eloquence.

An extended second verse

Where most poems in this chapter run two four-line verses, this one's second verse runs to six lines, widening from a single disciple's realization to a general statement about beings who, ‘through countless eons’, obtain their last and final body and reach the end of transmigration through births and deaths — ‘now there’ll be no more future lives’.

A monk this site can confirm, not merely guess at

Sujato's own comment identifies this ‘Kassapa the Prince’ as Kumārakassapa, ordained at twenty, who appears in this site's own MN 23 (questioned by a deity about a riddling anthill) and DN 23 (winning over the skeptic Pāyāsi through a series of similes), and who is named foremost in brilliant speech at AN 1.209–218, §217. This is a materially different situation from Thag 1.82's bare ‘Kassapa’, one of the early canon's most common names, where no such identification was possible — here, the fuller name Kumārakassapa and a surviving comment together confirm a specific, well-documented monk.

Key terms

sakkāyādhigatā — “obtained individual existence” — describing what beings acquire through countless eons before their final body.
carimoyaṁ samussayo — “this final body” — the last embodiment before transmigration ends.
jātimaraṇasaṁsāro — “the transmigration through births and deaths” — what the verse says comes to an end.
natthi dāni punabbhavo — “now there’ll be no more future lives” — a closing formula found elsewhere in this collection, including later in this same chapter.
Kumārakassapa — “Kassapa the Prince” — the full name behind this poem's title, distinguishing this well-documented monk from the many other Kassapas in the early canon.

The text

The text in full: one four-line verse and one six-line verse, without a translated attribution. Translation: Bhikkhu Sujato (CC0, SuttaCentral).

§1Oh, the Buddhas! Oh, the Dhammas! Oh, the accomplishments of the Teacher! Here a disciple may realize such a teaching for themselves.

§2Through countless eons they obtained substantial forms. This is their last, their very final body in the transmigration through births and deaths; now there’ll be no more future lives.
