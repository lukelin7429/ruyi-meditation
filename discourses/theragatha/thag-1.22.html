Why this text

Crested peacocks with beautiful blue necks cry out at Karaṁvī, playing in a cool breeze — and their cries, this verse says, ‘wake the sleeper to practice absorption’. Unlike Vanavaccha's nature poem in Chapter Two, admired purely for its own sake, this scene is given a direct practical function.

Reading guide

Nature imagery given a purpose

Vanavaccha's rocky crags, cool streams, and ladybugs in Chapter Two closed simply in delight: ‘these rocky crags delight me!’. Here, comparably vivid imagery — crested peacocks, blue necks, a cool breeze — serves a stated end: rousing a sleeping meditator to practice.

Peacocks at play, not merely present

The verse describes the peacocks as sītavātakīḷitā, ‘playing in the cool breeze’ — a livelier image than simply being stirred by wind, giving the birds themselves an active, almost joyful role in the scene.

A short chain from breeze to practice

The verse traces a small causal sequence: a cool breeze moves the peacocks to play, their play produces cries, and those cries wake a sleeper into meditative absorption — an ordinary natural event given a spiritual outcome.

Key terms

mora — “peacock” — the verse's central image, described as crested and blue-necked.
nīlā sugīvā — “blue-necked” — the descriptive compound opening this verse.
sītavātakīḷitā — “playing in the cool breeze” — describing the peacocks' lively activity, not mere passive stirring.
suttaṁ — “the sleeper” — the one roused by the peacocks' cries.
nibodhenti — “they wake” or “they rouse” — the verb naming this verse's stated outcome.

The text

The text in full: one verse, with no separate attribution line in this chapter. Translation: Bhikkhu Sujato (CC0, SuttaCentral).

§1Crested peacocks with beautiful blue necks cry out in Karaṁvī. Stirred by a cool breeze, they wake the sleeper to practice absorption.
