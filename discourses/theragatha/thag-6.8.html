Migajāla

Migajāla — The Book of the Sixes · Chapter One · Poem 8 of 14

Why this text

Unlike most poems in this collection, Migajāla's verse narrates no episode at all — six verses simply praise the teaching itself, in cascading epithets: emancipating, drying up craving's root, smashing the mechanism of deeds, dropping the thunderbolt of knowledge, and, at its close, the noble eightfold path itself, ‘the stilling of suffering, bliss.’

Reading guide

A poem with no narrative frame at all

Where most poems in this collection open on an event, a location, or an interlocutor, this poem opens directly on the qualities of the teaching itself: ‘it was well-taught by the Clear-eyed One, the Buddha, Kinsman of the Sun’. No crisis, no dialogue, no charnel ground — just six verses of sustained praise.

A cascade of active, almost violent verbs

The teaching is described as drying up (vis-osana), cutting off (chetvā), smashing (vighāṭano), and dropping a thunderbolt (vajiranipātano) — forceful, active language for what is elsewhere in this collection often described in gentler terms of peace and stilling. The third verse in particular pairs breaking (bhedāya) unknowing's root with the thunderbolt image, an unusually martial register for a description of insight.

Existence itself as a pit of burning coals

The fourth verse's closing image, ‘bhavaṅ aṅgārakāsuṅva, ñāṇena anupassano’, ‘contemplating with understanding all states of existence as a pit of burning coals’, gives one of this collection's starker images for the danger of continued rebirth — not a burden to be endured, but active danger to be seen clearly and avoided.

A caution about the name Migajāla

The Saṅyutta Nikāya records teachings to a monk named Migajāla (SN 35.63–64, not yet translated on this site) on solitary dwelling and the six sense fields. This reading guide follows this collection's established practice of not asserting an identification on name alone: without a comment or internal textual marker confirming the connection, this poem's Migajāla and the Saṅyutta figure are treated as possibly, not confirmedly, the same person.

The eightfold path itself, called bliss

The sixth and final verse, extended to six lines, closes on the noble eightfold path directly: ‘mahāraso sugambhīro... ariyo aṭṭhaṅgiko maggo, dukkhūpasamano sivo’, ‘very sweet and very deep... the noble eightfold path, the stilling of suffering, bliss’ — naming the path as itself pleasurable, not merely instrumental to a pleasure that comes later.

Key terms

ādiccabandhunā — “Kinsman of the Sun” — an epithet for the Buddha, from the solar lineage claimed by the Sakyan clan.
taṇhāmūlavisosano — “drying up the root of craving” — one of several active, forceful verbs describing the teaching's effect.
bhavaṅ aṅgārakāsuṅva — “all states of existence as a pit of burning coals” — the poem's starkest image for continued rebirth.
ariyo aṭṭhaṅgiko maggo — “the noble eightfold path” — named directly in the closing verse, called bliss in itself.
dukkhūpasamano sivo — “the stilling of suffering, bliss” — the poem's final words.

The text

The text in full: six verses, the sixth extended to six lines, with no separate attribution line in this chapter. Translation: Bhikkhu Sujato (CC0, SuttaCentral).

§1It was well-taught by the Clear-eyed One, the Buddha, Kinsman of the Sun, who has transcended all fetters, and destroyed all rolling-on.

§2Emancipating, it leads across, drying up the root of craving, and, having cut off the poisonous root, the slaughterhouse, it leads to quenching.

§3By breaking the root of unknowing, it smashes the mechanism of deeds, and drops the thunderbolt of knowledge on the taking up of consciousnesses.

§4It informs us of our feelings, releasing us from grasping, contemplating with understanding all states of existence as a pit of burning coals.

§5It’s very sweet and very deep, holding birth and death at bay; it is the noble eightfold path—the stilling of suffering, bliss.

§6Knowing deed as deed and result as result; seeing dependently originated phenomena as if they were in a clear light; leading to the great sanctuary and peace, it’s excellent at the end.
