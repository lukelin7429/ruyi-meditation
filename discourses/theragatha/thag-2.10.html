Why this text

Vasabha closes Chapter One with two separate teachings: a hunting image where self-harm becomes bait for harming others, and a claim that a brahmin's true color is worn on the inside — addressed, without explanation, to Sujampati, an epithet for Sakka — followed in the Pali text by this chapter's own colophon and ten-name mnemonic verse.

Reading guide

Self-harm as bait for harming others

The first verse pictures someone who ‘first kills oneself, then kills others’, comparing this to a hunter who kills one bird and then uses its own corpse as a decoy (vītaṁsa) to lure and kill more. The image makes self-corruption instrumental: harming oneself first isn't the end of the damage, but the means by which further harm to others becomes possible.

A brahmin's color, worn on the inside

The second verse states plainly that ‘a brahmin's color is not on the outside; a brahmin is colored on the inside’, then names the criterion directly: whoever harbors bad deeds is truly a ‘dark one’, regardless of birth or appearance. This site's own Snp 1.7, ‘The Lowlife’, develops the same deeds-not-birth theme in fuller narrative form — ‘you're a lowlife by your deeds, by deeds you're a brahmin’.

Addressed to Sakka, without explanation

The verse's closing word, ‘Sujampati’, is a standard epithet for Sakka, ‘husband of Sujā’. Nothing in the poem explains why this teaching about brahmins and dark deeds is addressed to him specifically — this guide notes the address as a textual fact without supplying a narrative reason the poem itself doesn't give.

A chapter's own closing line, left untranslated

Immediately after this poem, the Pali carries vaggo paṭhamo, ‘the first chapter is finished’ — the same untranslated structural colophon already seen closing every chapter of the Book of the Ones.

A mnemonic verse, naming all ten monks with an added epithet

The Pali then carries this chapter's own uddāna, naming all ten monks in sequence: Uttara, Piṇḍola, Valliya, Tīriya (an abbreviation of Gaṅgātīriya, here called ‘isi’, sage), Ajina, Meḷajina, Rādha, Surādha, Gotama, and Vasabha — closing with a collective epithet applied to all ten together, ‘mahiddhikā’, ‘of great power’. This mnemonic, like the chapter colophon before it, is absent from Sujato's translation and not included in this page's text.

Key terms

vītaṁsa — a “decoy” — a dead bird used by a hunter to lure and kill more birds, the poem's central image for self-harm turned instrumental.
vaṇṇo — “color” — what the second verse says a brahmin carries on the inside, not the outside.
kaṇho — “dark one” — what the verse calls anyone who harbors bad deeds, regardless of birth.
Sujampati — “husband of Sujā” — a standard epithet for Sakka, addressed at this verse's close without explanation.
vaggo — “chapter” — the structural unit this poem closes, marked in the untranslated Pali colophon ‘vaggo paṭhamo’, ‘the first chapter is finished’.

The text

The text in full: two verses, without a translated attribution. Translation: Bhikkhu Sujato (CC0, SuttaCentral).

§1First one kills oneself, then one kills others. One kills oneself, really dead, like one who kills birds using a dead bird as a decoy.

§2A brahmin’s color is not on the outside; a brahmin is colored on the inside. Whoever harbors bad deeds is truly a dark one, Sujampati.
