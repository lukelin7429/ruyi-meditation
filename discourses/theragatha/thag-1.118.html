Kimbila (1st)

Why this text

Kimbila describes old age falling ‘like a curse’ — the same body now feeling like someone else's — and yet insists in the same breath that he is ‘still the same’, that he ‘hasn't been away’. The verse holds both the estrangement and the continuity together, without resolving which is truer.

Reading guide

Old age as a curse falling

Abhisattova nipatati, ‘it falls like a curse’, doesn't describe old age as a slow, gradual process — it captures the subjective jolt of noticing it, as though struck suddenly rather than worn into over years. The comparison is to being cursed, hexed: something done to you, abruptly, rather than something you simply age into.

A paradox held, not resolved

The verse's second half states two things that seem to contradict each other and leaves them both standing: ‘I remember myself as if I were someone else’ — a felt estrangement from one's own past — set directly against ‘I'm still the same, I haven't been away’ — a flat denial of that same estrangement. The verse doesn't pick a side or explain how both can be true; it simply reports the felt experience of aging as containing both at once.

A name shared with a well-documented figure

Kimbila is also the name of one of a well-known trio of forest-dwelling monastic friends — alongside Anuruddha and Nandiya — already documented on this site in MN 31 and MN 128, praised there for practicing together in exceptional harmony. This is a considerably less common name than several other ‘same name’ cases in this collection, though without a surviving comment confirming the connection, this reading guide notes it as a plausible but unconfirmed fit rather than a settled identification.

Key terms

abhisatta — “cursed, hexed” — the verse's opening simile for how old age arrives.
nipatati — “falls” — describing the suddenness of this arrival.
vaya — “old age, decline” — the subject of the verse's central observation.
sarati — “remembers” — the verb behind the verse's account of recalling oneself as a stranger.
avippavasat — “not having gone away, not having been absent” — the verse's closing insistence on unbroken continuity.

The text

The text in full: one verse, with no separate attribution line in this chapter. Translation: Bhikkhu Sujato (CC0, SuttaCentral).

§1Old age falls like a curse; it’s the same body, but it seems like someone else’s. I remember myself as if I were someone else, but I’m still the same, I haven’t been away.
