Why this text

Unlike Subhūti's first-person hut verse just before it, this poem describes Mahākoṭṭhita from the outside: calm, unrestless, thoughtful in counsel, shedding bad qualities the way a gale shakes leaves off a tree — a portrait rather than a declaration.

Reading guide

Third-person praise, not first-person declaration

Where Subhūti's verse spoke as ‘my hut’ and ‘my mind’, this poem describes Mahākoṭṭhita entirely in the third person — ‘he shakes off bad qualities’ — the first instance in this chapter of a verse praising its subject rather than letting him speak about himself directly.

An image of natural, effortless shedding

The verse's single simile compares removing bad qualities to a gale shaking leaves off a tree — not a struggle or a battle, but something that happens naturally and thoroughly once the conditions (here, a calm and unrestless mind) are in place.

Four qualities, front-loaded before the simile

The verse packs four descriptive qualities — calm, still, thoughtful in counsel, not restless — into its first three lines, saving the entire fourth line for the single image that ties them together.

Key terms

upasanto — “calm” or “peaceful” — the first quality named in this verse.
uparato — “stilled” or “at rest” — paired directly with upasanto to open the verse.
mantabhāṇī — “thoughtful in speech” or “wise in counsel” — describing how Mahākoṭṭhita speaks, not only how he sits.
anuddhato — “not restless” or “not agitated” — completing the verse's four-part portrait.
dhunāti — “shakes off” — the verb behind this verse's central image, echoed in the simile of a gale shaking leaves from a tree.

The text

The text in full: one verse, with its closing attribution. Translation: Bhikkhu Sujato (CC0, SuttaCentral).

§1Calm and still, thoughtful in counsel, not restless—he shakes off bad qualities as the gale shakes leaves off a tree.

§2That is how this verse was recited by the senior venerable Mahākoṭṭhita.
