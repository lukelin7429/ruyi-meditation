Why this text

Where the chapter's opening poem was an impersonal teaching, this one is sharply personal: Ātuma compares his marriage — arranged for him, not chosen — to a young bamboo shoot that was easy to pull up when small but is now ‘hard to extract’ once grown woody and branching. He asks for release. It is the first verse anywhere in this collection so far to name a wife at all.

Reading guide

A bamboo shoot, grown too woody to pull

The verse's entire force rests on one image: a bamboo shoot (kaḷīra) is simple to pull up while young, but once it has grown tall and put out side-branches (pasākhajāto), it becomes ‘hard to extract’ (dunnikkhamo). Ātuma applies this directly to his own marriage — not a marriage he chose, but one ‘arranged for him’ (bhariyāya ānitāya) — which has grown just as entangling.

The collection's first named wife

Across the seventy-one poems already covered in this collection, no verse has named a wife until this one. Domestic renunciation has appeared before in general terms — leaving household life, giving up sensual pleasures — but this is the first time the specific relationship being left is spelled out as a marriage, and an arranged one at that.

A request, spoken after the fact

The verse's closing line is grammatically unusual: ‘give me permission’ (anumaññaṁ maṁ) sits beside ‘now I've gone forth’ (pabbajitomhi), a present-perfect form describing an already-completed act. Read plainly, Ātuma is asking for consent to something he has, in the same breath, already done. This reading guide does not resolve who the request is addressed to — the verse itself does not say — only notes the unusual timing: permission sought at, or just after, the moment of departure, not before it.

Two opening poems, two registers

Chapter Eight opens by juxtaposing genres: Vacchapāla's verse (Thag 1.71) is an impersonal teaching about a type of person, entirely free of narrative detail; Ātuma's verse, immediately after, is the opposite — a single concrete image drawn from one man's own domestic life. The chapter's first two poems could hardly be more different in kind.

Key terms

kaḷīra — “bamboo shoot” — the verse's central image, easy to pull when young.
dunnikkhama — “hard to extract, hard to pull out” — describing the bamboo once grown woody and branching.
pasākhajāta — “having put out side-branches” — the stage at which the shoot becomes hard to remove.
bhariyā — “wife” — named here for the first time in this collection.
pabbajita — “one who has gone forth”, i.e. ordained — here in a present-perfect form describing an act already completed.

The text

The text in full: one verse, with no separate attribution line in this chapter. Translation: Bhikkhu Sujato (CC0, SuttaCentral).

§1A young bamboo is hard to extract when the point is grown and become all woody. That’s how I feel with the wife who was arranged for me. Give me permission—now I’ve gone forth.
