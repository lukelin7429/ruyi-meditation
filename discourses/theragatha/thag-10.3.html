Theragatha · 10.3

Mahākappina

Why this text

‘If you're prepared for the future, both the good and the bad, then those who examine you for weakness will see none,’ Mahākappina opens — before turning, across the poem's second half, to one of this collection's most sustained direct reflections on death's plain, universal inevitability, closing with counsel on what actually matters in a household left behind.

Reading guide

Foresight as a form of invulnerability

The opening verse frames preparation for the future — both good outcomes and bad — as protection against exploitation: those who examine a prepared person for weakness, whether enemies or well-wishers, find none. This is a more strategic, worldly-sounding opening than most poems in this collection choose.

A verbatim couplet shared with Thag 8.1

The fourth verse — ‘a wise person lives on even after loss of wealth; but without gaining wisdom, even a rich person doesn't really live’ — matches Thag 8.1 (Mahākaccāyana)'s sixth verse word for word, a full couplet shared verbatim between two different named elders in two consecutive books. This joins this collection's growing list of shared formulas, recorded here without asserting which poem is prior or whether both draw on a still older common source.

Radiance echoing this site's own SN 21.11

The second and third verses describe a mind ‘clean... limitless and well-developed... it radiates in every direction’, comparing fulfilled mindfulness of breathing to ‘the moon freed from clouds’. This site's own SN 21.11, With Mahākappina, closes on a related image: a verse ranking the Buddha's own radiance above sun, moon, and warrior alike, describing this same elder's ‘distinctive-looking’ presence and immense attainment.

Death treated as ordinary, not as a special revelation

Verses six and seven make an unusual argument: death is ‘not a teaching just for today; it isn't incredible or amazing’, since ‘everyone who is born here dies here’. Rather than treating death as a profound mystery requiring special insight to accept, this poem insists on its sheer ordinariness — there is nothing remarkable in an entirely universal fact.

Grief examined for what it actually accomplishes

The eighth and ninth verses turn practical: nothing done for the dead helps the dead, ‘for the dead, there is only weeping’, and weeping itself ‘impairs the eye and the body; complexion, strength, and intelligence decline’. The poem doesn't argue against feeling loss, but against the specific, measurable harm of prolonged grief to the living, closing with counsel to value intelligence and learning in one's own household above lamentation.

Key terms

randhaṅ na passanti — “will see no weakness” — the outcome of foresight, named in the opening verse.
jīvate vāpi sappañño — “a wise person lives on” — the fourth verse's opening, shared verbatim with Thag 8.1.
abbhā muttova candimā — “like the moon freed from clouds” — the simile for a mind radiant with developed mindfulness of breathing.
na hetadatthāya matassa hoti — “the things useful for the living are of no use for the dead” — the practical argument opening the eighth verse.
nihīyati vaṇṇabalaṁ matī ca — “complexion, strength, and intelligence decline” — the measurable cost of prolonged weeping, named in the ninth verse.

The text

The text in full: ten verses, the second extended to six lines, with no separate attribution line in this chapter. Translation: Bhikkhu Sujato (CC0, SuttaCentral).

§1If you’re prepared for the future, both the good and the bad, then those, whether enemies or well-wishers, who examine you for weakness will see none.

§2One who has fulfilled, developed, and gradually consolidated mindfulness of breathing as it was taught by the Buddha: they light up the world, like the moon freed from clouds.

§3Yes, my mind is clean, limitless and well-developed; it has broken through and been uplifted—it radiates in every direction.

§4A wise person lives on even after loss of wealth; but without gaining wisdom, even a rich person doesn’t really live.

§5Wisdom questions what is learned; wisdom grows fame and reputation; a person united with wisdom finds happiness even among sufferings.

§6It’s not a teaching just for today; it isn’t incredible or amazing. When you’re born, you die—what’s amazing about that?

§7For anyone who is born, death always follows after life. Everyone who is born here dies here; such is the nature of living creatures.

§8The things that are useful for the living are of no use for the dead—not fame, not celebrity, not praise by ascetics and brahmins. For the dead, there is only weeping.

§9And weeping impairs the eye and the body; complexion, strength, and intelligence decline. Your enemies rejoice; but your well-wishers are not happy.

§10So you should wish that those who stay in your family have intelligence and learning, and do their duty through the power of wisdom, just as you’d cross a full river by boat.
