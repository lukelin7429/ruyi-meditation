Theragatha · 6.1

Uruveḷakassapa

Uruveḷakassapa — The Book of the Sixes · Chapter One · Poem 1 of 14

Setting

Why this text

Even seeing the Buddha's own demonstrations of power wasn't enough to make Uruveḷakassapa bow — ‘I was blinded by jealousy and conceit,’ he admits — until the Buddha, reading his thoughts, scolded him directly. What follows is this collection's first poem to name all six direct knowledges in sequence, closing where the trio of fire-worshipping Kassapa brothers, begun in the book before this one, is finally complete.

Reading guide

Jealousy that even a miracle couldn't dissolve

Despite seeing the Buddha's pāṭihīrāni (demonstrations of power), Uruveḷakassapa admits ‘issāmānena vañcito’, ‘I was blinded by jealousy and conceit’ — the miracle alone wasn't enough. Only when the Buddha read his thoughts directly and scolded him (‘codesi narasārathi’, ‘the trainer of men scolded me’) did saṅvega, a sense of spiritual urgency, take hold.

Petty powers set aside for a greater going forth

The third verse dismisses his own prior ascetic accomplishments as a jaṭila (matted-hair ascetic) with a single word: siddhi parittikā, ‘petty powers’. Whatever he had achieved through fire asceticism, it's framed here as minor next to what follows.

All six direct knowledges, named in a single poem

The fifth and sixth verses name past lives (pubbenivāsa), clairvoyance (dibbacakkhu), psychic power (iddhi), knowledge of others' minds (paracittaññū), clairaudience (dibbasota), and, in the closing line, the ending of all fetters — the full chalabhiññā, six direct knowledges, rather than the more common tisso vijjā (three knowledges) formula recurring elsewhere in this collection. This is the first poem on this site to list the complete six in sequence.

The last of three fire-worshipping brothers

This poem opens a new book, but its speaker closes an old thread: alongside Thag 5.6 (Nadīkassapa) and Thag 5.7 (Gayākassapa), Uruveḷakassapa completes the well-known trio of fire-worshipping brahmin brothers converted together with their many followers — the occasion, in the wider canon, for the teaching already translated on this site as SN 35.28, the Fire Sermon. As the eldest, his name (from Uruvelā, near the river Nerañjarā) anchors the group, though this poem itself doesn't narrate the conversion episode, focusing instead on the psychological beat of jealousy overcome.

Opening the Book of the Sixes, a structure confirmed fresh

Fourteen poems, one chapter — matching the single-chapter pattern of the three books before this one (Threes, Fours, Fives), though this had to be checked against bilara-data rather than assumed. The baseline verse form is six four-line verses; several poems in this book extend or contract that pattern, each noted as it comes.

Key terms

pāṭihīrāni — “demonstrations of power” — witnessed but insufficient, on their own, to inspire respect.
issāmānena vañcito — “blinded by jealousy and conceit” — the obstacle named in the poem's first verse.
narasārathi — “trainer of men” — an epithet for the Buddha, echoing the standard purisadammasārathi.
jaṭila — “matted-hair ascetic” — Uruveḷakassapa's former identity and fire-worshipping practice.
sabbasaṅyojanakkhayo — “the ending of all fetters” — the poem's final line, marking full awakening.

The text

The text in full: six verses, with no separate attribution line in this chapter, opening the Book of the Sixes. Translation: Bhikkhu Sujato (CC0, SuttaCentral).

§1Seeing the demonstrations of the renowned Gotama was not enough for me to bow to him—I was blinded by jealousy and conceit.

§2Knowing my thoughts, the trainer of men scolded me. I was struck with a sense of urgency, so astonishing and hair-raising!

§3Setting aside the petty powers I had before as a matted-hair ascetic, I then went forth in the victor’s instruction.

§4I used to be content with sacrifice, the realm of sensual pleasures was my priority. But later I eradicated desire, and hatred and also delusion.

§5I know my past lives; my clairvoyance is clarified; I have psychic powers, and know the minds of others; I have attained clairaudience.

§6I’ve attained the goal for the sake of which I went forth from the lay life to homelessness—the ending of all fetters.
