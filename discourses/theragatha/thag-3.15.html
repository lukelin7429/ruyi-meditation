Theragatha · 3.15

Why this text

This poem's three verses — on procrastination, on saying only what one will do, and on the bliss of extinguishment — are word for word identical to Thag 3.3, credited there to Bākula. This is the most complete duplication this reading guide has found anywhere on this site: not a shared line, not a shared couplet, but all twelve lines, attributed to two different named elders.

Reading guide

Every line, matching another poem exactly

Verse for verse, word for word, this poem's Pali is identical to Thag 3.3: the same warning against procrastination, the same rule about saying only what one will do, the same declaration of extinguishment as ‘sorrowless, stainless, secure’. This collection has found many partial echoes — shared openings, shared closings, shared single lines — but never before a complete poem repeated in full under a second name.

Two names, one text, no explanation offered

Neither this poem nor Thag 3.3 carries a surviving comment addressing the duplication. This reading guide does not know why the same twelve lines were preserved under both Bākula's name and Hārita's — whether one poet borrowed consciously from the other, whether both drew on a shared stock of teaching verses circulating independently of any single author, or whether the transmission itself duplicated the attribution by accident. The text offers no signal either way, and this reading guide does not guess beyond what the text supports.

Closing a second same-name chain in this book

This poem also completes this collection's Hārita chain: Hārita (1st), at Thag 1.29 in the Book of the Ones, commands himself by name to straighten his mind like a fletcher straightens an arrow. The two Hāritas share nothing but a name — this poem's content belongs entirely to the duplicate teaching it shares with Bākula, not to any theme carried over from the first Hārita.

Key terms

karaṇīyāni — “what should be done” — identical to Thag 3.3's opening term.
akarontaṁ bhāsamānaṁ — “one who talks without doing” — who the wise are said to recognize, matching Thag 3.3 exactly.
nibbānaṁ — “extinguishment” — declared sorrowless, stainless, and secure, in lines identical to Thag 3.3's closing verse.
sammāsambuddhadesitaṁ — “as taught by the fully awakened Buddha” — crediting the same source as Thag 3.3.
yattha dukkhaṁ nirujjhati — “where suffering all ceases” — the poem's final line, matching Thag 3.3 word for word.

The text

The text in full: three verses, with no separate attribution line in this chapter, and identical throughout to Thag 3.3. Translation: Bhikkhu Sujato (CC0, SuttaCentral).

§1Whoever wishes to do afterwards tasks they should have done before has lost the causes for happiness, and afterwards they’re tormented by regrets.

§2You should only say what you would do; you shouldn’t say what you wouldn’t do. The wise will recognize one who talks without doing.

§3Oh! Extinguishment is so very blissful, as taught by the fully awakened Buddha: sorrowless, stainless, secure, where suffering all ceases.
