Why this text

Somamitta pictures someone lost in a great sea who clambers onto a little log and sinks anyway, then applies the image directly: a person who otherwise lives well sinks too, by relying on a lazy companion — so seek out the noble ones instead, secluded, energetic, and practicing absorption.

Reading guide

A sinking log, in a great sea

The first verse spends its opening lines on pure image: lost in the middle of a great sea, clambering onto a little log offers no real safety — ‘you'll sink’ regardless. Only in the second half of the verse does the poem name what the log stands for: relying on a lazy person, which drags down even someone who ‘lives well’ on their own.

A second poem with an extended first verse

Like Thag 2.12 (Jotidāsa) two poems earlier, this poem's first verse runs to six lines rather than the book's usual four — the second such variation in this chapter, both times using the extra length to build out an image before applying it.

Direct instruction: who to dwell with instead

The second verse gives the positive counterpart: dwell with the noble ones, ‘secluded and determined’, ‘always energetic’, ‘astute’, and practicing absorption (jhāyī) — a specific, named alternative rather than just a warning to avoid bad company.

Good company, elsewhere on this site

This site's own SN 3.18, ‘Good Friends’, records the Buddha correcting Ānanda's suggestion that good friendship is only ‘half the spiritual life’ — insisting instead that it is the whole of it. This poem's sea-and-log simile makes a matching point from the opposite direction: the cost of bad company, rather than the value of good.

Key terms

mahaṇṇave — “in a great sea” — the setting for this poem's opening simile.
kusītaṁ — “a lazy person” — what the little log stands for, once the simile is applied.
parivījjeyya — “should avoid” — the direct instruction closing the first verse.
āraddhavīriyehi — “always energetic” — one of several qualities named for the noble companions to seek out instead.
jhāyibhi — “who practice absorption” — the closing quality named for the companions this poem recommends.

The text

The text in full: a six-line verse followed by a four-line verse. Translation: Bhikkhu Sujato (CC0, SuttaCentral).

§1If you’re lost in the middle of a great sea, and you clamber up on a little log, you’ll sink. So too, a person who lives well sinks by relying on a lazy person. Hence you should avoid such a lazy person who lacks energy.

§2Dwell with the noble ones who are secluded and determined and always energetic; the astute who practice absorption.
