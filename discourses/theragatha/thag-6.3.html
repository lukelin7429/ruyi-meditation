Theragatha · 6.3

Mahānāga

Mahānāga — The Book of the Sixes · Chapter One · Poem 3 of 14

Setting

Speaker
Mahānāga, on respect for one's spiritual companions
Form

Length
under 1 minute to read

Difficulty

Why this text

Three similes of failure, each answered by its exact opposite: a fish in a shrinking puddle against a fish in plenty of water; a rotten seed against a fine one; distance from extinguishment against nearness to it. Mahānāga's single subject — gārava, respect for one's spiritual companions — is stated only once, then run twice through, in reverse.

Reading guide

A single line, repeated six times as refrain

‘Yassa sabrahmacārīsu, gāravo nāti/upalabbhati’, ‘whoever has/does have no respect for their spiritual companions’, opens every one of the six verses, varying only in whether respect is present or absent. This is the poem's structural spine: six variations on one condition, not six separate teachings.

Three similes, doubled and inverted

Each of the three consequences named for disrespect gets its exact mirror for respect: a fish stranded in a shrinking puddle (maccho appodake) becomes a fish in plenty of water (maccho bavhodake); a rotten seed in a field (bījaṅ pūtikaṅ) becomes a fine seed (bījaṅ bhaddakaṅ); distance from extinguishment (ārakā nibbānā) becomes nearness to it (santike nibbānaṅ). The structure argues by symmetry rather than by explanation — no verse states why respect matters, only what follows from its presence or absence.

Sabrahmacārī, a term worth pausing on

‘Spiritual companions’ translates sabrahmacārī, literally ‘those who live the holy life together [with oneself]’ — fellow renunciants generally, not a specific teacher or elder. The poem's concern is horizontal respect within the Saṅgha, not the vertical respect of student toward teacher more commonly treated elsewhere in this collection.

A short poem making a large claim

The final pair of verses places the stakes at their highest: not mere social friction, but distance from or nearness to nibbāna itself, dhammarājassa sāsane, ‘in the teaching of the Dhamma king’. Whether or not one respects one's fellow practitioners is framed here as bearing directly on the goal of practice, not as a separate matter of etiquette.

A third distinct structure within three poems

After Thag 6.1's single continuous narrative and Thag 6.2's dialogue-with-refrain, this poem introduces a third form entirely: strict antithetical parallelism, verse for verse. Three poems into this book, three different structural approaches have already appeared.

Key terms

gāravo — “respect” — the single condition this poem examines from both directions.
sabrahmacārī — “spiritual companions”, literally “those who live the holy life together” — fellow renunciants generally.
maccho appodake — “a fish in a little puddle” — the first simile of falling away, mirrored later by a fish in plenty of water.
bījaṅ pūtikaṅ — “a rotten seed” — the second simile, mirrored by a fine seed thriving in a field.
dhammarājassa sāsane — “in the teaching of the Dhamma king” — the setting in which both distance from and nearness to extinguishment are measured.

The text

The text in full: six verses, with no separate attribution line in this chapter. Translation: Bhikkhu Sujato (CC0, SuttaCentral).

§1Whoever has no respect for their spiritual companions falls away from the true teaching, like a fish in a little puddle.

§2Whoever has no respect for their spiritual companions doesn’t thrive in the true teaching, like a rotten seed in a field.

§3Whoever has no respect for their spiritual companions is far from extinguishment, in the teaching of the Dhamma king.

§4Whoever does have respect for their spiritual companions doesn’t fall away from the true teaching, like a fish in plenty of water.

§5Whoever does have respect for their spiritual companions thrives in the true teaching, like a fine seed in a field.

§6Whoever does have respect for their spiritual companions is close to extinguishment in the teaching of the Dhamma king.
