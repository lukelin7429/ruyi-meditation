Since Infancy

Why this text

This poem's underlying story is genuinely troubling once read closely: a woman held in captivity her whole life is then deliberately, cruelly deceived into infidelity in order to ‘prove’ a man's claim about women's nature — meaning the verse's conclusion is not evidence of anything about women at all, but a record of the harm done to secure it.

Reading guide

A verse presenting a conclusion as if it were simply observed

The verse states its point as though self-evident: ‘the brahmin who played the harp while blindfolded had raised his wife since infancy — who would ever trust such women?’ Read on its own, this sounds like a simple cautionary proverb.

A story of captivity and engineered betrayal, not natural weakness

Per Sujato's comment, the full story is considerably darker: an innocent baby girl was sold into slavery and kept locked up under guard her entire life by a brahmin who married her — done specifically to feed his gambling addiction, refuting a rival's own lucky charm (an oath asserting that all women are faithless). When the brahmin began winning, his adversary employed a ‘knave’ who cruelly deceived the imprisoned woman's own attendant in order to engineer exactly the infidelity the wager required.

What the story actually demonstrates, read honestly

Read in full, this story does not show a natural tendency in the woman at its center — it shows a woman held captive her entire life, then deliberately and cruelly manipulated by outside actors specifically to produce a predetermined result for a gambling bet between two men. This reading guide presents that fuller context directly, since the bare verse alone conceals far more than it reveals about what actually happened.

Key terms

bhatā bhariyā — “had raised his wife since infancy” — the verse's own description of a woman held captive from childhood, giving this poem its traditional title.
ko jātu vissase — “who would ever trust such women?” — the verse's rhetorical conclusion, presented by the text as though self-evidently proven.
dice wager — per Sujato's comment, the actual motive behind the brahmin's marriage: an attempt to refute a rival gambler's lucky charm asserting all women are faithless.
Aṇḍabhūtajātaka — the traditional title of this tale, ‘Since Infancy’.
knave — per Sujato's comment, the agent employed by the brahmin's rival to deceive the captive woman's attendant, deliberately engineering the outcome the wager required.

The text

The text in full: a single verse, whose fuller context Sujato's own comment reveals to be considerably more disturbing than the bare text alone suggests, discussed above. Translation: Bhikkhu Sujato (CC0, SuttaCentral).
