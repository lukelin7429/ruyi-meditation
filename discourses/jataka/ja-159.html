Jataka · No. 159

The Peacock

Mora — Book of the Twos · Daṭhavagga (Chapter on the Strong), Poem 9 of 10 — the only poem in this vagga with a Sujato translation

Setting
Deep in the mountains, at dawn and at dusk

Length
1 minute to read
Northern parallel

Difficulty
★★★☆☆ — dense with Vedic solar-worship vocabulary worth unpacking

Why this text

This poem is the traditional source of the Mora Paritta, still recited today as a protective chant: a golden peacock, having fled to the mountains for safety, invokes the rising and setting sun in language borrowed directly from Vedic solar worship, then adds an entirely separate set of Buddhist homage verses alongside it.

Reading guide

A chant recited twice a day, for safety

Per Sujato's comment, a golden peacock-king, having fled deep in the mountains for safety, recites this verse every day. The first pair of stanzas is spoken at sunrise, invoking the sun as protection through the coming day; the second pair mirrors it almost exactly at sunset, asking the same protection through the night. Each half closes with the same narrative line marking what the peacock then does — wanders out searching for food by day, settles in for the night.

Vedic solar worship, with Buddhist verses laid alongside it

Sujato's comment is direct: ‘the poem takes straightforward solar worship in the Vedic style and adds quite separate Buddhistic verses.’ The sun is addressed as ‘ekarāja’, ‘the one king’ — per the comment, a title given to Indra himself at Rig Veda 8.37.3, and used of actual kings elsewhere in the Jātaka tradition (Ja 303 and Ja 542, neither part of this site's own selection). ‘Harissavaṇṇo’, ‘palomino-colored’, likewise echoes the Vedic ‘haryaśva’, ‘he of the yellow horses’, itself a name for Indra in solar hymns at Rig Veda 3.30 and 3.44. Set directly against this borrowed Vedic vocabulary, the peacock's own chant adds lines with no Vedic counterpart at all: homage to the Awakened Ones, to Awakening itself, to the freed ones, and to freedom.

A single grammatical detail, and a confirmed cross-reference

Sujato's comment notes one further detail: though the peacock speaks in the singular (‘I worship’), the safety he asks for is plural (‘let us live out this day’) — his own protection extended, in the asking, to others besides himself. The comment also cross-references this site's own Snp 5.5 for the repeated construction ‘taṁ taṁ’, a linguistic note rather than a thematic one.

Key terms

ekarāja — “the one king” — here an epithet for the sun, per Sujato's comment also used of Indra (Rig Veda 8.37.3) and of actual kings elsewhere in the Jātaka tradition.
Harissavaṇṇo — “palomino-colored” — echoing the Vedic ‘haryaśva’, a name for Indra in solar hymns, applied here to the sun.
Namo vimuttānaṁ, namo vimuttiyā — “homage to the freed ones, homage to freedom” — one of the chant's own Buddhist verses, with no counterpart in the Vedic material it otherwise draws on.
parittaṁ — “protection” — the chant's own self-description, giving this poem its traditional association as the Mora Paritta.
Morajātaka — the traditional title of this tale, ‘The Peacock’.

The text

The text in full: four short stanzas in two mirrored pairs, morning and evening. Translation: Bhikkhu Sujato (CC0, SuttaCentral).

§1This Clear-eyed One arises, the One King, palomino-colored, shining over the earth. I bow especially to you, palomino-colored, shining over the earth: guarded by you let us live out this day.

§2Those brahmins who are knowledge-masters with respect to all things, I worship them, may they keep me safe. Hail the Awakened Ones, hail Awakening! Homage to the freed ones, homage to freedom! Having made this protection, the peacock wanders seeking.

§3This Clear-eyed One sets, the One King, palomino-colored, shining over the earth. I bow especially to you, palomino-colored, shining over the earth: guarded by you let us live out this night.

§4Those brahmins who are knowledge-masters with respect to all things, I worship them, may they keep me safe. Hail the Awakened Ones, hail Awakening! Homage to the freed ones, homage to freedom! Having made this protection, the peacock made his home.
