The Mosquito

Why this text

This poem shifts the chapter's refrain from ignoring good advice to a related but distinct danger: well-meaning help delivered without judgment, illustrated by one of this collection's most darkly comic single images — a son who kills the mosquito on his father's head with an axe, killing his father in the process.

Reading guide

A modified refrain, and its own grim proof

The verse restates the chapter's underlying concern in new terms: ‘better is a considerate foe than an inconsiderate friend’ — then delivers its proof: ‘thinking, “I shall kill the mosquito”, the idiot son split open his father's head.’

Good intentions, applied without any judgment

Per Sujato's comment, when the carpenter asked his son to rid him of a troublesome mosquito on his scalp, ‘he did not think he would do it with an axe.’ The tragedy is not malice but a complete absence of proportion or judgment — the son genuinely wants to help, and that is precisely what makes the outcome possible.

Key terms

seyyo amitto matiyā upeto — “better is a considerate foe” — the poem's opening proverb, reframing this chapter's concern in terms of judgment rather than loyalty.
mativippahīno — “inconsiderate”, literally “devoid of judgment” — the disqualifying flaw in the well-meaning friend.
putto pitu abbhidā uttamaṅgaṁ — “the son split open his father's head” — the poem's darkly comic proof of its own proverb.
Makasajātaka — the traditional title of this tale, ‘The Mosquito’.
eḷamūgo — “idiot” — the verse's own blunt description of the son.

The text

The text in full: a single verse, continuing this chapter's sequence in modified form. Translation: Bhikkhu Sujato (CC0, SuttaCentral).

§1Better is a considerate foe than an inconsiderate friend. Thinking, “I shall kill the mosquito”, the idiot son split open his father’s head.
