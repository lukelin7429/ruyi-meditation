Why this text

This poem's underlying story is a well-known trickster tale — a crocodile disguised as a rock, trying to catch a monkey king — but its closing line carries a genuine double meaning that Sujato's comment resolves by pointing directly to the crocodile's own physical behavior in the story.

Reading guide

Four qualities that let the monkey king escape

The verse names its subject's virtues directly: ‘whoever possesses these four qualities like you, lord of langurs — truth, principle, steadfastness, and generosity — escapes the visible foe.’ Per Sujato's comment, a crocodile's pregnant wife craves the heart of the monkey king; her husband tries to satisfy her by pretending to be a rock in the river, but the monkey sees through the disguise.

A double meaning the story itself resolves

Sujato's comment identifies a deliberate ambiguity in the closing word ‘diṭṭha’: the traditional commentary reads it as ‘foe’ (giving ‘defeats the foe’), but its more common sense is simply ‘seen’. The comment resolves this in favor of the more literal reading by pointing to the crocodile's own action in the story: he shuts his eyes and opens his mouth to catch the langur — making ‘escapes the one seen [with eyes shut]’ the more pointed, story-supported reading.

A story from a recognized cycle of tales, and named leaders

Sujato's comment situates this tale within ‘a cycle of dohaḷa stories’ (concerning pregnancy cravings) in the Jātakas, and identifies the ‘vānara’ as a legendary race of aggressive, human-like monkeys, whose leaders (such as Sugrīva or Hanuman in the wider tradition) carry the same title, ‘lord of langurs’, given here.

Key terms

saccaṁ dhammo dhiti cāgo — “truth, principle, steadfastness, and generosity” — the four qualities the verse credits for the monkey king's escape.
dohaḷa — “pregnancy craving” — per Sujato's comment, the trigger for this story, part of a recognized cycle of such tales within the Jātakas.
diṭṭha — a deliberately ambiguous closing term, per Sujato's comment meaning either “foe” (per the traditional commentary) or, more literally, “seen” — resolved by the crocodile's own eyes-shut, mouth-open posture in the story.
vānarinda — “lord of langurs”, per Sujato's comment a traditional title for legendary monkey-king leaders such as Sugrīva or Hanuman.
Vānarindajātaka — the traditional title of this tale, ‘The Lord of Langurs’.

The text

The text in full: a single verse, opening a new matched pair with Ja 58, discussed above. Translation: Bhikkhu Sujato (CC0, SuttaCentral).

§1Whoever possesses these four qualities like you, lord of langurs—truth, principle, steadfastness, and generosity—escapes the visible foe.
