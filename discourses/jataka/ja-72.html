Why this text

This poem opens a matched pair with the next, Ja 73, both on the theme of ingratitude — here, a magnificent elephant freely gives up his own ivory to the very man he just rescued, once that man's gratitude curdles into greed.

Reading guide

A verse on the bottomless demands of ingratitude

The verse states its point in the sharpest possible terms: an ungrateful person, always looking for opportunity, would still not be satisfied even if given the whole earth. The generosity of whoever they depend on is treated as inexhaustible, and used accordingly.

Per Sujato's comment, a rescue repaid with greed

A magnificent white elephant rescues a lost man and carries him home on his own back. But, falling prey to greed, the man then asks the elephant for ivory from his own tusks — and the noble creature freely gives them up to the very person who had just been saved by him. This poem and the next, Ja 73, form a back-to-back pair on ingratitude toward a rescuer, each approaching the theme from a different angle.

Key terms

akataññussa posassa — “an ungrateful person” — the verse's opening description, its subject throughout.
niccaṁ vivaradassino — “always looking for opportunity” — the ungrateful person's defining habit of mind.
sabbañce pathaviṁ dajjā — “even if given the whole earth” — the verse's extreme measure of how much would still not be enough.
Sīlavahatthijātaka — the traditional title of this tale, ‘The Virtuous Elephant’.
Ja 73 — the next poem in this chapter, forming a matched pair with this one on the theme of ingratitude toward a rescuer.

The text

The text in full: a single verse. Translation: Bhikkhu Sujato (CC0, SuttaCentral).

§1An ungrateful person, always looking for opportunity, even if given the whole earth, would still not be satisfied.
