The Deer Named Lucky Spot

Why this text

This verse opens the Sīlavagga (‘Ethics Chapter’) with a direct contrast between two named deer-kings, one prospering through virtuous, protective leadership and one brought low by carelessness — setting the ethical theme this whole second chapter of the collection develops.

Reading guide

Two deer-kings, two fates

The verse states its principle, then shows it: ‘the virtuous ones prosper, who live extending protection. See Lucky Spot coming, surrounded by their family circle; then see this Black Mark, bereft of family.’ Per Sujato's comment, the traditional story explains the contrast directly — the wise and prudent deer-king Lucky Spot kept his herd safe on their journey, while the foolish and careless Black Mark lost his whole herd.

Names that carry their own meaning

Sujato's comment notes that ‘lakkhaṇa’ (Lucky Spot) means ‘possessing beautiful or auspicious marks’, fitting the common spotted (Chital) deer, elegant and gregarious; ‘kāḷa’ (Black Mark) carries the opposite sense, ‘unlucky’. The two deer-kings' names themselves foreshadow their contrasting fates before the verse even describes them.

Key terms

sīlavataṁ attho — “the virtuous ones prosper” — the verse's opening principle.
paṭisanthāravuttinaṁ — “who live extending protection” — the specific virtue the verse credits for that prospering.
lakkhaṇa — “Lucky Spot” — per Sujato's comment, “possessing beautiful or auspicious marks”, fitting a spotted (Chital) deer.
kāḷa — “Black Mark” — the contrasting, “unlucky” name of the careless deer-king.
Lakkhaṇamigajātaka — the traditional title of this tale, opening the Sīlavagga.

The text

The text in full: a single six-line verse. Translation: Bhikkhu Sujato (CC0, SuttaCentral).

§1The virtuous ones prosper, who live extending protection. See Lucky Spot coming, surrounded by their family circle; then see this Black Mark, bereft of family.
