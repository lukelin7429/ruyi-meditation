Jataka · No. 78

Herring, the Miser

Illisa — Book of the Ones · Chapter Eight (Varuṇavagga) · Poem 8 of 9

Setting
A rich man's household, and the king's court
Speaker
The king, unable to distinguish the two figures before him
Form
One four-line stanza
Length
Under 1 minute to read
Northern parallel
This reading guide does not assert a specific matching text for this poem in other Buddhist literatures.
Difficulty
★☆☆☆☆ — a clever, almost comic trick played on a miser

Why this text

Per Sujato's comment, a miserly rich man is so tight-fisted that Sakka, king of the gods, takes on his exact physical form — lameness, crossed eyes, warts, and all — in order to force the man's own vaults open for the people; even the king, asked to judge between them, cannot tell which is which.

Reading guide

A double so exact that even the king is fooled

The verse is spoken from the king's own bewildered vantage point: ‘both are lame, both crippled, both cross-eyed, and both covered in warts: I see not which is Herring.’ The verse's entire force rests on this single admission — the disguise is not merely convincing, it is total.

Per Sujato's comment, a name built from a fish, and a rich man's real-world namesake

A rich man was so miserly that Sakka took on his form, crippled though it was, specifically to open the miser's own vaults for the people; asked to adjudicate between the two identical figures, the king simply cannot. Sujato's comment traces the man's name, Illisa, to the Ilish herring — still popular eating today, and consequently threatened in modern times by overfishing, pollution, and global warming — and suggests it arose as wordplay on maccariya (‘miserly’) by way of maccha (‘fish’); his misadventures, in fact, were sparked by seeing a man eating rotten fish.

Key terms

Ubho khañjā ubho kuṇī — “both are lame, both crippled” — the king's own opening description of the impossible choice before him.
nāhaṁ passāmi illisan — “I see not which is Herring” — the verse's closing admission, giving this poem its traditional title.
Illisa — the rich miser's name, per Sujato's comment traced to the Ilish herring, still popular eating today and now threatened by overfishing, pollution, and global warming.
maccariya / maccha — “miserly” and “fish” — the wordplay Sujato's comment suggests lies behind the miser's own name.
Illisajātaka — the traditional title of this tale, ‘Herring, the Miser’.

The text

The text in full: a single verse. Translation: Bhikkhu Sujato (CC0, SuttaCentral).
